Capacitors

A capacitor stores energy by holding positive and negative charge apart. It stores far less energy than a battery, but it can hand all of it back in a fraction of a second, which is why camera flashes, defibrillators and the smoothing circuit in every phone charger use one. On this page you will see where the ½ in \(W = \tfrac{1}{2}QV\) comes from, why capacitors charge and discharge exponentially, and how a log graph turns that curve into a straight line.

Spec100Capacitance: C = Q/V

Capacitance is defined as C = Q/V; use this equation.

What a capacitor does

  • A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator (the dielectric).
  • When connected to a supply, electrons are pushed onto one plate and pulled off the other. One plate gets charge +Q, the other −Q. The total charge on the capacitor is zero; it separates charge.
  • "Charge stored Q" means the charge on one plate.
  • The p.d. across the plates rises as charge builds up. Q is proportional to V.
Formula sheet

Capacitance

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \]
\(C\): capacitance / F\(Q\): charge on one plate / C\(V\): p.d. across the capacitor / V
  • C = capacitance (farad, F), Q = charge on one plate (C), V = p.d. across the capacitor (V). 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹.
  • A farad is huge. Typical values: µF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • On a graph of Q against V, the gradient is C. On a graph of V against Q, the gradient is 1/C.

charge stored

A 470 µF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.

Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C

  • Capacitors "separate charge"; "the total charge on a capacitor is zero".
  • Sharing: when a charged capacitor is connected to an uncharged one, "the charge stored in each is less than the original, because the p.d. is shared".

Spec101Energy stored by a capacitor

Use W = ½QV; derive it from the area under a p.d.–charge graph; derive and use W = ½CV² and W = ½Q²/C.

Derivation from the V–Q graph

  • While charging, the p.d. across the capacitor rises in proportion to the charge: the graph of V against Q is a straight line through the origin (gradient 1/C).
  • To add a small charge ΔQ at p.d. V, work done = VΔQ: a thin strip under the graph.
  • Adding all the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle = ½ × Q × V.
Formula sheet

Energy stored in a capacitor

\[ W = \tfrac{1}{2}QV = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} \]
\(W\): energy stored / J\(Q\): charge / C\(V\): p.d. / V\(C\): capacitance / F

To move a small extra charge \(\Delta Q\) onto the plates when the p.d. is \(V\), you do work (p.d. is work done per unit charge):

\[ \Delta W = V\,\Delta Q \]

If \(\Delta Q\) is small, \(V\) hardly changes while you add it.

On a graph of \(V\) (vertical) against \(Q\) (horizontal), \(V\,\Delta Q\) is the area of a thin strip under the line. The total work done charging from 0 to \(Q\) is the sum of all the strips, which is the area under the graph:

\[ W = \sum V\,\Delta Q = \text{area under the } V\text{–}Q \text{ graph} \]

Because \(V \propto Q\), the graph is a straight line through the origin, so the area is a triangle with base \(Q\) and height \(V\):

\[ W = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{W = \tfrac{1}{2}QV} \]

The ½ is there because, on average, each coulomb was pushed on against only half of the final p.d.

Start from the result above and the definition of capacitance:

\[ W = \tfrac{1}{2}QV, \qquad C = \frac{Q}{V} \]

To get rid of \(Q\), write \(Q = CV\) and substitute:

\[ W = \tfrac{1}{2}(CV)V \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{W = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2} \]

To get rid of \(V\) instead, write \(V = Q/C\) and substitute:

\[ W = \tfrac{1}{2}Q\left(\frac{Q}{C}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{W = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C}} \]

All three are the same energy. Pick the one that uses the two quantities you already know.

  • W = energy stored (J), Q = charge (C), V = p.d. (V), C = capacitance (F).
  • The other forms come from substituting Q = CV (gives ½CV²) or V = Q/C (gives ½Q²/C).
  • Energy ∝ V²: double the voltage, four times the energy.

Why it is ½QV and not QV

The supply does work QV pushing charge Q through p.d. V. Only half ends up stored in the capacitor; the other half is dissipated as heat in the resistance of the circuit while it charges.

energy stored and released

A 2200 µF capacitor is charged to 12 V.

W = ½CV² = 0.5 × 2200 × 10⁻⁶ × 12² = 0.16 J

It discharges to 6.0 V. Energy released = ½C(V₁² − V₂²) = 0.5 × 2.2 × 10⁻³ × (144 − 36) = 0.12 J

The p.d. halved but three-quarters of the energy was released.

With a +20% tolerance on C, the maximum energy at 12 V is 1.2 × 0.158 = 0.19 J.

Worked example

A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a p.d. of 9.0 V. Calculate (a) the charge stored and (b) the energy stored. (c) The p.d. is increased to 18 V. How much extra energy is stored?

Show solution

(a) \(Q = CV = 220\times10^{-6} \times 9.0 = 1.98\times10^{-3}\ \text{C} \approx 2.0\ \text{mC}\)

(b) \(W = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 \times 220\times10^{-6} \times 9.0^2 = 8.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{J}\)

(c) Energy goes as \(V^2\), so doubling \(V\) gives four times the energy: \(4 \times 8.91\times10^{-3} = 3.56\times10^{-2}\ \text{J}\). The extra energy is \(3.56\times10^{-2} - 0.89\times10^{-2} = 2.7\times10^{-2}\ \text{J}\) (three times the original, not one).

Worked example

A camera flash uses a 150 μF capacitor charged to 300 V. When the flash fires, 90% of the stored energy is released in 1.0 ms. Calculate the mean power delivered to the flash tube, and explain why the camera cannot simply use its 3 V battery directly.

Show solution

Energy stored: \(W = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 \times 150\times10^{-6} \times 300^2 = 6.75\ \text{J}\)

Energy released: \(0.90 \times 6.75 = 6.08\ \text{J}\), so mean power \(= 6.08 / 1.0\times10^{-3} = 6.1\times10^{3}\ \text{W}\)

The battery has enough energy, but its internal resistance limits the current it can deliver, so it can only supply energy slowly (a few watts). The capacitor is charged slowly from the battery over a few seconds, then delivers the energy very quickly through the low resistance of the flash tube (small \(RC\)).

  • "Use of W = ½CV²" (or ½QV or ½Q²/C).
  • "Use of tolerance as +20%" is a separate mark: take the extreme value in the direction the question needs.
  • Comparisons need a conclusion: "184 J > 150 J, so enough energy".

Often asked as: energy stored; energy released between two voltages; tolerance questions; comparing with another energy store (battery, spring); MCQ on how W changes when V changes.

Spec102Charging and discharging curves; the time constant RC

Draw and interpret charge and discharge curves for RC circuits, and understand the significance of the time constant RC.

Charging through a resistor (from a supply V₀)

  1. At the start the capacitor has no charge, so VC = 0, all of V₀ is across R, and the current is maximum: I₀ = V₀/R.
  2. Charge builds up on the plates, so the p.d. across C rises.
  3. Since VR + VC = V₀, the p.d. across R falls, so the current falls (I = VR/R).
  4. Charge therefore arrives more slowly: Q and VC rise more and more slowly.
  5. Finally VC = V₀ and I = 0.

Discharging through a resistor

  • At the start V = V₀ and I₀ = V₀/R. As charge flows off, V falls, so I falls, so the charge flows off more slowly.
  • Q, V and I all decay exponentially: each falls by the same fraction in each equal time interval.

The time constant τ = RC

Time constant

\[ \tau = RC \]
\(\tau\): time constant / s\(R\): resistance / Ω\(C\): capacitance / F

After one half-life, the charge is half its starting value:

\[ \tfrac{1}{2}Q_0 = Q_0\, e^{-t_{1/2}/RC} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tfrac12 = e^{-t_{1/2}/RC} \]

Take natural logs of both sides. \(\ln \tfrac12 = -\ln 2\):

\[ -\ln 2 = -\frac{t_{1/2}}{RC} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{t_{1/2} = RC \ln 2 \approx 0.69\,RC} \]

The starting charge cancelled out, which is why the half-life is the same however full the capacitor is.

  • τ = time constant (s), R = resistance (Ω), C = capacitance (F). Ω × F = (V A⁻¹)(C V⁻¹) = C A⁻¹ = s.
  • Discharging: after one time constant Q, V and I have fallen to 1/e ≈ 37% of their initial values.
  • Charging: after one time constant Q and VC have risen to 63% of their final values.
  • After about 5RC the capacitor is effectively fully charged or discharged (less than 1% left).
  • Time to halve: t½ = RC ln 2 ≈ 0.69RC.
  • Bigger R or bigger C → bigger τ → slower charging and discharging.

time constant

R = 47 kΩ, C = 220 µF. τ = RC = 47 × 10³ × 220 × 10⁻⁶ = 10 s.

Time to fall to half: t½ = 0.69 × 10.3 = 7.2 s.

Six-mark charging explanation (indicative content):

  • "There is a current in the circuit; the charge on the capacitor increases."
  • "The p.d. across the capacitor increases, so the p.d. across the resistor decreases."
  • "So the current decreases (I = V/R) and the rate of charging falls."
  • "Finally the p.d. across C equals the supply p.d. and the current is zero."

Sketches: "exponential curve from the origin levelling at V₀", "passes about 2/3 of the maximum at t = RC", "levels off after 4–5 time constants"; discharge "starts on the y-axis" and "does not cross or crash into the t-axis". Label the axes with quantities and units.

Often asked as: sketch a charging or discharging graph of V or I; explain the shape; effect of a changed resistance on the time constant; where VR = VC (each half the supply).

Explaining with "charges moving" but never mentioning current and p.d.: no marks.

Using 37% on a charging curve (it is 63% for charging).

Curves that hit the time axis.

  • Components whose resistance changes with p.d. (an LED or diode in the discharge path): as V falls, R rises, so the time constant increases (WPH14 Jan 2020).
  • A capacitor as a backup supply: its p.d. "remains high enough to operate the device for a short time" (WPH14 Oct 2021).

Spec103Core Practical 11: capacitor charge and discharge

Use an oscilloscope or data logger to display and analyse the p.d. across a capacitor as it charges and discharges through a resistor.

Core practical 11: method

Circuit: d.c. supply (e.g. 6.0 V), two-way switch, resistor R (e.g. 100 kΩ) and capacitor C (e.g. 220–470 µF) in series. A voltmeter, data logger or oscilloscope goes in parallel with the capacitor. In one switch position the supply charges C through R; in the other, C discharges through R alone.

  1. Set the data logger to record V against time at a suitable rate (several readings per second for τ of tens of seconds).
  2. Switch to charge. Record V until it stops changing (about 5RC).
  3. Switch to discharge and record V as it falls.
  4. Repeat for other values of R (or C).

Oscilloscope version: replace the supply and switch with a square-wave signal generator, so the capacitor charges and discharges repeatedly. Adjust the time-base so one charge–discharge cycle fills the screen, then read V and t from the grid. The period of the square wave should be much longer than RC (about 10RC).

Safety: connect an electrolytic capacitor the right way round (+ to +) and do not exceed its working voltage, or it can overheat and burst.

Analysis

  • From V = V₀e−t/RC, taking natural logs: ln V = ln V₀ − t/RC.
  • Plot ln V (y) against t (x): a straight line with gradient −1/RC and intercept ln V₀.
  • So RC = −1/gradient, and C = −1/(R × gradient).
  • Quick checks: the time to fall to 0.37V₀ (discharge) or rise to 0.63V₀ (charge) is RC.

capacitance from a ln V graph

R = 100 kΩ. The best-fit line on a graph of ln V (V in volts) against t (in s) passes through (0, 1.79) and (40, 0.46).

Gradient = (0.46 − 1.79) ÷ 40 = −0.0333 s⁻¹

RC = 1 ÷ 0.0333 = 30.1 s, so C = 30.1 ÷ 1.00 × 10⁵ = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ F (300 µF)

Errors and improvements

  • Capacitors have large tolerances (often ±10% or ±20%), so compare your C with the nominal value using the tolerance.
  • A voltmeter with too low a resistance gives a second discharge path and makes the discharge look faster. A data logger or oscilloscope has a very high resistance.
  • Reading by eye with a stopwatch is limited by reaction time: choose RC of at least tens of seconds, or use a data logger.
  • Use the best-fit line over many points rather than one pair of readings.
Worked example

In Core Practical 11 a student discharges a capacitor through a 47 kΩ resistor and plots \(\ln(V/\text{V})\) against \(t\). The line passes through (0 s, 1.79) and (60 s, 0.29). Determine the capacitance, and the p.d. the capacitor was charged to.

Show solution

Gradient \(= \dfrac{0.29 - 1.79}{60 - 0} = -0.0250\ \text{s}^{-1}\)

Gradient \(= -1/RC\), so \(RC = 1/0.0250 = 40\ \text{s}\), and \(C = \dfrac{40}{47\times10^{3}} = 8.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{F} = 850\ \mu\text{F}\)

Intercept \(= \ln V_0 = 1.79\), so \(V_0 = e^{1.79} = 6.0\ \text{V}\)

  • Circuit: "battery, resistor and capacitor in series", "voltmeter / data logger in parallel with the capacitor", "switch arranged to give a discharge path through the resistor".
  • Why a given circuit is wrong: "when charging the voltmeter is not across C" or "when discharging the resistor is not in the circuit".
  • Tolerance: "6640 Ω is within 5% of 6800 Ω" with the calculation shown.

Often asked as: draw the circuit; read V and t off a data-logger or oscilloscope trace; find R or C from it; check a component is within tolerance.

Spec104Exponential discharge equations

Use Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC), derive and use I = I₀e^(−t/RC) and V = V₀e^(−t/RC), and the matching log equations.

Formula sheet

Capacitor discharge

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= Q_0\, e^{-t/RC} \\ I &= I_0\, e^{-t/RC} \\ V &= V_0\, e^{-t/RC} \end{aligned} \]
\(Q_0, I_0, V_0\): values at \(t = 0\)\(t\): time since the discharge began / s\(RC\): time constant / s

Start from the discharge equation and divide both sides by \(C\). Since \(V = Q/C\) at every instant:

\[ \frac{Q}{C} = \frac{Q_0}{C}\, e^{-t/RC} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{V = V_0\, e^{-t/RC}} \]

\(V_0 = Q_0/C\) is the p.d. at the start. \(C\) is a constant, so dividing by it doesn't change the shape.

During discharge the capacitor is connected directly across the resistor, so the p.d. across the resistor equals the p.d. across the capacitor. Ohm's law gives the current:

\[ I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{V_0}{R}\, e^{-t/RC} \]

\(V_0/R\) is the current at the start, \(I_0\), so:

\[ \boxed{I = I_0\, e^{-t/RC}} \]
  • Q₀, I₀, V₀ = values at t = 0; t = time since discharge began (s); RC = time constant (s).
  • Deriving the V form: V = Q/C, and C is constant, so V = Q₀e−t/RC/C = V₀e−t/RC.
  • Deriving the I form: during discharge the p.d. across R equals V, so I = V/R = (V₀/R)e−t/RC = I₀e−t/RC.

Log forms

Formula sheet

Log forms

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln Q &= \ln Q_0 - \frac{t}{RC} \\ \ln I &= \ln I_0 - \frac{t}{RC} \\ \ln V &= \ln V_0 - \frac{t}{RC} \end{aligned} \]
plot \(\ln V\) (or \(\ln Q\), \(\ln I\)) against \(t\): gradient \(= -1/RC\)

Take natural logs of both sides of \(Q = Q_0 e^{-t/RC}\):

\[ \ln Q = \ln\left(Q_0\, e^{-t/RC}\right) \]

The log of a product is the sum of the logs, \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\):

\[ \ln Q = \ln Q_0 + \ln\left(e^{-t/RC}\right) \]

\(\ln\) undoes \(e\), so \(\ln(e^{x}) = x\):

\[ \boxed{\ln Q = \ln Q_0 - \frac{t}{RC}} \]

Exactly the same steps on the p.d. and current equations give:

\[ \boxed{\ln V = \ln V_0 - \frac{t}{RC}} \qquad \boxed{\ln I = \ln I_0 - \frac{t}{RC}} \]

Compare with \(y = c + mx\): plotting \(\ln V\) against \(t\) gives a straight line with gradient \(-1/RC\) and intercept \(\ln V_0\).

  • Each has the form y = c + mx: plot ln Q (or ln I, ln V) against t to get a straight line of gradient −1/RC.
  • Rearranged to find a time: t = RC ln(V₀/V).

current and time during discharge

C = 100 µF charged to 12 V discharges through R = 2.0 kΩ. RC = 2.0 × 10³ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.20 s; I₀ = 12 ÷ 2000 = 6.0 mA.

Current after 0.30 s: I = 6.0 × e−0.30/0.20 = 6.0 × 0.223 = 1.3 mA

Time for V to fall to 3.0 V: t = RC ln(V₀/V) = 0.20 × ln(12/3.0) = 0.20 × 1.386 = 0.28 s

Charging equations

  • During charging the p.d. across the resistor decays: VR = V₀e−t/RC, and so does the current.
  • Because VC = V₀ − VR:

Capacitor charging

\[ \begin{aligned} V_C &= V_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) \\ Q &= Q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) \end{aligned} \]
\(V_0\): supply p.d. / V\(Q_0\): final charge, \(CV_0\) / C\(t\): time since charging began / s
  • Example: the same circuit charged from 12 V reaches 9.0 V when e−t/0.20 = 0.25, so t = 0.20 × ln 4 = 0.28 s.

Ways to find RC from a graph (all credited)

  1. Time-constant route: read the time when V = 0.37V₀ (discharge) or 0.63V₀ (charge).
  2. Tangent route: draw the tangent at t = 0; it meets the t-axis (or the final-value line) at t = RC.
  3. Substitution route: take a pair of (t, V) values and solve V = V₀e−t/RC using logs.
  4. Half-life route: read t½, then RC = t½/ln 2.
  5. Log-graph route: gradient of ln V against t = −1/RC.
Worked example

A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 12.0 V and then discharged through a 2.2 kΩ resistor. Calculate (a) the time constant, (b) the initial current, (c) the p.d. after 2.0 s, (d) the time for the p.d. to fall to 3.0 V and (e) the percentage of the original energy still stored at that moment.

Show solution

(a) \(RC = 2200 \times 470\times10^{-6} = 1.034\ \text{s} \approx 1.03\ \text{s}\)

(b) \(I_0 = V_0/R = 12.0/2200 = 5.45\times10^{-3}\ \text{A} = 5.45\ \text{mA}\)

(c) \(t/RC = 2.0/1.034 = 1.934\), so \(V = 12.0\, e^{-1.934} = 12.0 \times 0.1445 = 1.73\ \text{V}\)

(d) \(t = RC \ln(V_0/V) = 1.034 \times \ln(12.0/3.0) = 1.034 \times 1.386 = 1.43\ \text{s}\)

(e) \(W \propto V^2\), so the fraction left is \((3.0/12.0)^2 = 1/16 = 6.25\%\). Three quarters of the p.d. has gone, but nearly 94% of the energy has gone.

Quick checks

A charged capacitor discharges through a resistor. What fraction of its original charge is left after a time 2RC?

\(Q/Q_0 = e^{-2} = 0.135\). Each time constant multiplies the charge by 0.37, so two of them give \(0.37^2 \approx 0.14\). 0.25 would be two half-lives, and a half-life is shorter than \(RC\).

The p.d. across a capacitor is doubled. What happens to the energy stored?

\(W = \tfrac12 CV^2\) and \(C\) is fixed, so \(W \propto V^2\). Doubling \(V\) also doubles \(Q\), and \(W = \tfrac12 QV\) gives the same factor of 4.

Which of these is equivalent to the unit of the time constant, Ω × F?

\(\Omega = \text{V A}^{-1}\) and \(\text{F} = \text{C V}^{-1}\), so \(\Omega\,\text{F} = \text{C A}^{-1} = \text{(A s) A}^{-1} = \text{s}\).

An uncharged capacitor is connected in series with a resistor and a battery. At the instant the switch is closed, which statement is correct?

With no charge on it, the capacitor has no p.d. across it, so the whole e.m.f. is across the resistor. The current is \(V_0/R\), its largest value. It falls as \(V_C\) builds up.

A student plots ln I against t for a capacitor discharging through a resistor. What is the gradient of the line?

\(\ln I = \ln I_0 - t/RC\) has the form \(y = c + mx\) with \(m = -1/RC\). The intercept is \(\ln I_0\).

A capacitor is charged to a fixed p.d. and discharged through a resistor. The resistor is replaced with one of half the resistance and the experiment is repeated. Which statement is correct?

\(I_0 = V_0/R\) doubles and \(RC\) halves, so the discharge is twice as fast. But the capacitor started with the same \(Q_0 = CV_0\) and energy \(\tfrac12 CV_0^2\), and all of it still flows through the resistor (the area under the \(I\)–\(t\) graph is unchanged).
