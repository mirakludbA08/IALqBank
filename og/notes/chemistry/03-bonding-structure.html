Bonding and structure

Every chemical bond is an electrostatic attraction between positive and negative charges. What differs is where the charges are: between ions, between nuclei and shared electrons, or between metal ions and a sea of electrons. This topic explains each type of bond, the shapes that molecules take, how bonds become polar, and how the structure of a substance explains its melting temperature, conductivity and solubility.

Spec3.1Evidence for the existence of ions

Know and interpret the evidence that ions exist: the physical properties of ionic compounds, electron density maps, and the migration of ions.

Physical properties

Property of ionic compounds What it tells you
High melting and boiling temperatures strong forces act in every direction through a giant structure
Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, but not when solid charged particles exist; they are fixed in place in the solid but free to move in the liquid or solution
Often soluble in water, insoluble in non-polar solvents charged particles are attracted to polar water molecules
Brittle: they shatter when hit a small shift lines up like charges, which repel (3.4)
Electrolysis decomposes them, with products at both electrodes positive particles go to the cathode, negative ones to the anode

Electron density maps

  • X-ray diffraction measures how electron density is spread through a crystal. The result is drawn as a contour map, like a map of hills.
  • In sodium chloride each ion has its own separate set of roughly circular contours, and the electron density falls to almost zero between the ions. There is no build-up of electrons between them, so electrons are not shared: they belong to one ion or the other.
  • The Cl⁻ contours enclose a bigger region than the Na⁺ contours: Cl⁻ is the larger ion. Compare this with a covalent bond (3.10), where contours surround both nuclei and density stays high between them.

Migration of ions

If coloured ions move towards an electrode when a direct current flows, the colour must be carried by charged particles.

  • On a microscope slide: wet a strip of filter paper with an electrolyte (e.g. sodium chloride or copper(II) sulfate solution), clip it to a slide, connect each end to a low-voltage d.c. supply, and put a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) in the middle. A purple colour moves towards the positive electrode (anode): the MnO₄⁻ ion is negative. With a crystal of copper(II) sulfate, a blue colour moves towards the negative electrode (cathode): Cu²⁺ is positive.
  • In a U-tube: put green copper(II) chromate(VI) solution at the bottom and carefully layer a colourless electrolyte (e.g. dilute acid) on top in each arm. With a d.c. supply, a blue colour (Cu²⁺) rises towards the cathode and a yellow colour (CrO₄²⁻) towards the anode. The green colour was the two ions together.
  • Migration (3 marks): electrolysis using a d.c. supply; a crystal or solution of copper(II) sulfate on damp filter paper (on a microscope slide), or a U-tube; "blue copper(II) ions move to the negative electrode / cathode". A labelled diagram is accepted.
  • Solid ionic compound doesn't conduct because "the ions are fixed / not free to move". Not "there are no delocalised electrons".

Often asked as: describe an experiment to show ions migrate; MCQ on reading an electron density map; explain why an ionic compound conducts when molten but not when solid.

Saying a solid ionic compound doesn't conduct because it has "no free electrons". It has ions; they just can't move. Saying the electrons move in a molten ionic compound: the ions move.

Colours as evidence: blue Cu²⁺ moves to the cathode, purple MnO₄⁻ to the anode (WCH11 Oct 2025).

Spec3.2Forming ions by losing or gaining electrons

Describe how ions form when atoms lose or gain electrons.

  • Metals (few outer electrons) lose electrons to form positive ions (cations). Na: 2,8,1 → Na⁺: 2,8.
  • Non-metals (nearly full outer shell) gain electrons to form negative ions (anions). Cl: 2,8,7 → Cl⁻: 2,8,8.
  • In forming an ionic compound, electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms. The ions usually have the configuration of a noble gas.
  • In s, p, d notation: Mg 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² → Mg²⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (two 3s electrons lost); O 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ → O²⁻ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (two electrons gained into 2p).
  • The ion's charge follows from the group: Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 13 (Al) → 3+, Group 15 (N) → 3−, Group 16 → 2−, Group 17 → 1−.
  • "Electrons are transferred from the metal to the non-metal"; give the number of electrons and the charges ("each Mg atom loses two electrons to form Mg²⁺").

Often asked as: describe what happens to the electrons when sodium reacts with fluorine; part of a dot-and-cross question.

Saying atoms "share" electrons in an ionic compound. Describing the ion as the atom ("magnesium has a 2+ charge"): say magnesium ion.

Spec3.3Dot-and-cross diagrams for ionic compounds

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams to show the electrons in cations and anions.

How to draw one:

  1. Draw each ion separately, in square brackets, with its charge at the top right.
  2. Show the anion with a full outer shell of 8: its own electrons as dots, the electron(s) it gained as crosses.
  3. Show the cation with no outer electrons (or with the full shell beneath).
  4. Show the right number of each ion: CaCl₂ needs one Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ (or write "2" in front of [Cl]⁻).
Compound Ions drawn
NaF [Na]⁺ and [F]⁻ (8 electrons: 7 •, 1 ×)
MgO [Mg]²⁺ and [O]²⁻ (8 electrons: 6 •, 2 ×)
CaCl₂ [Ca]²⁺ and 2 [Cl]⁻
Al₂O₃ 2 [Al]³⁺ and 3 [O]²⁻
Li₃N 3 [Li]⁺ and [N]³⁻
  • One mark per ion, each with its charge. Na⁺ with 0 or 8 outer electrons; F⁻ with 8. Circles and brackets are not required.
  • ALLOW all dots or all crosses, or dots and crosses swapped. A missing charge may lose one mark.

Often asked as: dot-and-cross of NaF, NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂, BaCl₂, aluminium oxide.

  • Drawing a covalent (shared-pair) diagram for an ionic compound, or the reverse.
  • Wrong charges (Ca⁺), no charges at all, or a cation with a half-filled outer shell.

Spec3.4Ionic crystals are giant lattices

Describe ionic crystals as giant lattices of ions.

  • An ionic solid is a giant ionic lattice: a regular, repeating 3D arrangement of a huge number of positive and negative ions, alternating so that each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge.
  • In NaCl each Na⁺ has 6 Cl⁻ neighbours and each Cl⁻ has 6 Na⁺ (a cubic arrangement). MgO has the same structure.
  • The formula (NaCl) is only the ratio of ions; there are no molecules.
  • Brittleness: if a force shifts one layer by one ion, ions of the same charge line up next to each other, repel, and the crystal splits.
  • Drawing: "at least 9 correctly labelled ions", alternating + and −, in a regular (cubic) array.
  • Say "giant ionic lattice", not "molecule" or "macromolecule".

Often asked as: draw a section of the NaCl lattice; state the structure of a solid such as barium chloride.

Calling NaCl a molecule. Drawing ions of the same charge next to each other, or leaving ions unlabelled.

Spec3.5Ionic bonding is a net electrostatic attraction

Know that ionic bonding is the strong net electrostatic attraction between ions.

  • Ionic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • In the lattice every ion is attracted to all the oppositely charged ions around it and repelled by the like-charged ions slightly further away. The attractions are bigger than the repulsions, so there is a net attraction.
  • The attraction acts in all directions (it is non-directional), and there are huge numbers of ions, so a lot of energy is needed to separate them: high melting temperatures.
  • "(Strong) electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions" (or "between positive and negative ions").

Often asked as: define ionic bonding; explain why an ionic compound has a high melting temperature.

Saying "attraction between atoms", or "between the nucleus and electrons" (that's covalent or metallic). Saying ionic bonds are "weak".

Spec3.6How ionic charge and radius affect the strength of ionic bonding

Understand how ionic radius and ionic charge affect the strength of ionic bonding.

  • Higher charge on the ions → stronger attraction.
  • Smaller ions → the centres of the ions are closer → stronger attraction.
  • Stronger ionic bonding → more energy needed to separate the ions → higher melting temperature (and a more exothermic lattice energy, which you meet in Unit 4).
Compare Melting temperature / °C Reason
NaF vs NaCl 993 vs 801 F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻
NaCl vs KCl 801 vs 771 Na⁺ is smaller than K⁺
MgO vs NaCl 2852 vs 801 Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ are doubly charged (and Mg²⁺ is smaller)
BaCl₂ vs CsCl 962 vs 645 Ba²⁺ has a bigger charge and is smaller than Cs⁺

Worked example: a 4-mark comparison

Explain why barium chloride has a higher melting temperature than caesium chloride.

1. The barium ion has a greater charge (2+) than the caesium ion (1+).

2. The barium ion is smaller than the caesium ion.

3. So the electrostatic attraction between the ions is stronger in BaCl₂.

4. More energy is needed to overcome it, so the melting temperature is higher.

  • Name the ions and compare charge and size, then "stronger electrostatic attraction between the ions", then "more energy needed".
  • Comparing MgO with MgSO₄: "oxide ion is smaller than the sulfate ion" so stronger attraction between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻. Comparisons with sulfur or sulfide are not awarded.
  • "Higher charge density" is accepted.

Often asked as: compare the melting temperatures of two ionic compounds; MCQ ranking lattice strength.

  • Writing about atoms ("magnesium has a higher charge"): it's the magnesium ion.
  • Using atomic radius, electronegativity or intermolecular forces when the question says "consider the ions".
  • Confusing ionic charge with nuclear charge, or ionisation energy with bond strength.

Spec3.7Trends in ionic radius

Understand the trends in ionic radius down a group, and across an isoelectronic series such as N³⁻ to Al³⁺.

Ions compared with their atoms

  • A cation is smaller than its atom: it has lost its whole outer shell, and the same number of protons now pulls on fewer electrons. Na atom 0.186 nm → Na⁺ 0.102 nm.
  • An anion is bigger than its atom: extra electrons increase the repulsion between electrons, and the same nuclear charge holds more electrons. Cl atom 0.099 nm → Cl⁻ 0.181 nm.

Down a group

Ionic radius increases down a group (Li⁺ 0.076 < Na⁺ 0.102 < K⁺ 0.138 nm; F⁻ 0.133 < Cl⁻ 0.181 < Br⁻ 0.196 < I⁻ 0.220 nm), because each ion has one more shell of electrons.

An isoelectronic series

  • N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ are isoelectronic: all have 10 electrons, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, with the same shielding.
  • From N³⁻ to Al³⁺ the number of protons increases from 7 to 13. The bigger nuclear charge pulls the same 10 electrons closer, so the radius decreases.
  • Isoelectronic trend: "ionic radius decreases as atomic number increases" (a trend, not a comparison of two ions), "same number of electrons", "more protons" so electrons are pulled closer. DO NOT AWARD "atomic radius".
  • Na⁺ smaller than F⁻: same number of electrons, but Na⁺ has more protons, so the electrons are attracted more strongly. ALLOW "greater nuclear charge". IGNORE shielding.

Often asked as: plot the radii of N³⁻ to Al³⁺ and read off a value; describe and explain the trend; explain why Na⁺ is smaller than F⁻.

  • Comparing just N³⁻ and Al³⁺ when a trend for the whole series is asked.
  • Writing "atomic radius" or "smaller atom" for an ion.
  • Saying shielding changes across an isoelectronic series: it doesn't; only the number of protons changes.

Reading an unknown ionic radius from a line of best fit through the series (a range is accepted) (WCH11 Jan 2023).

Spec3.8Polarisation of ions

Understand what "polarisation" means for ions.

  • The simple ionic model treats ions as perfect spheres of charge.
  • In reality a cation attracts the electron cloud of a nearby anion and distorts it towards itself. This is polarisation.
  • Some electron density now sits between the two ions, a bit like a shared pair. So the bond gets some covalent character.
  • The more the anion is polarised, the more covalent the compound: lower melting temperature than expected, or even a molecular structure (AlCl₃ sublimes at about 180 °C; AlF₃ stays solid until about 1290 °C).
  • In Unit 4 you'll see the evidence: lattice energies calculated from the pure ionic model don't match experimental values for polarised compounds.
  • "The cation distorts / polarises the electron cloud of the anion", giving "covalent character".

Often asked as: explain why aluminium chloride has covalent character; why fluoride is hard to polarise (3.9).

Saying the cation is polarised. In this unit it is the anion's electron cloud that is distorted, by the cation.

Spec3.9Polarising power and polarisability

Understand that the polarising power of a cation depends on its radius and charge, and that the polarisability of an anion also depends on its radius and charge.

Bigger effect when the ion is… Examples (increasing)
Polarising power of a cation small and highly charged (high charge density) K⁺ < Na⁺ < Li⁺; Na⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Al³⁺; Ba²⁺ < Ca²⁺ < Be²⁺
Polarisability of an anion large and highly charged F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻; Cl⁻ < S²⁻
  • A small, highly charged cation concentrates its charge in a small volume, so it pulls hard on nearby electrons.
  • In a large anion the outer electrons are far from its nucleus and well shielded, so they are easily pulled away. Extra negative charge adds to this.
  • Most covalent character: small, highly charged cation + large, highly charged anion (e.g. AlI₃, BeCl₂, LiI). Most ionic: large, singly charged cation + small, singly charged anion (e.g. CsF, KF).

Worked example: ranking covalent character

Put LiF, LiI and KI in order of increasing covalent character.

Li⁺ is smaller than K⁺, so it is more polarising. I⁻ is larger than F⁻, so it is more polarisable.

KI (large cation) < LiF (small anion) < LiI (small cation and large anion) has the most covalent character.

(KI vs LiF can be argued either way; questions usually avoid that pair or give data.)

  • Covalent character in AlCl₃: "the aluminium ion is (very) small and highly charged", so it "polarises / distorts the chloride ion / anion".
  • Why F⁻ isn't polarised: "the fluoride ion has a single charge and a small ionic radius", so "the electron cloud is difficult to distort". The reverse argument for large, highly charged anions is allowed.
  • BeCl₂ vs CaCl₂: Be²⁺ is smaller / has a higher charge density, so it is more polarising (or: Be is more electronegative).

Often asked as: explain covalent character in AlCl₃ or BeCl₂; why the fluoride ion isn't easily polarised; MCQ on which cation is most polarising.

  • Using the cation's size to explain the anion's polarisability.
  • Saying fluoride is "large with a small charge". Not using the word distort.

Spec3.10Covalent bonding and the evidence for it

Understand that a covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between two nuclei and the shared pair of electrons between them, based on evidence from (i) the physical properties of giant atomic structures and (ii) electron density maps of simple molecules.

What a covalent bond is

  • A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the two nuclei and the shared pair of electrons between them.
  • Each atom usually contributes one electron to the pair. The shared pair sits between the nuclei and is attracted to both, which holds them together.
  • A double bond is two shared pairs; a triple bond is three.

(i) Evidence from giant atomic (giant covalent) structures

  • Diamond, graphite, silicon and silicon dioxide (SiO₂) are giant lattices of atoms joined by covalent bonds.
  • They have very high melting temperatures, are hard (except graphite), insoluble, and (except graphite) don't conduct.
  • To melt them you must break many strong covalent bonds, which needs a lot of energy. So covalent bonds must be strong. Their fixed shapes (e.g. tetrahedral around each atom in diamond and SiO₂) show the bonds are directional.

(ii) Evidence from electron density maps

  • In an electron density map of H₂ or Cl₂, the contours surround both nuclei together, and the electron density stays high in the region between the nuclei.
  • That build-up of electrons between the nuclei is the shared pair. Compare NaCl (3.1), where density falls to almost zero between the ions.
  • In HCl the contours are pulled towards the Cl atom, showing a polar bond (3.14).
  • Definition: "(electrostatic) attraction" between "the shared pair of electrons" and "the nuclei (of the two atoms)". Both the word attraction and the nuclei are needed.
  • Electron density map of a covalent molecule: contours drawn around both nuclei, enclosed by a shared contour; not two separate circles.
  • Giant covalent: "giant lattice", "many strong covalent bonds", "a lot of energy to break them".

Often asked as: define a covalent bond (often poorly answered); sketch or interpret an electron density map; explain the high melting temperature of SiO₂ or carbon.

  • Defining a covalent bond as "a shared pair of electrons" with no attraction and no nuclei.
  • Mentioning ions, metallic bonding or intermolecular forces for a giant covalent structure.
  • Drawing a contour map that looks like two separate atoms.
  • σ and π bonds (spec 5.1): a σ bond is the head-on overlap of orbitals between the nuclei; a π bond is the sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the σ bond. A double bond is one σ + one π (WCH11 Jan 2022).
  • SiO₂ is giant covalent: each Si bonded to 4 O atoms tetrahedrally, each O to 2 Si; no mobile electrons, so it doesn't conduct (WCH11 Oct 2021).

Spec3.11Dot-and-cross diagrams for covalent substances, including dative bonds

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for covalent substances: (i) molecules with single, double and triple bonds; (ii) species with dative covalent (coordinate) bonds, including Al₂Cl₆ and the ammonium ion.

(i) Single, double and triple bonds

Rules:

  1. Use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's.
  2. Put each shared pair between the two atoms (in the overlap if you draw circles). One pair = single bond, two = double, three = triple.
  3. Show all the non-bonding (lone) pairs, so you can count 8 outer electrons round each atom (2 for H).
  4. Then count: every atom's own electrons must add up to its group number.
Molecule Bonds Lone pairs
Cl₂ one single bond 3 on each Cl
H₂O two O–H 2 on O
NH₃ three N–H 1 on N
CH₄ four C–H none
O₂ O=O (two shared pairs) 2 on each O
CO₂ two C=O 2 on each O, none on C
C₂H₄ C=C and four C–H none
N₂ N≡N (three shared pairs) 1 on each N

Exceptions to the octet rule

  • Electron-deficient central atoms have fewer than 8: BeCl₂ (4 electrons round Be), BCl₃ and AlCl₃ (6 round B or Al).
  • Expanded octets: elements in Period 3 and below can have more than 8, because they have d orbitals available: PCl₅ (10 round P), SF₆ (12 round S). Nitrogen can't do this, so NCl₅ doesn't exist.

(ii) Dative covalent (coordinate) bonds

  • A dative covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons in which both electrons come from the same atom.
  • You need a donor with a lone pair and an acceptor with an empty orbital (often an electron-deficient atom, or H⁺).
  • Show it with an arrow from the donor atom to the acceptor atom. In a dot-and-cross diagram, both electrons in that pair are the same symbol (both from the donor).
  • Once formed, a dative bond is identical to any other covalent bond: all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are the same.
Species Donor → acceptor
NH₄⁺ lone pair on N of NH₃ → H⁺
H₃O⁺ lone pair on O of H₂O → H⁺
Al₂Cl₆ lone pair on a Cl of one AlCl₃ → the Al of the other AlCl₃ (two dative bonds)
NH₃BF₃ lone pair on N → B (which had only 6 electrons)
  • AlCl₃ has only 6 electrons round Al. At lower temperatures in the vapour, two AlCl₃ molecules join: a lone pair on a Cl of each molecule is donated to the Al of the other, giving the dimer Al₂Cl₆.
  • Dot-and-cross: any combination of dots, crosses or circles; inner shells ignored. BeCl₂: "2 single covalent bonds" and "6 non-bonding electrons on each chlorine"; no lone pair on Be. An ionic diagram for a covalent molecule scores 0.
  • Dative bond definition: "a (shared) pair of electrons" where "both electrons come from the same atom".
  • Dative arrow goes from the donor (Cl) to the acceptor (Al). In a dot-and-cross, the two dative electrons "must appear the same".
  • NH₄⁺: square brackets and a + charge, with one N–H shown as coordinate.
  • Expanded octet (ClF₃): "10 electrons / 5 pairs in the outer shell of chlorine", "has expanded its octet".

Often asked as: dot-and-cross of N₂, Br₂, CCl₄, SCl₂, BeCl₂, NCl₃, B(OH)₃, ClF₃, SbCl₅, SF₄; Al₂Cl₆ with dative bonds; NH₄⁺; define a dative bond; why N can't form NCl₅.

  • Lone pairs left off Cl or Br; an extra lone pair added to Be or B.
  • A single bond in N₂, or drawing a halogen molecule when N₂ was asked.
  • Dative arrows from Al to Cl; an Al–Al bond; extra Cl⁻ ions in Al₂Cl₆.
  • Drawing NH₃ when NH₄⁺ was asked, or leaving off the charge. Calling the dative pair a "lone pair" after it has bonded.
  • Merging O and H into one symbol in B(OH)₃; diagrams too small to count.
  • Expanded octets in unfamiliar molecules: ClF₃, SbCl₅, SF₄, S₂F₁₀ (WCH11 Oct 2019, Jan 2021, Jan 2025).
  • Why nitrogen can't form NCl₅: it has no 2d orbitals (no available orbitals in the second shell), so it can't expand its octet (WCH11 Jan 2021).
  • Negative ions: add the extra electron(s) to the central atom's count, e.g. GeH₃⁻ has a lone pair on Ge (WCH11 Oct 2022). Oxoanions such as SO₄²⁻ (WCH11 Oct 2025).

Spec3.12Giant lattices of carbon: graphite, diamond and graphene

Describe the giant structures of carbon (graphite, diamond and graphene) and discuss their uses.

Diamond Graphite Graphene
Structure giant covalent; each C bonded to 4 others, tetrahedral (109.5°) giant covalent layers of hexagons; each C bonded to 3 others (120°) a single layer of graphite, one atom thick
Delocalised electrons none: all 4 outer electrons in bonds one per C, free to move along the layers one per C, across the sheet
Between layers n/a weak London forces; layers 0.335 nm apart n/a
Electrical conductivity doesn't conduct conducts (along the layers) conducts very well
Hardness very hard soft, slippery: layers slide very strong for its mass; flexible
Density 3.5 g cm⁻³ (higher) 2.3 g cm⁻³ (lower: layers far apart) very light; almost transparent
Uses cutting tools, drill tips, jewellery electrodes, lubricant, pencils electronics, touchscreens, light strong composites (e.g. sports equipment), conductive inks, sensors
  • Both diamond and graphite have very high melting temperatures: graphite's layers are held by weak forces, but to melt it you must break the strong covalent bonds within the layers.
  • Each use should be linked to a property: graphite electrodes because it conducts, has a high melting temperature and is fairly unreactive; lubricant because the layers slide; diamond cutting tools because it is the hardest substance.
  • Diamond: "each carbon covalently bonded to four others"; "no delocalised electrons", so no conduction; hard. Graphite: "layers", "each carbon bonded to three others", "one delocalised electron per atom", so it conducts; "weak forces between layers", so they slide.
  • Differences must be properties (conductivity, hardness, density). Melting temperature is not accepted as a difference between diamond and graphite.
  • Graphene: "a single layer of graphite"; a use with the property that suits it (strong, light, conducts, transparent). "Lubricant" alone is not credited for graphene.
  • Drawing graphite: "at least two layers of hexagons (at least 3 hexagons per layer)" and "labelled forces of attraction between the layers".

Often asked as: compare diamond and graphite (properties and structure); relate graphene to graphite and give a use; why graphite is used as an electrode; draw graphite layers; explain the high melting temperature of carbon.

  • Calling graphite or diamond "molecular", or saying diamond has "intermolecular forces".
  • Mixing up the weak forces between layers with weak bonds within the layers.
  • Writing about diamond, then graphite, in separate paragraphs in a "compare" question: compare in the same sentence.
  • Confusing graphene with graphite.
  • Showing the electrons in diamond as a dot-and-cross section: each C with four shared pairs (WCH11 Jun 2024).
  • Graphite as the electrode in aluminium extraction at about 950 °C: giant covalent, high melting temperature, delocalised electrons carry the current (WCH11 Jun 2023).

Spec3.13Electronegativity

Understand what electronegativity means for atoms in a covalent bond.

  • Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • It is measured on the Pauling scale. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0). Noble gases have no value (they don't usually bond).
Group 1 2 13 14 15 16 17
Period 1 H 2.1
Period 2 Li 1.0 Be 1.5 B 2.0 C 2.5 N 3.0 O 3.5 F 4.0
Period 3 Na 0.9 Mg 1.2 Al 1.5 Si 1.8 P 2.1 S 2.5 Cl 3.0
Period 4 K 0.8 Ca 1.0 Br 2.8

Trends

  • Increases across a period: more protons (bigger nuclear charge), similar shielding, smaller atom, so the bonding pair is attracted more strongly.
  • Decreases down a group: more shells, more shielding and a larger distance from the nucleus to the bonding pair, so the attraction is weaker.
  • "The ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons (in a covalent bond)." "Attract electrons" alone is too vague.

Often asked as: define electronegativity; use values to decide which end of a bond is δ−.

Defining electronegativity as "how easily an atom gains an electron" (that's electron affinity) or leaving out "in a covalent bond / bonding pair".

Spec3.14The bonding continuum: from covalent to ionic

Know that ionic and covalent bonding are the two extremes of a continuum, and explain this through electronegativity differences: polar bonds, polar molecules, and ionic bonding when the difference is large enough.

Electronegativity difference Bond Example
zero pure (non-polar) covalent: pair shared equally H–H, Cl–Cl
small polar covalent: pair pulled towards the more electronegative atom, which gets δ−; the other gets δ+ Hδ+–Clδ− (difference 0.9)
large ionic: the electron is effectively transferred NaCl (2.1), NaF (3.1)
  • Write polar bonds as Hδ+–Clδ−. The δ− goes on the more electronegative atom.
  • There is no sharp cut-off between the types. A polar covalent bond has some ionic character; an ionic bond with a polarised anion has some covalent character (3.8). Bonds sit somewhere on a continuum.
  • The bigger the difference, the more ionic character: BaCl₂ (3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1) is more ionic than BeCl₂ (3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5).
  • "Ionic and covalent are extremes of a continuum"; the electronegativity difference decides where a bond lies. Both compounds have "some ionic and some covalent character"; one is "more ionic / less covalent".
  • State which element is more electronegative, then put δ− on it.

Often asked as: use electronegativity values to compare two compounds; mark δ+ and δ− on a bond.

  • Treating a difference as a fixed cut-off ("1.7 or more is ionic"). Examiners mark that as a misconception.
  • Putting δ+ and δ− the wrong way round; drawing a dipole arrow when δ+/δ− was asked.

Spec3.15Polar bonds and polar molecules

Distinguish between polar bonds and polar molecules, and predict whether a molecule is likely to be polar.

  • A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be non-polar overall. It is a polar molecule only if the bond dipoles don't cancel: one end of the molecule is slightly positive and the other slightly negative (a permanent dipole).
  • Symmetrical shapes with identical outer atoms and no lone pair on the central atom cancel out.
Molecule Polar bonds? Shape Polar molecule?
CO₂ yes (C=O) linear no: dipoles cancel
CCl₄, CH₄ C–Cl yes; C–H nearly non-polar tetrahedral no
BF₃ yes trigonal planar no
SF₆ yes octahedral no
H₂O yes bent yes
NH₃ yes trigonal pyramidal yes
HCl yes linear (two atoms) yes
CHCl₃, CH₃Cl yes tetrahedral but not symmetrical yes

Method

  1. Work out the shape (3.18 and 3.19).
  2. Find the polar bonds (electronegativity difference).
  3. Ask whether the bond dipoles cancel because of symmetry.

The charged rod test

Run a thin jet of liquid from a burette past a charged rod (rubbed polythene or acetate). Jets of water and ethanol bend towards the rod: their molecules are polar and turn so their oppositely charged end faces the rod. A jet of cyclohexane isn't deflected: its molecules are non-polar. The polar jet bends towards the rod whether the rod is positive or negative.

  • CCl₄: "chlorine is more electronegative than carbon (so the bonds are polar)" but "the molecule is not polar because the dipoles cancel / the molecule is symmetrical". IGNORE "the polar bonds cancel".
  • Polar molecule: "one end slightly positive and the other slightly negative".
  • CO₂: oxygen is more electronegative, so δ+ on C and δ− on each O; but linear, so non-polar.

Often asked as: is CCl₄ / CO₂ polar, and why; define a polar molecule; MCQ from jet deflection.

  • Arguing that a difference is "not big enough" to be polar.
  • Getting the shape wrong in one part and carrying it into the polarity answer.
  • Calling C–H bonds strongly polar.
  • Predicting polarity of an unfamiliar molecule from its shape: SF₄ (see-saw) is polar, SF₆ (octahedral) is not (WCH11 Jan 2025).
  • Induced dipoles: the π electrons of an alkene repel the electrons in a Cl₂ or Br₂ molecule, distorting its electron cloud and inducing a dipole (WCH11 Jan 2025; see Topic 5).

Spec3.16Electron-pair repulsion theory

Understand the principles of electron-pair repulsion theory, used to explain and predict the shapes of simple molecules and ions.

The principles

  1. The electron pairs in the outer shell of the central atom (both bonding pairs and lone pairs) repel each other.
  2. They arrange themselves as far apart as possible, to minimise repulsion. That arrangement fixes the shape.
  3. Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, because a lone pair is held closer to the central nucleus and spreads out more. The order is: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair.
  4. Each lone pair squeezes the bonds closer together, cutting the bond angle by roughly 2.5° (109.5° → 107° → 104.5°).
  5. A double (or triple) bond counts as one bonding region for working out the shape.
  6. The shape is named from the atoms only, but the lone pairs still affect it.

Counting the pairs

For a central atom bonded only by single bonds:

  • electrons = central atom's outer electrons + 1 for each bonded atom + 1 for each negative charge − 1 for each positive charge
  • pairs = electrons ÷ 2; lone pairs = pairs − bonding pairs.
  • If double bonds are present, draw the dot-and-cross diagram first and count each double bond as one region.
Total pairs (regions) Arrangement Angle with no lone pairs
2 linear 180°
3 trigonal planar 120°
4 tetrahedral 109.5°
5 trigonal bipyramidal 120° and 90°
6 octahedral 90°

Nitrogen has five outer electrons. Three are shared with the three hydrogen atoms, which leaves one lone pair. So there are 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair.

The four electron pairs repel each other and move as far apart as possible, into a tetrahedral arrangement.

The lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs, so it pushes the N–H bonds slightly closer together.

The shape is trigonal pyramidal and the H–N–H bond angle is 107°, smaller than the 109.5° of a regular tetrahedron.

Exam answers need all of these: the number of each kind of pair, that pairs repel to get as far apart as possible, that lone pairs repel more (or, with no lone pairs, that the pairs repel equally), and then the name of the shape and the angle.

  • "Electron pairs repel (each other)" and "get as far apart as possible / to minimise repulsion". ALLOW "maximum separation".
  • "Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs."
  • Teach and use electron pairs. One recent mark scheme ignored "bonds" or "electron regions"; another accepted regions of electron density for a double bond. So say "pairs of electrons", and say a double bond "counts as one region".

Often asked as: inside every shapes question (3.18, 3.19).

  • "The atoms (or bonds) repel", or "the molecule moves to minimise repulsion". It is the electron pairs that repel.
  • Saying lone pairs repel "the atoms".
  • Quoting "2.5° per lone pair" without the reason (lone pairs repel more).

Spec3.17Bond length and bond angle

Understand the terms bond length and bond angle.

  • Bond length: the distance between the nuclei of two atoms joined by a covalent bond. It is where attraction (nuclei to the shared pair) and repulsion (nucleus to nucleus) balance.
  • Bond angle: the angle between two bonds that meet at the same atom (between the lines joining the nuclei).
  • More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: multiple bonds are shorter and stronger.

Worked example: the full explanation for NH₃

  1. N has 5 outer electrons; 3 H atoms add 3 → 8 electrons = 4 pairs: 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair.
  2. The electron pairs repel and get as far apart as possible: a tetrahedral arrangement of pairs.
  3. The lone pair repels more than the bonding pairs, so it squeezes the N–H bonds together.
  4. Shape: trigonal pyramidal; bond angle 107° (less than 109.5°).

Worked example: why CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O differ

All three have 4 electron pairs round the central atom.

CH₄: 4 bonding pairs, 0 lone pairs → 109.5°.

NH₃: 3 bonding pairs, 1 lone pair → 107°.

H₂O: 2 bonding pairs, 2 lone pairs → 104.5°.

Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, so each extra lone pair reduces the angle.

Sketch 3D shapes with wedges and dashes, label the angle between two bonds at the central atom, and give one value per angle. Write "trigonal bipyramidal" in full: "trigonal pyramidal" is a different shape.

  • Standard 2-mark answer: the angle (e.g. 109.5°) and "(four) electron pairs / bonding pairs repel to get maximum separation / minimum repulsion".
  • Always state the numbers of bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  • Water-like angles: 103° to 104.5° accepted in recent mark schemes. Learn 104.5°.
  • CO₂: two C=O regions, no lone pairs on C → linear, 180°.
  • PCl₅: "trigonal bipyramidal", "90° and 120°". SF₆: "octahedral", "90°".

Often asked as: a table of bonding pairs, lone pairs, shape and angle (CH₄, NH₃, H₂O); explain why the angles differ; explain the shape of a named molecule; NH₃ vs NH₄⁺.

  • Forgetting a lone pair, which turns NH₃ or NCl₃ into "trigonal planar".
  • Calling the dative pair in NH₄⁺ a lone pair.
  • Mentioning lone pair–lone pair repulsion in a molecule with only one lone pair (NH₃, PCl₃).
  • "Trigonal pyramidal" for BCl₃ or AlCl₃ (no lone pair: trigonal planar).
  • Not knowing both angles in PCl₅.

Spec3.19Predicting the shapes of other molecules and ions

Apply electron-pair repulsion theory to predict the shapes and bond angles of molecules and ions similar to those in 3.18.

Method (show every step)

  1. Find the central atom's outer electrons (its group number).
  2. Add one for each atom bonded by a single bond; add the negative charge, or subtract the positive charge.
  3. Halve it to get the number of pairs; split them into bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  4. Pick the arrangement from the number of pairs (3.16), then name the shape from the atoms.
  5. Adjust the angle for lone pairs (about 2.5° less per lone pair, from 109.5°).

Worked example: four analogues

PCl₄⁺: 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 → 4 pairs, 4 bonding, 0 lone → tetrahedral, 109.5° (like NH₄⁺).

PCl₆⁻: 5 + 6 + 1 = 12 → 6 pairs, 6 bonding → octahedral, 90° (like SF₆).

NCl₃: 5 + 3 = 8 → 4 pairs, 3 bonding, 1 lone → trigonal pyramidal, 107° (like NH₃).

ClF₃: 7 + 3 = 10 → 5 pairs, 3 bonding, 2 lone → T-shaped, about 87.5° (just under 90°). The two lone pairs sit in the equatorial positions, where they have most room.

Start with the central atom's outer electrons: oxygen has 6.

Add one electron for each atom bonded by a single bond (3 H), then adjust for the charge: a 1+ charge means one electron fewer. \( 6 + 3 - 1 = 8 \) electrons, which is 4 pairs.

Three pairs bond to H atoms, so there are 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair: the same as ammonia.

H3O+ is trigonal pyramidal, with bond angles of about 107°.

Xenon has 8 outer electrons, and the four fluorine atoms add one each: \( 8 + 4 = 12 \) electrons, so 6 pairs.

Four pairs bond to F atoms, leaving 2 lone pairs. Six pairs point to the corners of an octahedron.

The two lone pairs repel each other most, so they take opposite positions, 180° apart, above and below the plane.

The four F atoms lie in a flat square around Xe: the shape is square planar and the F–Xe–F bond angle is 90°.

  • Quote "bond pairs and lone pairs" for each centre: e.g. in a ring with an O atom, "four bond pairs around C (109.5°); two bond pairs and two lone pairs around O (104.5°)".
  • Shape "and justification": "electron pairs arranged to minimise repulsion", with the count of pairs.
  • SF₄: see-saw, angles about 90° and 180° (about 173° accepted). TlCl₄³⁻: has a lone pair on Tl, so see-saw.
  • Deduce the charge of an ion such as PCl₄⁺ or PCl₆⁻ from its shape.

  • Not counting the lone pairs from the dot-and-cross diagram you've just drawn.
  • Forgetting to add or subtract electrons for the ion's charge.
  • Giving 180° for an O–B–O angle in B(OH)₃ (it's trigonal planar at B, 120°).
  • The beryllium chloride polymer in the solid: each Be has four bonding pairs (two of them dative), so it is tetrahedral round Be (WCH11 Oct 2021).
  • Shapes with 5 or 6 pairs including lone pairs: ClF₃, SF₄, TlCl₄³⁻ (WCH11 Oct 2019, Jan 2025, Oct 2024).
  • Angles in organic molecules: 109.5° at a saturated C, 104.5° at an O with two lone pairs (WCH11 Jan 2020).

Spec3.20The structure of metals

Understand that metals are giant lattices of metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.

  • In a metal, each atom releases its outer electrons, which become delocalised: free to move through the whole structure, not tied to any one atom.
  • What's left is a giant lattice of positive metal ions (cations), arranged in regular layers, held in a "sea" of delocalised electrons.
  • The number of delocalised electrons per ion equals the ion's charge: Na gives 1 (Na⁺), Mg gives 2 (Mg²⁺), Al gives 3 (Al³⁺).
  • Diagram: a regular array of cations with charges, plus electrons in an irregular arrangement between them, about twice as many electrons as ions for a 2+ ion. Label the electrons or show them as e⁻ or dots.

Often asked as: draw a labelled diagram of calcium, silver or magnesium.

Drawing too many or too few electrons, or arranging them in a regular grid so the diagram looks ionic. Calling the particles "atoms" instead of ions.

Spec3.21Metallic bonding

Know that metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between metal ions and delocalised electrons.

  • Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons.
  • It acts throughout the lattice and isn't directional.
  • It gets stronger when the ions have a higher charge and there are more delocalised electrons per ion (and when the ions are smaller). So: Na < Mg < Al.

Worked example: magnesium vs sodium

Explain why magnesium has a higher melting temperature than sodium.

1. Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ ions; sodium forms Na⁺ ions (higher charge).

2. Magnesium has two delocalised electrons per atom; sodium has one.

3. So the attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to overcome it.

  • "(Strong) electrostatic attraction between (metal) cations / positive ions and delocalised electrons". The word strong was required (WCH11 Jan 2023). IGNORE protons or nuclei. Any mention of another bond type scores 0.
  • Mg vs Na (2 marks): greater charge on the ion and more delocalised electrons. In this mark scheme ionic size was ignored and "just a higher charge" wasn't enough.

Often asked as: describe metallic bonding in sodium or aluminium; compare the melting temperatures of two metals.

Saying "free electrons" or "charge carriers" instead of delocalised electrons. Describing attraction between positive and negative ions in a metal. Leaving out "strong".

Spec3.22Explaining the properties of metals

Use the metallic bonding model to explain simple properties of metals, including electrical conductivity and high melting temperature.

Property Explanation
Conduct electricity (solid and liquid) the delocalised electrons can move through the structure; when a potential difference is applied they flow and carry the charge
High melting and boiling temperatures the strong attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons needs a lot of energy to overcome
Good thermal conductors delocalised electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the lattice
Malleable and ductile layers of ions can slide over each other; the delocalised electrons move with them, so the bonding isn't broken

Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements. The ions of different sizes disrupt the regular layers, so the layers can't slide over each other as easily: alloys are harder and stronger than the pure metal.

A white solid melts at 1710 °C. It does not conduct electricity as a solid or when molten, and it does not dissolve in water. What type of structure does it have?

The very high melting temperature rules out a simple molecular structure: something strong holds every particle in place.

It does not conduct when molten, so it has no ions that become mobile on melting: it is not ionic. It does not conduct as a solid, so it is not a metal.

It must be giant covalent. (These are the properties of silicon(IV) oxide.)

Check conductivity in both states: solid only tells metal and graphite apart from the rest; molten tells ionic compounds apart from giant covalent ones.

  • Conductivity: "delocalised electrons that move / flow" in both the solid and the liquid. Any reference to ions moving in a metal is not awarded.
  • Malleability: "layers of cations slide over each other" and "the bonding is not broken".
  • Alloys: "ions of different size" so "layers don't slide as easily". The word ion is needed.
  • A stated use needs a matching property (e.g. aluminium cables: good conductor, low density, ductile).

Often asked as: why a metal conducts when solid and when molten; why it is malleable; why an alloy is harder; why aluminium is used for a given job.

  • "Electrons are delocalised" without saying they move.
  • Explaining a metal's conductivity by the charge on its ions alone.
  • Mixing models: "delocalised electrons in the molten ionic compound" or "mobile ions in the metal".
  • A general list of metal properties when the question is about one metal and one use.
  • Alloys: silver alloyed with copper is harder because the copper ions are a different size and stop the layers sliding (WCH11 Jun 2024).
  • Choosing aluminium for a use from its conductivity, ductility and low density (WCH11 Oct 2021).

Quick checks

What is the shape of a PCl3 molecule, and its approximate bond angle?

Phosphorus has 5 outer electrons; three bond to Cl, leaving one lone pair. Four pairs give a tetrahedral arrangement, and the lone pair squeezes the bonds to about 107°, so the shape is pyramidal. Trigonal planar would need three pairs and no lone pair, as in BCl3.

Why does magnesium oxide have a much higher melting temperature than sodium chloride?

Both compounds are giant ionic lattices. Doubly charged ions, closer together, attract each other much more strongly, so more energy is needed to separate them. MgO has no molecules, so "intermolecular forces" is wrong.

Which of these molecules is polar?

All four contain polar bonds, but in CO2, CCl4 and BF3 the symmetrical shape makes the bond dipoles cancel. In CHCl3 one position holds H instead of Cl, so the dipoles no longer cancel and the molecule is polar.

Which ion has the largest radius?

All four have 10 electrons. Oxide has the fewest protons (8), so its electrons are held least tightly and it is the biggest. Mg2+, with 12 protons, is the smallest.

Why does graphite conduct electricity but diamond does not?

Conduction needs mobile charged particles. Each carbon in graphite uses only three electrons in bonds, and the fourth is delocalised within its layer. Sliding layers explain why graphite is soft, not why it conducts. Graphite contains no ions.

The H–O–H bond angle in water is 104.5°, smaller than 109.5°. Why?

Oxygen has four electron pairs, arranged roughly tetrahedrally. Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, and two lone pairs squeeze the angle by about 5° from 109.5°. Electronegativity affects the polarity of the bonds, not the angle.

In the ammonium ion, NH4+, where do the two electrons of the dative bond come from?

An H+ ion has no electrons at all, so both electrons in the new bond come from nitrogen's lone pair. Once formed, the four N–H bonds are identical and the ion is tetrahedral.

A grey solid melts at 1083 °C, conducts electricity as a solid and when molten, and is insoluble in water. What is its structure?

Conducting as a solid points to delocalised electrons, and conducting when molten as well, with a high melting temperature, fits a metal (this is copper). An ionic solid does not conduct until it melts.
