Carbonyls, carboxylic acids and chirality

The C=O group is the busiest functional group in organic chemistry. This topic covers aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and the compounds made from them, the mirror-image molecules that some of their reactions produce, and the instrumental methods (NMR, mass spectrometry and chromatography) that chemists use to work out what they have made.

Spec15.1Optical isomerism and the chiral centre

Know that optical isomerism comes from chirality in molecules with a single chiral centre.

What makes a molecule chiral

  • A chiral centre (asymmetric carbon) is a carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups. Mark it with an asterisk (*).
  • A molecule with one chiral centre is chiral: it is not superimposable on its mirror image, like your left and right hands.
  • So it exists as two optical isomers (enantiomers, 15.2). Optical isomerism is a type of stereoisomerism: same structural formula, different arrangement in space.

Finding the chiral centre

  1. Ignore any carbon with two identical groups: CH₃, CH₂ and C=C or C=O carbons (only three groups) can't be chiral.
  2. For each remaining carbon, list the four groups. Look along each branch until the groups differ: CH₂CH₃ and CH₂CH₂CH₃ are different.
  3. If all four are different, it's a chiral centre.

Often asked as: explain why a compound shows optical isomerism (1–2 marks); MCQ "which compound has a chiral centre?"; explain why one product is chiral and another isn't (e.g. the HCN products of pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one).

  • "Four different molecules" attached to the carbon: it's four different atoms or groups.
  • Listing several reasons when one is wrong: a wrong statement cancels the right one.
  • Confusing a chiral centre with a planar molecule or a planar carbocation.
  • Counting several chiral centres in a bigger molecule: menthol has 3. With n chiral centres there can be up to 2ⁿ optical isomers, so menthol has 8 (WCH14 Jun 2021). The 2ⁿ rule over-counts if the molecule is symmetrical.
  • Chiral carbons "hidden" in rings, such as in the cyclic dimer of lactic acid (lactide) (WCH14 Jan 2023).

Spec15.2Enantiomers and 3D diagrams

Understand that optical isomers (enantiomers) are object and non-superimposable mirror image, and draw them in 3D.

Enantiomers

  • The two optical isomers are called enantiomers. They are mirror images of each other that can't be superimposed: however you turn one, it never matches the other.
  • They have the same boiling point, melting point, density and chemical reactions with non-chiral reagents. They differ in:
    • the direction they rotate plane-polarised light (15.3);
    • how they react with other chiral molecules, such as enzymes and receptors. That's why one enantiomer of a drug can be useful and the other inactive or harmful, and why two enantiomers can smell different.

Drawing them

  1. Draw the chiral carbon with a tetrahedral arrangement: two bonds in the plane of the paper (plain lines), one wedge (coming towards you) and one hashed bond (going away). Put the wedge and hashed bonds next to each other.
  2. Draw a dashed mirror line.
  3. Draw the mirror image: reflect every group across the line. Don't just swap two labels at random and don't draw the same isomer twice.
  • "a three-dimensional diagram … showing at least one dotted bond and at least one wedged bond which are next to each other", then "the mirror image of the first structure".
  • Groups in any order; CO₂H for COOH is fine.

Often asked as: draw both enantiomers of lactic acid, 2-hydroxybutanenitrile or another product (2 marks).

  • Drawing the same enantiomer twice. The safest way is to draw a true reflection across a mirror line.
  • Flat (2D) structures with no wedge or hashed bonds, or bonds at 90° with no 3D shape.
  • Wrong atoms joined (e.g. the bond going to the H of OH): draw the bond to the atom actually attached.

Spec15.3Optical activity

Know that optical activity is the ability of one optical isomer to rotate the plane of polarisation of plane-polarised monochromatic light.

Plane-polarised light

  • Ordinary light vibrates in all planes at right angles to its direction. A polarising filter lets through only light vibrating in one plane: plane-polarised light.
  • Monochromatic light (one wavelength, e.g. from a sodium lamp) is used because the angle of rotation depends on wavelength.

Rotating the plane

  • A solution of one enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light. It is optically active.
  • The other enantiomer rotates it by the same angle in the opposite direction: one clockwise (+), one anticlockwise (−).
  • A polarimeter measures this: light source → polariser → tube of sample solution → second polarising filter (analyser) that you rotate until the light passing through is brightest (or darkest); the angle turned is the rotation.
  • "one isomer rotates the plane of (monochromatic) plane-polarised light in one direction and the other in the opposite direction"; ALLOW clockwise and anticlockwise.
  • "Optically active" means the molecules "rotate the plane of plane-polarised light".

Often asked as: how the two enantiomers differ (1–2 marks); what "optically active" means; IP1 of a 6-mark answer on optical isomerism.

  • Saying the light is "reflected", "deflected" or "bent": it is the plane of polarisation that is rotated.
  • Mentioning plane-polarised light but not saying the two isomers rotate it in opposite directions.
  • An SN2 product made from one enantiomer is optically active and "rotates plane-polarised light in the opposite direction" (inversion) (WCH14 Oct 2024). See 15.5.

Spec15.4Racemic mixtures

Know what a racemic mixture is.

Definition

  • A racemic mixture (racemate) contains equal amounts (equal moles) of the two enantiomers: a 50 : 50 mixture.
  • It is optically inactive: the rotation caused by one enantiomer is cancelled by the equal and opposite rotation of the other.
  • A reaction that makes a chiral centre from non-chiral starting materials gives a racemic mixture, because there is an equal chance of forming each enantiomer (15.5). Separating them is difficult and expensive, which matters for drugs where only one enantiomer works.
  • "(mixture containing) equal amounts of (both) enantiomers"; ACCEPT "equimolar / 50 : 50 mixture".
  • Why inactive: "the rotations caused by both enantiomers cancel".
  • Just stating that equal amounts of each enantiomer form scores the racemic-mixture mark.

Often asked as: define a racemic mixture; explain why a product is not optically active although it has a chiral centre.

  • Saying only that a racemic mixture "has no optical activity" without saying what it contains.
  • Saying "two enantiomers" without "equal amounts".
  • Partial racemisation: a product with more of one enantiomer than the other is optically active, but less so than the starting material. It shows the reaction went partly by SN1 and partly by SN2 (WCH14 Oct 2025).

  • Writing "SN1" or "SN2" in an answer about nucleophilic addition: it automatically loses the marks.
  • Saying "a planar carbocation" forms in the HCN reaction. There is no carbocation; the C=O group is planar.
  • Saying "the molecule is planar": only the arrangement around the carbonyl carbon is.
  • Giving the stock SN1 answer (racemic) for an SN2 reaction.
  • Which product shows optical isomerism: the HCN product of pentan-2-one has a chiral carbon; the product of pentan-3-one (two ethyl groups) doesn't. Both ketones still react (WCH14 Jan 2025).

Spec15.6Naming and drawing aldehydes and ketones

Name aldehydes and ketones and draw their structural, displayed and skeletal formulae.

The functional groups

  • Both contain the carbonyl group, C=O.
  • Aldehyde: C=O at the end of the chain, written –CHO. Name ends -al. The CHO carbon is always C1, so no number is needed: methanal HCHO, ethanal CH₃CHO, propanal CH₃CH₂CHO.
  • Ketone: C=O inside the chain, joined to two carbons, written –CO–. Name ends -one, with a number when needed: propanone CH₃COCH₃, butanone CH₃COCH₂CH₃, pentan-2-one CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃, pentan-3-one CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃.
  • Aldehydes and ketones with the same number of carbons are functional group isomers: propanal and propanone are both C₃H₆O.

Names with substituents

  • Number the chain so the C=O gets the lowest number, then add substituents: 2-methylpropanal (CH₃)₂CHCHO; 3-methylbutan-2-one CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂; 1-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one HOCH₂COCH₂CH(CH₃)₂.

  • Hydroxynitrile names: the nitrile carbon counts in the chain and the nitrile is the main group, so ethanal + HCN gives 2-hydroxypropanenitrile and propanal + HCN gives 2-hydroxybutanenitrile (WCH14 Jun 2022). Don't call them "cyanides".

Spec15.7Physical properties of aldehydes and ketones

Understand that aldehydes and ketones don't hydrogen bond with each other (which affects their physical properties) but can hydrogen bond with water (which affects their solubility).

Forces between carbonyl molecules

  • The C=O bond is polar (O is more electronegative): Cδ+=Oδ−. So carbonyl molecules have permanent dipole–dipole forces as well as London forces.
  • They have no H on an O (or N or F), so they cannot hydrogen bond to each other.
  • Result: boiling temperatures are higher than alkanes of similar Mr (extra dipole–dipole forces) but lower than alcohols (no hydrogen bonds).

Solubility in water

  • The lone pairs on the carbonyl O can accept a hydrogen bond from the δ+ H of a water molecule. So small aldehydes and ketones dissolve well (propanone mixes with water in all proportions).
  • As the carbon chain gets longer, solubility falls: the non-polar chain can't hydrogen bond with water, and London forces between the bigger molecules get stronger.
  • "pentan-2-one does not form hydrogen bonds (between molecules)"; "hydrogen bonds require more energy to break (so pentan-2-one has a lower boiling temperature)". ALLOW "(only) permanent dipole–dipole and London forces". IGNORE "pentan-2-ol has stronger London forces". DO NOT AWARD "more hydrogen bonds".
  • "propanal forms permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces" (not hydrogen bonds).

Often asked as: compare the boiling temperatures of a carbonyl and an alcohol of similar Mr (2 marks); 6-mark comparing an aldehyde, an alcohol and a carboxylic acid; MCQ on forces.

  • Saying an aldehyde or ketone hydrogen bonds with itself.
  • Naming the forces in each compound without comparing their strengths and linking to the energy needed.
  • Talking about hydrogen bonding with water when the question is about boiling temperature.
  • Saying covalent bonds break on boiling.
  • A hydrogen bond needs an H on N/O/F on one side only: an ester or ketone O can accept one from water even though the molecule has no O–H (WCH14 Jan 2022).
  • London forces are similar when molecules have a similar number of electrons, so you can compare the other forces directly (WCH14 Jan 2021).

Spec15.8Reactions of carbonyl compounds

Know the reactions of aldehydes and ketones with: (i) Fehling's/Benedict's, Tollens' and acidified dichromate(VI); (ii) LiAlH₄ in dry ether; (iii) HCN with KCN (mechanism, including optical activity); (iv) 2,4-DNPH, including identification from derivative melting temperatures; (v) iodine in alkali (iodoform test).

(i) Telling aldehydes from ketones: oxidation

  • Aldehydes have an H on the carbonyl carbon, so they are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids: RCHO + [O] → RCOOH.
  • Ketones have no such H and are not oxidised by these mild reagents (it would need a C–C bond to break).
  • The aldehyde reduces the reagent; the colour change shows it.

  • Mixing up Tollens' (silver mirror) and Fehling's/Benedict's (red precipitate).
  • Leaving out "acidified" before dichromate.
  • Adding water as a product in RCHO + [O] → RCOOH.
  • Writing just "positive" or "it reacts": give the observation.

(ii) Reduction with LiAlH₄

  • Reagent: lithium tetrahydridoaluminate(III), LiAlH₄, in dry ether (ethoxyethane), at room temperature. Then add water or dilute acid to finish. Water must be absent during the reduction because LiAlH₄ reacts violently with it.
  • Use [H] in equations:
    • aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH₃CH₂CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (propan-1-ol)
    • ketone → secondary alcohol: CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (propan-2-ol)
  • LiAlH₄ supplies hydride ions, H⁻, which act as nucleophiles: H⁻ attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon and the O⁻ formed is then protonated. It attacks C=O but not C=C, because C=C is non-polar (no δ+ carbon to attract H⁻).
  • "lithium tetrahydridoaluminate / lithium aluminium hydride / LiAlH₄"; "dry and ether / ethoxyethane". ALLOW "anhydrous ether". DO NOT AWARD heat or reflux. Name the product with its number (propan-1-ol, not "propanol").
  • Why C=O and not C=C: "the C=O bond is polarised by the (electronegative) oxygen"; "H⁻ is attracted to the δ+ carbon"; "the C=C bond is non-polar".

Often asked as: reagent and conditions (2 marks); name the products from an aldehyde and a ketone; balanced equation with [H]; MCQ picking the reducing agent.

  • "Dry ester" for dry ether, or adding heat/reflux.
  • Calling the reaction "nucleophilic addition" when asked for the type: the spec classes it as reduction.
  • Unbalanced equations: two [H] per C=O reduced.
  • The mechanism of hydride addition: dipole on C=O, lone pair on H⁻, arrow from H⁻ to the carbonyl C, arrow from the C=O bond to O; then the O⁻ is protonated by acid (WCH14 Jan 2023).

(iii) Nucleophilic addition of HCN

  • Reagents: HCN in the presence of KCN (or KCN with a little dilute acid), at room temperature. Both are highly toxic: use a fume cupboard.
  • Why KCN? HCN is a very weak acid, so it hardly dissociates and gives very little CN⁻. KCN supplies plenty of CN⁻, which is the real nucleophile. CN⁻ is regenerated in step 2, so it acts as a homogeneous catalyst.
  • Product: a 2-hydroxynitrile (hydroxynitrile). The carbon chain grows by one carbon, which is useful in synthesis.
    • ethanal + HCN → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH₃CH(OH)CN
    • propanone + HCN → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN

The mechanism, step by step

  1. The C=O bond is polar: show δ+ on C and δ− on O.
  2. Step 1: a lone pair on the carbon of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon (curly arrow from the lone pair to the C). At the same time the π bond of C=O breaks, with both electrons moving to the O (curly arrow from the C=O bond to the O).
  3. This gives a negatively charged intermediate (an alkoxide ion, with O⁻).
  4. Step 2: a lone pair on O⁻ attacks the H of an HCN molecule (or H⁺ / H₂O); the H–C bond in HCN breaks, re-forming CN⁻.
  5. Product: the 2-hydroxynitrile.
  • Why "nucleophilic addition": the attacking species is a nucleophile (CN⁻, an electron-pair donor attracted to the δ+ carbon), and two species add across the C=O to give one product, with nothing removed.
  • With an aldehyde (other than methanal) or an unsymmetrical ketone the product is a racemic mixture: see 15.5.
  • Mechanism marks: dipole on C=O; arrow from the C=O bond to O; lone pair on the C of CN⁻ and the arrow from it to the carbonyl carbon; intermediate with its negative charge; lone pair on O⁻ and arrow to the H of HCN (or H⁺); product.
  • Role of KCN: "catalyst"; "homogeneous" = same phase as the reactants.
  • Role of NaOH (if used instead of KCN): "reacts with HCN to produce CN⁻ / moves the dissociation equilibrium of HCN to the right / increases [CN⁻]". IGNORE "speeds up the reaction".
  • "Justify nucleophilic addition": "the (negative) cyanide ion attacks a δ+ centre / region of low electron density"; "nothing is lost / only one product".

Often asked as: the full mechanism (4 marks, very common); reagents for adding one carbon; role of KCN or NaOH; the rate equation; why the product is not optically active.

  • Arrows that start in empty space or end on a bond instead of an atom.
  • No lone pair on CN⁻, or the lone pair drawn on N with the bond made from C.
  • Missing negative charges on CN⁻ and on the O of the intermediate; a positive charge on the carbonyl C (it's only δ+).
  • Using the wrong carbonyl compound (read the stem carefully).
  • Explaining "nucleophilic addition" by just repeating the words "nucleophile" and "add".
  • Rate equation: rate = k[CH₃CHO][CN⁻]. HCN is not in it, because step 1 (CN⁻ attack) is the slow step (WCH14 Oct 2024).
  • A diketone adds HCN at both C=O groups: CH₃C(OH)(CN)CH₂CH₂C(OH)(CN)CH₃ from hexane-2,5-dione (WCH14 Jun 2023).

(iv) 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH, Brady's reagent)

  • Test for a carbonyl group (C=O in aldehydes and ketones): add 2,4-DNPH solution. An orange (or yellow/red) precipitate forms. Both aldehydes and ketones give it.
  • Carboxylic acids and esters also contain C=O but don't react.
  • The reaction is a condensation (addition, then loss of water). You don't need the equation.

Identifying the carbonyl compound from the derivative

  1. Filter off the orange precipitate (Buchner funnel, reduced pressure).
  2. Recrystallise it to purify:
    • dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent (so the solution is saturated and as much as possible crystallises on cooling);
    • cool (then in ice) so crystals of the derivative form while soluble impurities stay in solution;
    • filter and wash with a little ice-cold solvent (removes the impure solution without dissolving many crystals);
    • dry (between filter papers, in a low oven or a desiccator).
  3. Measure the melting temperature and compare it with a data table of known 2,4-DNPH derivatives. A pure compound melts sharply at a value matching the table.

Worked example: identifying a carbonyl from its derivative

An unknown C₄H₈O gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH and no change with Tollens' reagent. After recrystallisation the derivative melts at 115–116 °C.

Melting temperatures of 2,4-DNPH derivatives (example data): butanal 123 °C; butanone 116 °C; 2-methylpropanal 182 °C.

The negative Tollens' test shows a ketone, and the melting temperature matches butanone.

  • "2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine / 2,4-DNPH" (ALLOW Brady's reagent); "orange precipitate" (ALLOW yellow / red; DO NOT AWARD reddish-brown).
  • "the solids need to be recrystallised"; "their melting temperatures determined and compared with data / reference books". DO NOT AWARD boiling temperature. IGNORE the colour of the precipitate as a way of identifying.
  • Full outline: filter; dissolve in minimum hot solvent and cool; rinse with ice-cold solvent; dry; melting temperature compared with data.

Often asked as: test for a carbonyl; how to identify which carbonyl it is (2–3 marks, or a 6-mark on recrystallisation); link a positive 2,4-DNPH and a negative Tollens' to a ketone.

  • Boiling temperature instead of melting temperature: very common.
  • "Purify it" without saying recrystallise; "fractional distillation" for a solid.
  • Using the colour of the precipitate to tell carbonyls apart.
  • Thinking 2,4-DNPH reacts only with aldehydes.
  • Suggesting NMR or IR when the question asks for a school-lab chemical method.
  • The purification and identification routine: filter, recrystallise, measure the melting temperature, never the boiling temperature, then compare with data. This has been asked on four papers (WCH14 Oct 2024).

(v) The iodoform (triiodomethane) test

  • Reagents: iodine solution and sodium hydroxide solution (alkaline iodine), warm gently.
  • Positive result: a pale yellow precipitate of triiodomethane, CHI₃ (iodoform), which has an antiseptic smell.
  • Positive for: compounds with a CH₃CO– group (methyl ketones and ethanal, the only aldehyde).
  • Also positive for alcohols with a CH₃CH(OH)– group (ethanol and secondary alcohols such as propan-2-ol and butan-2-ol), because the alkaline iodine first oxidises them to CH₃CO– compounds.
  • Negative for: methanal and other aldehydes; ketones without a methyl next to the C=O (e.g. pentan-3-one); methanol; primary alcohols other than ethanol; tertiary alcohols.

Equation (propanone): CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CH₃COONa + CHI₃ + 3NaI + 3H₂O

  • The CH₃ group becomes CHI₃; the rest of the molecule becomes the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid with one fewer carbon.

  • Methanal is an aldehyde, so it gives a positive Fehling's/Benedict's test, but it has no CH₃CO group, so no iodoform. Ethanal is the only aldehyde that gives both (WCH14 Oct 2022).

Spec15.9Naming and drawing carboxylic acids

Name carboxylic acids and draw their structural, displayed and skeletal formulae.

Naming

  • The carboxyl group, –COOH, is always at the end of a chain. Its carbon is C1 and is counted in the stem.
  • Name ends -oic acid: methanoic acid HCOOH, ethanoic acid CH₃COOH, propanoic acid CH₃CH₂COOH, butanoic acid CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH. General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH.
  • Substituents are numbered from the COOH carbon: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid) CH₃CH(OH)COOH; 2-methylpropanoic acid (CH₃)₂CHCOOH; 3-hydroxy-2-methylbutanoic acid CH₃CH(OH)CH(CH₃)COOH.
  • Two COOH groups: -dioic acid, e.g. ethanedioic acid HOOC–COOH; benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH.
  • Unsaturated acids: the COOH carbon is still C1, so CH₂=CHCH₂COOH is but-3-enoic acid.

Formulae

  • Structural: CH₃CH₂COOH (or CH₃CH₂CO₂H).
  • Displayed: show C=O and the O–H bond separately.
  • Skeletal: see the figure in 15.6; the =O and OH are drawn on the last carbon.
  • "but-3-enoic acid"; "2-methylpropenoic acid"; IGNORE missing hyphens, spaces, commas, capitals; ACCEPT "3-butenoic acid".
  • "2-hydroxypropanoic acid"; ALLOW "propaneoic"; DO NOT AWARD "hydroxo".

Often asked as: name an acid from its structure; draw or name isomers of a C₄ acid.

  • Numbering the alkene as position 1: the COOH carbon must get the lowest number.
  • Forgetting that the COOH carbon is part of the stem (CH₃CH₂COOH is propanoic, not ethanoic, acid).
  • Drawing carbons with five bonds when listing isomers.
  • Isomers of unsaturated acids, including E/Z isomers and a ring isomer such as cyclopropanecarboxylic acid (WCH14 Jun 2024).

Spec15.10Hydrogen bonding in carboxylic acids

Understand how hydrogen bonding affects the boiling temperatures and solubility of carboxylic acids.

Boiling temperatures

  • The –COOH group has an O–H bond (δ+ H) and two O atoms with lone pairs, so carboxylic acid molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other, as well as London and dipole–dipole forces.
  • So their boiling temperatures are high: ethanoic acid (Mr 60) boils at 118 °C, well above propanal (49 °C) and propan-1-ol (97 °C) of similar Mr.
  • More energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds between the molecules.

Solubility

  • The –COOH group hydrogen bonds with water (both through its O–H and through the lone pairs on its O atoms), so the small acids (C₁–C₄) mix completely with water.
  • As the non-polar hydrocarbon chain gets longer, solubility falls: the chain can't hydrogen bond with water, and London forces between the large molecules are stronger.

Drawing a hydrogen bond to water

  • Show the δ+ H of one molecule lined up with a lone pair on an O of the other, the H···O bond straight (about 180°), labelled "hydrogen bond", with δ+ and δ− on the atoms involved and on the atoms bonded to them.
  • Hydrogen bond diagram (3 marks for five points): water drawn correctly and aligned at "160–200°"; "hydrogen bond" labelled; "lone pair on the relevant O lined up with the hydrogen bond"; δ+ and δ− on the two atoms in the hydrogen bond; and on the atoms bonded to them.
  • Solubility: "can form hydrogen bonds (with water)"; "the larger the (non-polar hydrocarbon) chain the lower the solubility" because "London forces between larger acid molecules are stronger (more electrons)".
  • Boiling: the acid "forms hydrogen bonds but the others do not / hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular force".

Often asked as: draw a hydrogen bond between an acid and water; explain the trend in solubility; rank the boiling temperatures of an acid, an alcohol and an aldehyde.

  • Hydrogen bond drawn to the H of water from the H of the acid, or at an angle; no lone pair shown.
  • Writing water as HO₂.
  • Explaining boiling temperature with hydrogen bonding to water.

You may meet the idea that carboxylic acids form dimers (pairs held by two hydrogen bonds), which helps explain why their boiling temperatures are even higher than alcohols of similar Mr. It hasn't been examined: the marks are for "hydrogen bonds between molecules need more energy to break".

Spec15.11Preparing carboxylic acids

Know that carboxylic acids can be made by oxidising primary alcohols or aldehydes, and by hydrolysing nitriles.

Oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes

  • Reagents and conditions: potassium dichromate(VI) and dilute sulfuric acid (acidified K₂Cr₂O₇), in excess, heat under reflux. Colour change orange → green.
  • Reflux lets the reaction run hot for a long time without losing volatile material, so the aldehyde formed first is oxidised all the way to the acid. (If you distil the mixture as it forms instead, you collect the aldehyde.)
  • Equations:
    • CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O (primary alcohol → acid)
    • CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH (aldehyde → acid)
  • Secondary alcohols give ketones (no further oxidation); tertiary alcohols are not oxidised.

Hydrolysis of nitriles

  • Acid hydrolysis: heat under reflux with dilute hydrochloric (or sulfuric) acid:
    CH₃CH₂CN + 2H₂O + HCl → CH₃CH₂COOH + NH₄Cl
    (ionic version: RCN + 2H₂O + H⁺ → RCOOH + NH₄⁺)
  • Alkaline hydrolysis: reflux with NaOH(aq) gives the salt and ammonia: CH₃CH₂CN + H₂O + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + NH₃. Then add a strong acid to release the free carboxylic acid.
  • Combined with 15.8(iii), this gives a two-step route from a carbonyl to a 2-hydroxy acid with one extra carbon:
    CH₃CHO → (HCN, KCN) → CH₃CH(OH)CN → (dilute HCl, reflux) → CH₃CH(OH)COOH (lactic acid)
  • "potassium dichromate((VI)) / K₂Cr₂O₇ and sulfuric acid / H₂SO₄; (heat under) reflux". ALLOW Cr₂O₇²⁻ and H⁺, sodium dichromate. DO NOT AWARD hydrochloric acid or another reagent.
  • Nitrile: "hydrolysis; (dilute) hydrochloric acid / HCl(aq); heat (under reflux)"; equation (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CN + 2H₂O + HCl → (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂COOH + NH₄Cl. Leaving out water or HCl, or giving HCN as a product, loses the mark.

Often asked as: reagents and conditions for oxidation; equation for nitrile hydrolysis; a two-step synthesis via a hydroxynitrile.

  • Calling the sulfuric acid a catalyst (it's a reagent here); using HCl or KMnO₄ with dichromate.
  • Distillation instead of reflux when the acid is wanted.
  • Nitrile hydrolysis products such as HCN, or no NH₄Cl.
  • For the second step of a synthesis, giving an oxidising agent instead of dilute acid.
  • Two-step synthesis: carbonyl → hydroxynitrile → hydroxy acid, with reagents for each step (WCH14 Jan 2024).

Spec15.12Reactions of carboxylic acids

Know the reactions of carboxylic acids with LiAlH₄ in dry ether, with bases to make salts, with PCl₅, and with alcohols (acid catalyst).

(i) Reduction with LiAlH₄

  • LiAlH₄ in dry ether, room temperature, then water/dilute acid. The acid is reduced to a primary alcohol:
    CH₃COOH + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O
  • Four [H] are needed (C=O and C–OH both changed), and water is formed.

(ii) With bases: salts

  • Carboxylic acids are weak acids, but strong enough to react with:
    • alkalis: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
    • carbonates and hydrogencarbonates, with effervescence of CO₂ (turns limewater milky):
      2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O
      CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O
    • reactive metals: 2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂
  • Fizzing with Na₂CO₃ or NaHCO₃ is a test for –COOH: alcohols and phenols don't do it.
  • The salts are ionic: write CH₃COO⁻Na⁺ or CH₃COONa, never a covalent O–Na bond. Name: sodium ethanoate.

(iii) With phosphorus(V) chloride, PCl₅

  • Add solid PCl₅ to the dry acid at room temperature: steamy (misty) fumes of HCl. The product is an acyl chloride:
    CH₃COOH + PCl₅ → CH₃COCl + POCl₃ + HCl
  • PCl₅ reacts with any –OH group, so alcohols give the same steamy fumes (ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl). It is a test for OH, not specifically for COOH.

(iv) With alcohols: esterification

  • Heat the carboxylic acid and alcohol under reflux with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid as a catalyst:
    CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (ethyl ethanoate)
  • It is a condensation (water lost) and is reversible, so the yield is limited. To push the equilibrium right: use an excess of one reactant, or remove the ester or water as it forms. Conc. H₂SO₄ also absorbs some water.
  • Esters have sweet, fruity smells: used in flavourings, perfumes and as solvents.

Making and purifying an ester (suggested practical)

  1. Mix ethanol and ethanoic acid in a round-bottomed flask, add a few drops of concentrated H₂SO₄ and anti-bumping granules.
  2. Heat under reflux for about 20 minutes.
  3. Distil off the crude ester (with some ethanol, acid and water).
  4. Shake the distillate with sodium carbonate solution in a separating funnel to remove acids (release the pressure: CO₂ forms). Run off and discard the lower aqueous layer.
  5. Shake with calcium chloride solution to remove ethanol; discard the aqueous layer.
  6. Dry the ester layer with anhydrous calcium chloride or anhydrous magnesium sulfate until clear.
  7. Redistil, collecting the fraction that boils near the ester's boiling temperature (ethyl ethanoate 77 °C).
  • LiAlH₄: "dry and ether"; product a primary alcohol.
  • Salt: CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂COONa + CO₂ + H₂O; ALLOW the ionic form and H₂CO₃ for CO₂ + H₂O. DO NOT AWARD a covalent O–Na bond.
  • PCl₅: "PCl₅ / phosphorus(V) chloride / phosphorus pentachloride"; "steamy / misty fumes"; DO NOT AWARD PCl₅(aq) or "fruity smell".
  • Esterification: "(concentrated) sulfuric acid"; its role is "catalyst" (DO NOT AWARD just "proton donor").

Often asked as: equations for each reaction; test results that identify a carboxylic acid (fizzing with carbonate); reagents to make a named ester; MCQ choosing the reducing agent; identify the acid and alcohol that make a given ester.

  • O–Na drawn as a covalent bond in a sodium carboxylate.
  • "Sodium" or "potassium" as the metal that gives fizzing in a test: use magnesium or a carbonate.
  • Forgetting that PCl₅ also reacts with an alcohol OH elsewhere in the molecule.
  • Unbalanced PCl₅ equations (a diacid needs 2PCl₅).
  • A hydroxy acid reacts with NaHCO₃ at the COOH only; the alcohol OH doesn't react: CH₃CH(OH)COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃CH(OH)COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂ (WCH14 Jun 2021).
  • With excess PCl₅, both the COOH and an alcohol OH in the same molecule are converted to –Cl (WCH14 Jun 2024). A dicarboxylic acid gives a diacyl chloride: HOOCCOOH + 2PCl₅ → ClOCCOCl + 2POCl₃ + 2HCl (WCH14 Jan 2025).

Spec15.14Reactions of acyl chlorides

Know the reactions of acyl chlorides with water, alcohols, concentrated ammonia and amines.

Why acyl chlorides are so reactive

  • The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two electronegative atoms (O and Cl), so it is strongly δ+ and attacked readily by nucleophiles with a lone pair (O in water and alcohols, N in ammonia and amines).
  • The overall change is nucleophilic addition–elimination: the nucleophile adds to the C=O, then HCl is eliminated (the Cl becomes Cl⁻). In each case the –Cl is replaced.
  • The reactions are fast and exothermic at room temperature, and give steamy fumes of HCl (with water and alcohols).

Spec15.15Hydrolysis of esters

Understand ester hydrolysis in acidic and in alkaline solution.

Acid hydrolysis

  • Heat under reflux with dilute sulfuric or hydrochloric acid (the acid is a catalyst).
  • It is the reverse of esterification and is reversible, so it doesn't go to completion:
    CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH
  • Products: the carboxylic acid and the alcohol.

Alkaline hydrolysis

  • Heat under reflux with sodium hydroxide solution:
    CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + CH₃CH₂OH
  • It goes to completion (one-way arrow). The carboxylic acid formed is immediately turned into the carboxylate ion, which can't react with the alcohol to re-form the ester.
  • Products: the sodium salt of the acid and the alcohol. To get the free acid, add excess strong acid: CH₃COONa + HCl → CH₃COOH + NaCl.

Splitting an ester

Break the bond between the C=O carbon and the single-bonded O. Add OH to the C=O side (acid) and H to the O side (alcohol). Methyl propanoate gives propanoic acid and methanol.

Worked example: purity of an ester by back titration

1.60 g of impure ethyl ethanoate (Mr 88.0) is refluxed with 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH (an excess). The leftover NaOH needs 16.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl.

NaOH added = 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.0250 mol; NaOH left = 0.500 × 0.0160 = 0.00800 mol

NaOH used by ester = 0.0170 mol = moles of ester (1 : 1)

mass of ester = 0.0170 × 88.0 = 1.50 g; purity = 1.50 ÷ 1.60 × 100 = 93.5%.

  • Triester (a fat or oil) with excess NaOH: "correct structure of propane-1,2,3-triol; correct structure of sodium ethanoate (or the salt); mole ratio" (3 NaOH, 3 salt). DO NOT AWARD a molecular formula.
  • To release the acid: "add (excess) HCl(aq)" or any named strong acid.

Often asked as: products of acid and alkaline hydrolysis (MCQ); the reagent to free the acid from its salt; back-titration to find the percentage of ester; products of hydrolysing a triester.

  • Using ⇌ for alkaline hydrolysis, or → for acid hydrolysis.
  • Giving the free acid (not the salt) as the product of alkaline hydrolysis.
  • Sodium ethanoate drawn with a covalent O–Na bond.
  • Answering "acid hydrolysis" when asked for a reagent: name the acid.
  • Back titration of the excess NaOH to find the percentage of ester (WCH14 Jan 2020).
  • Triesters (fats and oils) with excess alkali give propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) and three carboxylate salts (soaps) (WCH14 Oct 2022).

Spec15.16Polyesters

Understand how polyesters such as Terylene form by condensation polymerisation.

Condensation polymerisation

  • In condensation polymerisation, monomers join and a small molecule (usually water, or HCl from an acyl chloride) is lost at each link.
  • Each monomer needs two functional groups so the chain can keep growing at both ends.
  • A polyester forms from:
    • a dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl chloride) + a diol, or
    • a single monomer with –OH at one end and –COOH at the other (a hydroxy acid, e.g. lactic acid).
  • Each new link is an ester link, –COO–.

Terylene (PET)

  • Monomers: benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid, HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH, and ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂CH₂OH.
  • n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH + n HOCH₂CH₂OH → –[–OC–C₆H₄–COO–CH₂CH₂–O–]ₙ– + 2n H₂O
  • Repeat unit: –OC–C₆H₄–COO–CH₂–CH₂–O– (one acid residue + one diol residue).

Poly(lactic acid)

  • From 2-hydroxypropanoic acid, CH₃CH(OH)COOH: repeat unit –O–CH(CH₃)–CO–.
  • Polyesters can be broken down by hydrolysis of the ester links (acid or alkali), so some (e.g. poly(lactic acid)) are biodegradable, unlike addition polymers such as poly(ethene).

Drawing and reading polymers

  • Draw the repeat unit with continuation bonds at both ends (through the brackets) and no –OH or –H left on the ends.
  • To find the monomers from a polymer: break each ester link; put –OH back on the C=O and –H on the O.
  • "ester linkage"; "remainder of the structure including continuation bonds". ALLOW more than one repeat unit; IGNORE brackets and n.
  • Poly(lactic acid) repeat unit "–[OCH(CH₃)CO]–" or "–[CH(CH₃)COO]–"; reaction type "esterification" (ALLOW condensation).
  • Monomers "in either order"; OH groups written correctly on the monomers.

Often asked as: draw the repeat unit (or two repeat units) from given monomers; identify the monomers from a polymer section; MCQ on the type of polymerisation; hydrolysis of a polyester.

  • Ether, ketone or plain carbon links in place of the ester link.
  • Leaving –OH or –COOH groups in the repeat unit, or extra OH groups branching off the chain.
  • No continuation bonds; carbons with three or five bonds.
  • Using a dialdehyde instead of a diacid as a monomer.
  • Polyesters from a single hydroxy-acid monomer (WCH14 Jan 2024), and the cyclic lactide formed from two lactic acid molecules (WCH14 Jan 2023). Terylene itself hasn't been named in a paper: questions give you the monomers.

Spec15.17Mass spectra: accurate relative molecular mass

Use mass spectra to suggest structures from accurate relative molecular masses, and calculate accurate Mr from relative atomic masses given to four decimal places.

High-resolution mass spectrometry

  • The molecular ion peak, M⁺, is the peak with the highest m/z (ignoring small isotope peaks). Its m/z = Mr.
  • A high-resolution spectrometer measures m/z to 4 decimal places. Because accurate isotope masses aren't whole numbers (¹H = 1.0078, ¹⁶O = 15.9949, ¹⁴N = 14.0031, ¹²C = 12.0000 exactly), molecules with the same nominal Mr have different accurate Mr.
  • So the accurate Mr fixes the molecular formula. It can't tell isomers apart: they have identical formulae.

Worked example: which formula?

A compound has M⁺ at m/z = 88.0524. Which is it: C₄H₈O₂, C₅H₁₂O or C₃H₈N₂O?

C₄H₈O₂ = 4(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) + 2(15.9949) = 88.0522

C₅H₁₂O = 60.0000 + 12.0936 + 15.9949 = 88.0885

C₃H₈N₂O = 36.0000 + 8.0624 + 28.0062 + 15.9949 = 88.0635

The closest match is C₄H₈O₂ (e.g. ethyl ethanoate, methyl propanoate or butanoic acid). Other evidence (IR, NMR) is needed to choose between isomers.

Fragments tell isomers apart

  • In the spectrometer, molecular ions break into fragments; only the positive fragments are detected.
  • Propanal and propanone both have M⁺ = 58, but propanal gives a peak at m/z 29 (C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺), while propanone gives a big peak at m/z 43 (CH₃CO⁺) and none at 29.

Both are C5H10O, so both give the molecular ion at m/z 86.

Pentan-3-one, CH3CH2COCH2CH3, breaks next to the C=O to give [CH3CH2CO]+ at m/z 57 (and C2H5+ at 29): [CH3CH2COCH2CH3]+• → CH3CH2CO+ + •CH2CH3.

Pentan-2-one, CH3COCH2CH2CH3, gives a strong peak at m/z 43 from CH3CO+ and one at 71 from CH3CH2CH2CO+, but no peak at 57.

The same molecular ion but different fragments: fragmentation patterns tell isomers apart.

  • Each fragment needs its m/z, its formula and a positive charge: "a fragment m/z = 136 (with a positive charge)". DO NOT AWARD a molecular formula or a negative charge.
  • Fragmentation happens because "(high energy) electrons are fired at the molecules", "breaking bonds".
  • "propanal and propanone have the same molecular ion peak m/z = 58"; "propanal has a peak at m/z 29 (C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺) but propanone does not".

Often asked as: MCQ choosing the compound from an accurate Mr; identify a fragment; use M⁺ in a combined-spectra problem.

  • Saying two isomers have "the same mass spectrum": the M⁺ is the same, but the fragments differ.
  • Writing fragments with no + charge.
  • Rounding accurate masses before comparing.
  • Distinguishing propanal from propanone using the fragment peaks at m/z 29 and 43 (WCH14 Jun 2025).

Spec15.18Carbon-13 NMR: what it shows

Understand that ¹³C NMR gives information about the positions of ¹³C atoms in a molecule.

How it works (in outline)

  • About 1% of carbon atoms are ¹³C. Their nuclei behave like tiny magnets: in a strong magnetic field they absorb radio waves of a particular frequency (¹²C nuclei don't).
  • The exact frequency depends on the electron environment of each carbon. Electronegative atoms (O, N, Cl) nearby pull electrons away, so the carbon absorbs at a higher chemical shift, δ.
  • δ is measured in ppm relative to the reference TMS, tetramethylsilane Si(CH₃)₄, set at δ = 0. TMS is used because it's inert, volatile (easily removed) and gives a single peak away from most others.
  • Each different carbon environment gives one peak. Carbons in identical environments (related by symmetry) give the same peak.
  • Samples are dissolved in a solvent with no interfering signals, such as CDCl₃.
  • Answers must refer to carbon environments, not to hydrogen atoms.

Often asked as: as part of 15.19 questions; the idea isn't usually asked on its own.

  • Confusing ¹³C and ¹H NMR, e.g. discussing splitting or peak areas for a ¹³C spectrum.
  • Mixing up IR ranges and NMR shifts.

A ketone C₅H₁₀O has three peaks in its ¹³C spectrum, one at about 210 ppm. Which isomer is it?

Pentan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃, has 5 environments. 3-Methylbutan-2-one, CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂, has 4. Pentan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃, is symmetrical: CH₃ (×2), CH₂ (×2) and C=O, so 3 peaks. The 210 ppm peak is the ketone C=O.

  • "three peaks in its carbon-13 NMR spectrum; the other three have four peaks; labelling of the two equivalent carbon atoms". ACCEPT "three carbon environments".
  • Shift answers need both the range and the environment: "190–225 ppm for C=O, 0–60 for C–C" (a value within the range is fine).

Often asked as: number of peaks for given structures; which isomer fits a given number of peaks; draw an isomer with a stated number of environments; assign a peak to a carbon.

  • Drawing a structure with more environments than the question allows (only about one in five got this right in one paper).
  • Quoting a shift outside the allowed range, or with no environment.
  • Not saying which carbon gives which peak.
  • Choosing between isomers from the number of environments alone, using symmetry (WCH14 Jan 2025).

Worked example: ethyl ethanoate or methyl propanoate?

Worked example: deducing a structure

C₃H₇Br gives two peaks: a doublet at 1.7 ppm and a heptet at 4.2 ppm, area ratio 6 : 1.

Two environments, ratio 6 : 1 → two equivalent CH₃ groups and one CH. The heptet means the CH has 6 neighbouring H; the doublet means each CH₃ has 1 neighbour. The CH at 4.2 ppm is next to Br.

Structure: 2-bromopropane, CH₃CHBrCH₃. (1-Bromopropane would give three peaks, 3 : 2 : 2: a triplet, a sextet and a triplet.)

Sketching a low-resolution spectrum

One line per environment, heights in the area ratio, at sensible shifts, no splitting.

A compound contains 54.5% carbon, 9.1% hydrogen and 36.4% oxygen by mass. Divide by Ar: C 54.5 ÷ 12 = 4.54, H 9.1 ÷ 1 = 9.1, O 36.4 ÷ 16 = 2.28. Divide by the smallest: C : H : O = 2 : 4 : 1, so the empirical formula is C2H4O (mass 44).

The mass spectrum has its molecular ion at m/z 88, so the molecular formula is C4H8O2.

The IR spectrum has a strong absorption at 1740 cm−1 (C=O) but no broad absorption at 2500 to 3300 cm−1, so there is no O–H: the compound is an ester, not a carboxylic acid.

The 1H NMR spectrum shows three peaks: δ 4.1, quartet, area 2; δ 2.0, singlet, area 3; δ 1.3, triplet, area 3.

The quartet and triplet together are an ethyl group, and the CH2 at δ 4.1 must be joined to oxygen (O–CH2CH3). The singlet of area 3 at δ 2.0 is a CH3 next to C=O with no neighbouring hydrogens (CH3CO–).

The compound is ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3. Check: its 13C NMR spectrum would show four peaks, including one near 171 ppm for the ester C=O.

Spec15.21Chromatography: mobile and stationary phases

Know that chromatography separates a mixture using a mobile phase and a stationary phase.

The principle

  • The stationary phase stays in place: the solid silica or alumina layer on a TLC plate, the paper (and water held in its fibres) in paper chromatography, the packed solid in HPLC, the liquid coating in GC.
  • The mobile phase moves through or over it, carrying the mixture: the solvent in paper chromatography, TLC and HPLC; an inert gas in GC.
  • Each component is shared (distributed) between the two phases:
    • stronger attraction to the stationary phase (adsorption) → spends longer stuck → moves less (lower Rf, longer retention time);
    • more soluble in the mobile phase → moves further (higher Rf, shorter retention time).
  • Different components have different balances of these, so they separate.

Polarity

  • Silica (TLC stationary phase) is very polar (Si–OH groups). Polar molecules, especially ones that can hydrogen bond, are held strongly and move slowly.
  • A non-polar solvent (e.g. hexane) moves non-polar compounds well, but leaves polar ones near the baseline; a more polar solvent moves polar compounds further.
  • A compound moves a short distance because "only London forces form between it and hexane / it is polar and hexane is non-polar" and "weaker interaction between hexane and the compound / it is less soluble in hexane". DO NOT AWARD a mention of hydrogen bonds with hexane.
  • Stationary phase with amine groups: the molecule with more OH groups is held more strongly because it "can form more / stronger hydrogen bonds (with the amine groups)".

Often asked as: MCQ on why one spot moves furthest; explain the position of a spot in terms of the two phases.

  • Discussing the solubility of the wrong pair (e.g. the solvent in the solvent) instead of the compound in the mobile phase.
  • Just writing the Rf formula when the question asks you to explain a position.

Spec15.22Rf values in paper and thin-layer chromatography

Calculate Rf values from one-way paper and TLC chromatograms and understand why Rf values differ.

Running a chromatogram

  1. Draw a baseline in pencil (ink would dissolve and run) about 1–2 cm from the bottom.
  2. Put small, concentrated spots of the mixture and of pure reference compounds on the line.
  3. Stand the plate/paper in a beaker with solvent below the baseline (so the spots don't dissolve into the solvent pool). Cover the beaker so the air is saturated with solvent vapour and the solvent doesn't evaporate off the plate.
  4. Remove the plate when the solvent is near the top and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away (before it evaporates).
  5. Dry the plate and locate colourless spots: UV light (TLC plates often have a fluorescent coating), iodine vapour, or ninhydrin spray for amino acids.

Calculating Rf

Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front

  • Measure both distances from the baseline, to the centre of the spot. Rf has no units and is always between 0 and 1.

Worked example: Rf

The solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. Spot B has moved 5.6 cm.

Rf = 5.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.70.

Spot A (2.4 cm) has Rf = 0.30: it is held more strongly by the stationary phase (or is less soluble in the solvent) than B.

Why Rf values differ, and how to use them

  • Rf depends on the compound's relative attraction to the stationary and mobile phases (polarity, hydrogen bonding, solubility).
  • It also depends on conditions: the solvent, the type of plate or paper, and the temperature. So compare an unknown with reference compounds run on the same plate at the same time, rather than with a table.
  • Two different compounds can have the same Rf in one solvent; running again in a different solvent helps.
  • "Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front" (distractors: dividing by the whole paper length; the reciprocal).
  • Drawing a TLC plate for a given Rf: "origin / start line above the bottom of the paper"; "straight line for the solvent front, labelled"; spot at the right fraction of the distance. DO NOT AWARD a wavy line or the top of the paper as the front.

Often asked as: calculate Rf from a diagram or measurements (MCQ or 1–2 marks); draw a plate for a given Rf; why the baseline is in pencil; why the solvent must be below the baseline.

  • Measuring to the top of the paper instead of the solvent front, or from the bottom edge instead of the baseline.
  • Freehand or wavy lines, no solvent front drawn, a spot impossibly close to the front.
  • Not labelling which spot is which compound.

Spec15.23HPLC and GC

Know that HPLC and GC are column chromatography methods that separate substances by their different retention times, and that they can be linked to mass spectrometry (e.g. in forensics and drug testing in sport).

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

  • Stationary phase: very small solid particles packed into a narrow steel column (small particles = large surface area = better separation).
  • Mobile phase: a liquid solvent pumped through at high pressure.
  • A detector (often UV absorbance) records each component as it leaves the column. The chromatogram plots absorbance (y) against time (x).

Gas chromatography (GC)

  • Stationary phase: a liquid with a high boiling temperature coated on a solid support, inside a long coiled column in an oven.
  • Mobile phase: an inert carrier gas (helium, nitrogen or argon), which must not react with the sample.
  • The sample is injected and vaporised. Components that are more volatile, or less attracted to the stationary liquid, come out first.

Reading a chromatogram

  • Retention time: the time from injection to the component reaching the detector. Identify a component by comparing its retention time with a pure reference sample run under the same conditions.
  • Peak area is proportional to the amount of that component.
  • Retention time gets longer with a stronger attraction to the stationary phase, a slower flow of mobile phase, a longer column or (in GC) a lower temperature.

Linking to mass spectrometry

  • Different compounds can have the same retention time, and some unknowns have no reference sample. So the column output is fed into a mass spectrometer (GC–MS, HPLC–MS).
  • The chromatography separates the components; the mass spectrum (M⁺ and fragmentation pattern) identifies each one, by comparison with a computer database.
  • Uses: forensics (drugs, explosives, fire accelerants), drug testing in sport (urine and blood samples), food and environmental testing.
  • "(use the pure compound to) find the retention time". IGNORE retention factor/Rf here.
  • Name the technique for volatile liquids: "gas chromatography / GC / GLC". DO NOT AWARD several techniques.
  • HPLC chromatogram axes: time on the x-axis, absorbance on the y-axis.
  • Carrier gas: "inert".

Often asked as: what retention time means and how to use it; which technique suits a sample; how to reduce the retention time (MCQ: increase the carrier gas flow rate); why MS is added; scaling a sample mass for analysis.

  • Using Rf (paper/TLC) when the question is about HPLC or GC: they use retention time (only about one in five scored on this).
  • Thinking GC or HPLC alone identifies an unknown with certainty.
  • Peak area ∝ amount of each component, used to say which is most abundant (WCH14 Jun 2023).
  • Preparing a standard solution for HPLC and scaling up the mass of a natural product (e.g. spinach for oxalic acid) needed for a given concentration (WCH14 Jan 2025).

Quick checks

Which of these molecules has a chiral centre?

In butan-2-ol, carbon 2 carries H, OH, CH3 and C2H5: four different groups. In propan-2-ol the two CH3 groups are the same.

A single enantiomer of 2-bromobutane reacts with OH− by an SN2 mechanism. What is the product?

SN2 attack comes from the side opposite the bromine in one step, inverting the centre. There is no carbocation; a racemate would point to SN1.

Which reagent gives a positive result with propanal but not with propanone?

Only the aldehyde is oxidised, reducing Ag+ to a silver mirror. 2,4-DNPH reacts with both; the iodoform test is positive for propanone (a methyl ketone) but not propanal.

Why is the product of HCN addition to ethanal optically inactive?

2-hydroxypropanenitrile does have a chiral centre, but both enantiomers form in equal amounts, so their rotations cancel.

Which reaction is the best way to make ethyl ethanoate quickly and completely?

Acyl chlorides react with alcohols at room temperature, quickly and irreversibly. Acid-catalysed esterification with the acid is slow and reaches equilibrium.

How many peaks are there in the 1H NMR spectrum of methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3, and what is the splitting of the CH3 joined to oxygen?

There are three environments: CH3 of the ethyl group (triplet), CH2 (quartet) and O–CH3. The O–CH3 is a singlet because its neighbour across the oxygen is the C=O carbon, which carries no hydrogen.

Which is NOT a reason for using TMS as the reference in NMR?

TMS is added in small amounts as a reference and gives a peak at δ 0. Dissolving the sample without adding peaks is the job of a solvent such as CDCl3.

On a TLC plate the solvent front moved 8.0 cm and a spot moved 2.6 cm. What is its Rf value?

Rf = 2.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.325 ≈ 0.33. Rf is always less than 1; 3.1 is the ratio upside down.
