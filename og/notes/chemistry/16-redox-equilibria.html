Redox equilibria

Every battery in your phone or car is a pair of redox half-reactions kept apart so that the electrons must travel through a wire. This topic shows how standard electrode potentials measure the pull of each half-reaction for electrons, how they predict which reactions can happen, and how redox titrations put oxidation and reduction to work in quantitative analysis.

Spec16.1Oxidation and reduction: electrons and oxidation numbers

Define oxidation and reduction as electron transfer and as changes in oxidation number, and apply this to s-, p- and d-block elements.

The two definitions you use at A level

In terms of electronsIn terms of oxidation number
Oxidationloss of electronsoxidation number increases
Reductiongain of electronsoxidation number decreases
  • Memory aid: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).
  • An oxidising agent takes electrons, so it is itself reduced. A reducing agent gives electrons, so it is itself oxidised.
  • Oxygen and hydrogen definitions (gain of O, loss of H) still work for simple cases, but only the electron and oxidation-number definitions work for everything.

Across the blocks

BlockTypical behaviourExample
s-block metalsoxidised to 1+ or 2+ ionsNa → Na⁺ + e⁻; Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻
p-block metalsoxidised to positive ionsAl → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻; Sn shows +2 and +4
p-block non-metalsusually reduced to negative ions (charge = group number − 8)Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻; O₂ + 4e⁻ → 2O²⁻
d-block metalsoxidised to ions in several oxidation statesFe²⁺ and Fe³⁺; Mn from +2 to +7

High d-block oxidation states rarely exist as bare ions. They are found in oxo-ions: V(+5) is VO₂⁺, V(+4) is VO²⁺, Mn(+7) is MnO₄⁻, Cr(+6) is Cr₂O₇²⁻.

Worked example: oxidation numbers in Unit 5 ions

MnO₄⁻: Mn + 4(−2) = −1 → Mn = +7

MnO₄²⁻: Mn + 4(−2) = −2 → Mn = +6

Cr₂O₇²⁻: 2Cr + 7(−2) = −2 → Cr = +6 (the same in CrO₄²⁻)

VO₂⁺: V + 2(−2) = +1 → V = +5; VO²⁺: V + (−2) = +2 → V = +4

S₂O₃²⁻ (thiosulfate): 2S + 3(−2) = −2 → S = +2

S₄O₆²⁻ (tetrathionate): 4S + 6(−2) = −2 → S = +2.5 (an average)

C₂O₄²⁻ (ethanedioate): 2C + 4(−2) = −2 → C = +3

[Rh(CO)₂I₂]⁻: CO is neutral, each I is −1 → Rh − 2 = −1 → Rh = +1

Balancing a half-equation in acid

Use this every time; it works for any oxo-ion.

  1. Balance the element that changes oxidation number.
  2. Balance O by adding H₂O.
  3. Balance H by adding H⁺.
  4. Balance charge by adding electrons (e⁻) to the more positive side.

Worked example: MnO₄⁻ to Mn²⁺

Mn already balanced: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺

Add 4H₂O for the oxygens: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O

Add 8H⁺ for the hydrogens: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O

Charges: left +7, right +2. Add 5e⁻ to the left:

MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)

Check: Mn goes +7 → +2, a gain of 5 electrons. ✓

To build the overall equation, multiply each half-equation so the electrons are equal, add them, then cancel electrons and any H⁺ or H₂O that appear on both sides.

  • Name the element and give both oxidation numbers: "Mn reduced from (+)4 to (+)2"; "Cl oxidised from −1 to 0 (in Cl₂)" (WCH15 Jan 2023).
  • DO NOT ACCEPT "HNO₃ is reduced": say "nitrogen is reduced from +5 to +2" (WCH15 Jun 2023).
  • Not redox: "the oxidation number of vanadium is +5 in both species". IGNORE "there are no electrons in the equation" (WCH15 Oct 2020).

Often asked as: which element is oxidised/reduced and how you know; is this reaction redox?; oxidation number of a metal in an unfamiliar complex.

Naming the compound or ion ("nitric acid is reduced") instead of the element.

Saying H or O is oxidised or reduced when it is really the metal that changes.

Calling a reaction redox just because the formulae change: Cr₂O₇²⁻ → CrO₄²⁻ is not redox (Cr is +6 in both).

Using the change in oxidation number from titration data to find an unknown final oxidation state (WCH15 Oct 2021).

Oxidation numbers in organometallic catalysts, e.g. Rh is +1 in [Rh(CO)₂I₂]⁻ (WCH15 Jan 2026).

Spec16.2Standard electrode potential, E°

Know what a standard electrode potential is.

What it measures

  • A half-cell is an element in contact with its ions (or two ions of one element), set up so an equilibrium like Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s) is established at the electrode surface.
  • You can't measure the potential of one half-cell on its own. You can only measure the potential difference between two half-cells.
  • So every half-cell is compared with the same reference: the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), defined as 0.00 V.

The standard electrode potential, E°, of a half-cell is the potential difference (emf) of a cell made from that half-cell and a standard hydrogen electrode, measured under standard conditions (298 K, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ ions, 100 kPa gases).

What the sign tells you

  • Half-equations are always written as reductions (electrons on the left).
  • More positive E°: the left-hand species is more easily reduced than H⁺. It is a better oxidising agent. Example: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻, E° = +1.36 V.
  • More negative E°: the right-hand species is more easily oxidised. It is a better reducing agent. Example: Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn, E° = −0.76 V, so zinc readily gives electrons.

E° is never asked as a bare definition in WCH15 papers so far. It is tested through the conditions and the SHE inside a "draw the cell" or "describe how to measure" question. Learn the definition anyway: it is the backbone of those answers.

Writing a half-equation as an oxidation when quoting E°. Data-book half-equations are always reductions.

Spec16.3Standard conditions for E°

Know the standard conditions for measuring E°: 298 K, 100 kPa for gases, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ for ions.

Pressure of any gas100 kPachanges the position of an equilibrium involving a gas
Concentration of each ion1.00 mol dm⁻³changes the position of the half-cell equilibrium

  • If H⁺ appears in the half-equation (MnO₄⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻), [H⁺] must also be 1.00 mol dm⁻³.

Often asked as: the conditions labelled on a cell diagram; MCQ on the pressure in Pa (100 000 Pa).

Leaving out the temperature because it is not "attached" to a piece of apparatus (WCH15 Jun 2023 examiner report). Forgetting the H₂ pressure or the H⁺ concentration (WCH15 Oct 2023 examiner report).

Dilution on mixing. If you mix equal volumes of two solutions to make the half-cell electrolyte, each concentration halves. Fe₂(SO₄)₃ supplies two Fe³⁺ per formula unit, so 0.5 mol dm⁻³ Fe₂(SO₄)₃ gives 1.0 mol dm⁻³ Fe³⁺ (WCH15 Jun 2021).

Spec16.4The standard hydrogen electrode and why a reference is needed

Know the features of the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) and why a reference electrode is needed.

Why you need a reference

  • A voltmeter needs two connections. Putting a second wire into the same solution would create a second electrode with its own potential, so a single electrode potential can never be measured.
  • The fix: agree on one half-cell as the zero and measure everything against it. That reference is the SHE, given a potential of exactly 0.00 V.

Features of the SHE

  • Hydrogen gas at 100 kPa bubbled over the electrode.
  • A solution with [H⁺] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³ (e.g. 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl).
  • An inert platinum electrode coated with platinum black (finely divided Pt). The large surface area lets the equilibrium 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) be reached quickly, and platinum conducts electrons without reacting.
  • 298 K.

Why it's awkward in practice

The SHE needs a steady supply of hydrogen at exactly 100 kPa, the platinum is easily "poisoned" by impurities, and equilibrium is slow to set up. Labs often use a secondary standard (such as a calomel electrode) whose potential against the SHE is known.

  • "1 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq) and platinum (black) electrode"; "hydrogen gas … 100 kPa (at 298 K)"; "hydrogen gas must be able to enter the tube" (WCH15 Oct 2023).
  • Platinum black is there "to increase the rate of the equilibrium between hydrogen gas and hydrogen ions" (WCH15 Jan 2021).
  • Advantage of a calomel electrode (any one): "does not require a continuous supply of hydrogen"; "platinum is easily poisoned"; "difficult to ensure the hydrogen electrode is at equilibrium". IGNORE cheaper, safer, quicker, "hydrogen is explosive" (WCH15 Jun 2023).

Often asked as: draw and label the SHE as part of a cell; purpose of platinum black; advantage of another reference electrode.

Hydrogen drawn with no way into the tube, or flowing away from the platinum; one beaker with no liquid; a salt bridge that doesn't dip into both solutions (WCH15 Oct 2023 examiner report).

Using 1 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄: that gives 2 mol dm⁻³ H⁺. Use 1 mol dm⁻³ HCl. (0.5 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄ gives 1 mol dm⁻³ H⁺; one mark scheme refused it, two later ones accepted it, so HCl is the safe answer.)

Calomel electrode (Hg₂Cl₂ | Hg in saturated KCl), E = +0.24 V against the SHE. You are given its value and work out the other electrode from the measured emf. The KCl is saturated to keep [Cl⁻] constant (not "to act as a salt bridge") (WCH15 Jun 2023).

Understand the different set-ups for (i) a metal or non-metal in contact with its ions and (ii) two ions of the same element in different oxidation states.

TypeElectrodeSolutionExample

  • Solution: "Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions, 1 mol dm⁻³ of each", e.g. "1 mol dm⁻³ FeSO₄ and 0.5 mol dm⁻³ Fe₂(SO₄)₃" (WCH15 Jan 2025).

Giving "Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺" with no concentrations or no named compound. Explaining the salt bridge as "balancing charge" without saying ions move (WCH15 Jan 2025 examiner report).

Spec16.6Core Practical 12: investigating electrochemical cells

Set up electrochemical cells, measure their emf, and compare the results with values calculated from standard electrode potentials.

Core practical 12: method in brief

  1. Clean strips of metal (e.g. Cu, Zn, Fe, Ag) with emery paper to remove the oxide layer, then rinse.
  2. Put each strip in a small beaker of a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ solution of its own ions (e.g. CuSO₄, ZnSO₄, FeSO₄; AgNO₃ for silver).
  3. Make a salt bridge from a strip of filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃(aq), dipping into both beakers.
  4. Connect the two metals to a high-resistance voltmeter with leads and crocodile clips. Record the reading once it is steady.
  5. Repeat for other pairs, using a fresh salt bridge each time.
  6. For a two-ion half-cell, use a platinum (or carbon) electrode in a solution containing both ions, e.g. Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ at 1.0 mol dm⁻³ each.
  7. Compare each measured emf with E°cell calculated from the Data Booklet.

Making sense of the results

  • The metal connected to the negative terminal (when the reading is positive) is the half-cell with the more negative E°: it is where oxidation happens.
  • Measured values are usually a little lower than the Data Booklet values, because:

- conditions are not standard (room temperature isn't exactly 298 K; concentrations aren't exactly 1.00 mol dm⁻³); - oxide layers or impurities remain on the metal surface; - the voltmeter draws a small current.

  • School labs often use Cu²⁺/Cu (+0.34 V) as the reference instead of a SHE, then add 0.34 V to convert.

Safety

AgNO₃ stains skin and is an irritant; CuSO₄ is harmful and toxic to aquatic life; wear eye protection and gloves where needed. Dispose of metal-ion solutions in the residues container, not the sink.

For "describe how you would measure E° of Co²⁺|Co" (6-mark, WCH15 Oct 2024), the indicative points are:

  • SHE: H₂ gas, platinum electrode, "1 atm, 1.0 mol dm⁻³ HCl and 298 K";
  • salt bridge "filter paper soaked in (saturated) KNO₃";
  • "1.0 mol dm⁻³ of a named cobalt salt, e.g. Co(NO₃)₂", with a cobalt electrode;
  • "(high-resistance) voltmeter … to complete the circuit";
  • "voltmeter reading = standard electrode potential".

Often asked as: draw and label the apparatus (3–5 marks); 6-mark description of measuring E° for a metal or for Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺.

Writing "Co²⁺(aq), 1.0 mol dm⁻³" without naming a cobalt compound (WCH15 Oct 2024 examiner report).

Using platinum when the question gives you copper and zinc electrodes (WCH15 Jun 2022 examiner report). Drawing the salt bridge as an unlabelled line: label it and say what is in it.

Reading the sign of the voltmeter. The right-hand electrode is connected to the positive terminal, so a positive reading means the left electrode is the negative one (WCH15 Jun 2023).

Spec16.7Calculating E°cell

Calculate the standard emf of a cell, E°cell, from two standard electrode potentials.

E°cell = E°(right) − E°(left) = E°(reduction half-cell) − E°(oxidation half-cell)

For a working (feasible) cell, this is more positive minus less positive, so E°cell is positive.

  • The half-cell with the more positive E° is where reduction happens: it is the positive terminal.
  • The half-cell with the more negative E° is where oxidation happens: it is the negative terminal.
  • Never multiply an E° value when you multiply a half-equation to balance electrons. E° is a property of the half-cell, not of the amount.

Worked example: the Daniell cell

Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn, E° = −0.76 V; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu, E° = +0.34 V

Cu²⁺/Cu is more positive, so Cu²⁺ is reduced (right); Zn is oxidised (left).

E°cell = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = +1.10 V. Copper is the positive electrode.

Worked example: working back to an unknown E°

A cell is made from Zn²⁺|Zn (left, −0.76 V) and an unknown half-cell (right). E°cell = +1.56 V.

E°cell = E°right − E°left → 1.56 = E°right − (−0.76) → E°right = +0.80 V.

The Data Booklet shows this is Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag.

  • "E°cell = E°R − E°L (or most positive − least positive)". The sign must be shown: "+1.17 V"; "−1.17 (V) scores 1 mark" (WCH15 Jun 2025).
  • Choose half-equations only from the Data Booklet when told to: "No other metals will score" (WCH15 Jan 2025).

Often asked as: one-mark MCQ calculations; find an unknown E° from a measured E°cell; choose a metal that gives the largest E°cell.

Doubling E° when you double the half-equation. Dropping the + sign. Comparing two E° values in words when the question asks for E°cell (WCH15 Jun 2024 examiner report).

Using the wrong half-equation for a species that appears in two of them, e.g. thiosulfate/sulfur instead of thiosulfate/tetrathionate (WCH15 Oct 2022 examiner report).

Using a numbered table of unfamiliar half-equations, including alkaline ones, and picking the right pair (WCH15 Jun 2021; Jun 2024). Alkaline cells: Zn/MnO₂ (WCH15 Jan 2023).

Ni2+/Ni is −0.25 V and Ag+/Ag is +0.80 V. Silver is more positive, so Ag+ is reduced and nickel is oxidised.

E⦵cell = +0.80 − (−0.25) = +1.05 V. Cell diagram: Ni(s) | Ni2+(aq) || Ag+(aq) | Ag(s).

Combine the half-equations so the electrons cancel: Ag+ + e− → Ag is doubled, giving Ni + 2Ag+ → Ni2+ + 2Ag.

Never multiply E⦵ values when you double a half-equation: they measure a tendency per electron, not an amount.

Spec16.8Cell diagrams

Write cell diagrams using the conventional representation of half-cells.

The rules

  • A single vertical line ( | ) marks a phase boundary, e.g. between a solid metal and a solution.
  • A double vertical line ( || ), or a dashed one, is the salt bridge.
  • A comma separates two species in the same phase.
  • Oxidation half-cell on the left, reduction half-cell on the right (the two r's go together: Reduction on the Right).
  • Electrodes go on the outside edges. Pt is written in wherever neither species is a metal.
  • Write each side in the direction it reacts: on the left, reactant (the reduced form) then product; on the right, reactant (the oxidised form) then product.
  • Include state symbols and, where the half-equations have them, the molar ratios, H⁺ and H₂O.

Worked examples: cell diagrams

Zn/Cu: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s) E°cell = +1.10 V

SHE with copper: Pt(s) | H₂(g) | 2H⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s) E°cell = +0.34 V

Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ with chlorine: Pt(s) | Fe²⁺(aq), Fe³⁺(aq) || Cl₂(g) | 2Cl⁻(aq) | Pt(s) E°cell = 1.36 − 0.77 = +0.59 V

Chloride with dichromate: Pt(s) | 2Cl⁻(aq) | Cl₂(g) || [Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) + 14H⁺(aq)], [2Cr³⁺(aq) + 7H₂O(l)] | Pt(s)

  • Allowed: a comma or a vertical line between ions in one phase; a dashed line for the salt bridge.
  • Penalised (once each): missing molar ratios; a single solid line between the half-cells; electrons written in the diagram (WCH15 Oct 2023).
  • Part marks: "1 for the whole diagram reversed"; "1 if Pt missing on both sides but otherwise correct".

Often asked as: pick the correct diagram (MCQ); write the diagram for two given half-equations (2 marks).

Leaving out the molar ratios, the H⁺ and H₂O, or one of the platinum electrodes. Examiners say it is "very rare for both marks to be awarded" (WCH15 Oct 2023 examiner report).

Writing an overall equation or two half-equations when a cell diagram was asked for.

Spec16.9Why the conditions matter: non-standard electrode potentials

Understand why the conditions matter when measuring an electrode potential, E.

Concentration: think Le Chatelier

Each half-cell is an equilibrium written as a reduction: oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form.

  • Increase the oxidised form (e.g. more Cu²⁺), or decrease the reduced form → equilibrium shifts right, electrons are removed → E becomes more positive.
  • Decrease the oxidised form (e.g. less Zn²⁺) → equilibrium shifts left, electrons are released → E becomes more negative.
  • H⁺ in the half-equation (MnO₄⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻): more acid shifts the equilibrium right, so E is more positive and the ion is a stronger oxidising agent. That is why these oxidants are used in acid.
ChangeEffect on E of that half-cellEffect on E°cell (Zn/Cu cell)
[Cu²⁺] increased (right-hand cell)more positiveincreases
[Zn²⁺] decreased (left-hand cell)more negativeincreases
[Zn²⁺] increasedless negativedecreases

Temperature and pressure

  • Temperature changes the position of every equilibrium, so E changes with temperature too.
  • For a gas electrode, a higher gas pressure has the same effect as a higher concentration.
  • Lower [Zn²⁺] in a Zn/Cu cell: "Ecell would increase / become more positive"; "Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn would shift to the left, making the Zn half-cell more negative". Standalone marks (WCH15 Jan 2025).
  • Concentrated HCl with dichromate (E°cell = 1.33 − 1.36 = −0.03 V under standard conditions): "increases [H⁺] in the first equilibrium (shifting it right) so increases E"; "increases [Cl⁻] in the second equilibrium (shifting it left) so decreases E"; "the values are very close, so Ecell becomes positive". Every mark needs both the shift and its effect on E (WCH15 Oct 2023).
  • The practical reason concentrated HCl is not used to acidify dichromate: it would produce toxic chlorine (WCH15 Oct 2024).

Often asked as: effect of changing one concentration on E or Ecell; why a reaction with a slightly negative E°cell can still happen.

A bare "the conditions are non-standard" earns nothing. Say which equilibrium shifts, which way, and what that does to E (WCH15 Jan 2025 examiner report).

Saying a concentration above 1 mol dm⁻³ makes a reduction potential less positive. More of the oxidised form makes it more positive (WCH15 Jun 2022 examiner report).

The formula is always given. For oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form:

E = E° + (RT / nF) ln([oxidised] / [reduced])

R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = 298 K, F = 96 500 C mol⁻¹, n = electrons transferred. For a metal/metal-ion half-cell, [reduced] (the solid metal) is taken as 1, so E = E° + (RT/nF) ln[Mⁿ⁺].

Asked in WCH15 Oct 2020, Jun 2022 and Oct 2024.

Worked example: Nernst equation both ways

Find E for Zn²⁺|Zn with [Zn²⁺] = 0.010 mol dm⁻³ (E° = −0.76 V, n = 2).

RT/nF = (8.31 × 298) ÷ (2 × 96 500) = 0.01283 V

E = −0.76 + 0.01283 × ln 0.010 = −0.76 + 0.01283 × (−4.605) = −0.76 − 0.059 = −0.82 V (more negative, as Le Chatelier predicts).

Find the concentration when E = −0.79 V.

ln[Zn²⁺] = (−0.79 − (−0.76)) ÷ 0.01283 = −2.338

[Zn²⁺] = e−2.338 = 0.097 mol dm⁻³

Keep unrounded values in your calculator: one paper refused an answer of 9 × 10⁻⁴ when the working gave 8.99 × 10⁻⁴ (WCH15 Oct 2024).

Spec16.10Using E° to predict whether a reaction is feasible

Use standard electrode potentials to predict the thermodynamic feasibility of a reaction.

A reaction is thermodynamically feasible if E°cell is positive, where E°cell = E°(half-cell being reduced) − E°(half-cell being oxidised).

The anticlockwise rule

Write the two half-equations as reductions, the more negative one on top. The reaction that goes is anticlockwise: the lower (more positive) one goes forwards (reduction), the upper one goes backwards (oxidation).

A method that always works

  1. Find the two half-equations in the Data Booklet.
  2. Decide which species must be reduced for the reaction you're asked about.
  3. E°cell = E°(that reduction) − E°(the other half-equation).
  4. Positive → feasible. Negative → not feasible (the reverse reaction is feasible).
  5. Write the overall equation: reverse the oxidation half-equation, balance electrons, add, cancel.

Worked example: will iron(III) oxidise iodide? bromide?

Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, +0.77 V; I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻, +0.54 V; Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻, +1.09 V

Fe³⁺ with I⁻: Fe³⁺ reduced, I⁻ oxidised. E°cell = 0.77 − 0.54 = +0.23 V → feasible.

2Fe³⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + I₂(aq)

Fe³⁺ with Br⁻: E°cell = 0.77 − 1.09 = −0.32 V → not feasible. (Instead, Br₂ oxidises Fe²⁺: +0.32 V.)

Worked example: choosing an oxidising agent

Which halogen could oxidise Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺? It needs E° more positive than +0.77 V.

Cl₂ (+1.36 V) and Br₂ (+1.09 V) can; I₂ (+0.54 V) can't.

Cl₂(aq) + 2Fe²⁺(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Fe³⁺(aq), E°cell = 1.36 − 0.77 = +0.59 V

  • E°cell must carry a + sign or the word "positive" (WCH15 Oct 2025).
  • Equations and E°cell values score separately: four correct half-equations score even if not combined (WCH15 Oct 2022).
  • "VO²⁺ is not oxidised any further as E°cell is −0.2 V / negative" (WCH15 Oct 2020). Uncancelled electrons, H⁺ or H₂O are penalised once.

Often asked as: write the equation, calculate E°cell and say whether it is feasible (3–5 marks); 6-mark on which reducing agents can take vanadium to which oxidation state.

Calculating every E°cell but writing no equations, or the reverse. Give both (WCH15 Jan 2023 examiner report).

Not saying where a reduction stops, e.g. "so V³⁺ is not reduced to V²⁺ because E°cell is negative" (WCH15 Oct 2022 examiner report).

A reaction with a small negative E°cell, e.g. −0.01 V, may still happen to some extent if conditions change (WCH15 Oct 2022). The anticlockwise rule with an unfamiliar middle couple, e.g. Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77 V) between I₂/I⁻ and S₂O₈²⁻/SO₄²⁻ (WCH15 Jan 2026).

Fe3+/Fe2+ is +0.77 V and I2/I− is +0.54 V. For Fe3+ to be reduced and I− oxidised, E⦵cell = 0.77 − 0.54 = +0.23 V: feasible. 2Fe3+ + 2I− → 2Fe2+ + I2.

Br2/Br− is +1.09 V. For Fe3+ to oxidise Br−, E⦵cell = 0.77 − 1.09 = −0.32 V: not feasible. The reverse is: bromine oxidises Fe2+ to Fe3+.

ΔS°totalpositiveΔS°total ∝ E°cell
ln Kpositive (K > 1)ln K ∝ E°cell
  • The bigger E°cell, the bigger ΔS°total and the bigger K. A large positive E°cell means the equilibrium lies far to the right: the reaction goes almost to completion.
  • A small positive E°cell (a few tenths of a volt) still gives K > 1, but both reactants and products may be present at equilibrium.
  • A negative E°cell means ΔS°total is negative and K < 1: the reverse reaction is the feasible one.

Think of three gauges of one quantity. If one is positive, the others are too. MCQs often show a straight line through the origin for E°cell against ΔS°total or ln K: that is the "directly proportional" idea.

  • "E°cell is proportional to ΔS°total"; "so the total entropy change is positive as E°cell is positive"; "a positive ΔS°total means the reaction is (thermodynamically) feasible" (WCH15 Jan 2026).
  • ACCEPT ΔS°total = nFE°cell ÷ T; "(R) ln K is proportional to E°cell".

Often asked as: MCQ on the signs of ΔS°total and ln K for a positive E°cell; a 3-mark explanation linking E°cell to feasibility.

ΔS°total = nFE°cell ÷ T and ln K = nFE°cell ÷ RT (accepted in mark schemes, but numerical use has not been required).

For the Daniell cell (n = 2, E°cell = +1.10 V): ΔS°total = 2 × 96 500 × 1.10 ÷ 298 = +712 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; ln K = 85.7, so K ≈ 10³⁷: the reaction goes to completion.

Spec16.12Limitations of predictions from E°

Understand why predictions from standard electrode potentials can be wrong: kinetic stability and non-standard conditions.

E° tells you "can it?", never "how fast?"

  • A positive E°cell means the reaction is thermodynamically feasible. It says nothing about the rate.
  • If the activation energy is high, a feasible reaction may be too slow to see. The reactants are kinetically stable (thermodynamically unstable, but kinetically inert).
Feasible but slowE°cellWhy it's slow
S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂2.01 − 0.54 = +1.47 Vtwo negative ions repel, so high Ea (see 17.31)
2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂1.77 − 0.68 = +1.09 Vhigh Ea; fast only with a catalyst such as MnO₂
MnO₄⁻ + C₂O₄²⁻ at room temperaturepositiveanions repel; needs heating and Mn²⁺ (see 17.32)

Conditions are rarely standard

  • Real concentrations are not 1.00 mol dm⁻³, temperatures are not 298 K, and many reactions don't happen in aqueous solution at all. Under different conditions E changes (16.9).
  • If two E° values are close (within about 0.1 V), a modest change in concentration can reverse the prediction.
  • E° values describe aqueous ions. They can't predict reactions between solids or gases, or in concentrated solutions.
  • Feasible but doesn't happen: "the activation energy for the reaction is high" and "the reactants repel (because they are both negatively charged)" (WCH15 Jan 2026); "reaction rate must be slow / activation energy high" (WCH15 Oct 2022).
  • Prediction wrong the other way: "the conditions must be non-standard and the E° values are so close" (WCH15 Jun 2022). ACCEPT "high kinetic energy barrier".

Often asked as: explain why a reaction with a positive E°cell doesn't happen; explain why an unexpected reaction does happen.

"The reaction is feasible despite a negative E°cell because conditions are non-standard" with no further reasoning (WCH15 Oct 2023 examiner report). Name the equilibrium and the direction it shifts.

Writing only "no reaction" when asked why, with no reference to rate or activation energy (WCH15 Oct 2022 examiner report).

Spec16.14Disproportionation and E°

Use standard electrode potentials to predict whether a disproportionation reaction is thermodynamically feasible.

What disproportionation is

Disproportionation: one species is both oxidised and reduced in the same reaction, so one element ends up in a higher and a lower oxidation state.

  • Example: 2Cu⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). Copper goes +1 → +2 and +1 → 0.
  • The reverse, where two oxidation states combine to make one in between, is comproportionation.

How to test it with E°

The species appears in two half-equations: once on the left (being reduced) and once on the right (being oxidised).

  1. Find the half-equation where the species is reduced (it's on the left).
  2. Find the half-equation where the species is formed (it's on the right); this one runs backwards as the oxidation.
  3. E°cell = E°(reduction of the species) − E°(the one that forms it).
  4. Positive → the species disproportionates. Negative → it is stable to disproportionation.

Worked example: copper(I) disproportionates

Cu⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu, E° = +0.52 V (Cu⁺ reduced)

Cu²⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu⁺, E° = +0.15 V (run backwards: Cu⁺ oxidised)

E°cell = 0.52 − 0.15 = +0.37 V → feasible

2Cu⁺(aq) → Cu(s) + Cu²⁺(aq)

This is why simple Cu⁺ salts dissolved in water give a brown copper precipitate and a blue solution. Cu(I) is only stable in water when it is in a precipitate (CuI) or a complex.

Worked example: iron(II) does not disproportionate

Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe, E° = −0.44 V (Fe²⁺ reduced)

Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, E° = +0.77 V (run backwards: Fe²⁺ oxidised)

E°cell = −0.44 − 0.77 = −1.21 V → not feasible. 3Fe²⁺ → Fe + 2Fe³⁺ doesn't happen. The reverse (Fe + 2Fe³⁺ → 3Fe²⁺, comproportionation) does.

Worked example: manganate(VI) in acid

MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ MnO₂ + 2H₂O, E° = +2.26 V (MnO₄²⁻ reduced, Mn +6 → +4)

MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ ⇌ MnO₄²⁻, E° = +0.56 V (run backwards: MnO₄²⁻ oxidised, Mn +6 → +7)

E°cell = 2.26 − 0.56 = +1.70 V → feasible

Electrons: the reduction takes 2e⁻, the oxidation gives 1e⁻, so double the oxidation:

2MnO₄²⁻ → 2MnO₄⁻ + 2e⁻

MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → MnO₂ + 2H₂O

3MnO₄²⁻(aq) + 4H⁺(aq) → 2MnO₄⁻(aq) + MnO₂(s) + 2H₂O(l)

Green manganate(VI) turns purple, with a brown-black solid.

Hydrogen peroxide disproportionates too: O goes −1 → −2 (in H₂O) and −1 → 0 (in O₂). E°cell = 1.77 − 0.68 = +1.09 V, so 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is feasible. It is slow without a catalyst: a link to 16.12.

Latimer-style diagrams

Some questions give the E° values on a line of species from highest to lowest oxidation state, with the E° for each step written over the arrow:

Cu²⁺ —(+0.15 V)→ Cu⁺ —(+0.52 V)→ Cu

A species in the middle disproportionates if the E° on its right is more positive than the E° on its left (here 0.52 > 0.15, so Cu⁺ disproportionates). This is the same calculation as above in a compact form.

  • "Cu²⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu⁺ E° = +0.15 V and Cu⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu E° = +0.52 V"; "E°cell = 0.52 − 0.15 = (+)0.37 (V) and therefore the reaction is (thermodynamically) feasible". "No TE on incorrect half-equations" (WCH15 Jan 2021).
  • "Unbalanced equation with all species correct scores (1)" (WCH15 Jun 2021).
  • Comproportionation with E°cell = −0.03 V: "the E° values are close"; "the reaction may be shifted in the required direction using concentrated alkali" (WCH15 Jun 2021).

Often asked as: write the ionic equation and use E° values to show the disproportionation is feasible; derive the equation for an unfamiliar Mn species from given clues.

Choosing the wrong pair of half-equations: you need the two in which the same species appears, once on each side (WCH15 Jan 2021 examiner report).

Getting the three Mn species right but failing to balance. Write the two half-equations first, then combine (WCH15 Jan 2024 examiner report).

Not saying the E°cell is positive after calculating it.

Disproportionation in alkali using the alkaline half-equation: 3MnO₄²⁻ + 2H₂O → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (WCH15 Jun 2021).

Unfamiliar species from a Latimer-style table, e.g. H₃MnO₄ (Mn +5) → MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂, E°cell = +1.62 V (WCH15 Jan 2024).

Spotting a disproportionation in an MCQ: 2Ag²⁺ → Ag⁺ + Ag³⁺ (WCH15 Oct 2021).

Spec16.15Redox titration calculations

Carry out structured and unstructured redox titration calculations, including iron(II) with manganate(VII) and iodine with thiosulfate.

The two key reactions

TitrationIonic equationMole ratio
Manganate(VII) and iron(II)MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5
Manganate(VII) and ethanedioate2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 10CO₂MnO₄⁻ : C₂O₄²⁻ = 2 : 5
Dichromate(VI) and iron(II)Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O + 6Fe³⁺Cr₂O₇²⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 6
Iodine and thiosulfateI₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2

The route through any titration calculation

  1. Write (or find) the balanced equation and the mole ratio.
  2. Moles of the titrant = concentration × titre (in dm³).
  3. Use the ratio to get moles of the other reactant in the portion titrated.
  4. Scale up to the whole sample (e.g. × 10 for 25.0 cm³ taken from 250.0 cm³).
  5. Convert to what is asked: mass (× M), concentration (÷ volume), percentage.

Iodine–thiosulfate titrations are usually indirect: an oxidising agent reacts with excess iodide to release I₂, and you titrate the I₂. Chain the ratios: oxidant → I₂ → S₂O₃²⁻.

Worked example: purity of iron(II) sulfate (MnO₄⁻, structured)

5.00 g of impure FeSO₄·7H₂O (M = 277.9 g mol⁻¹) is dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 250.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean titre of 17.60 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄.

n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 17.60/1000 = 3.52 × 10⁻⁴ mol

n(Fe²⁺) in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 3.52 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.76 × 10⁻³ mol

n(Fe²⁺) in 250.0 cm³ = 1.76 × 10⁻² mol

mass FeSO₄·7H₂O = 1.76 × 10⁻² × 277.9 = 4.891 g

purity = 4.891 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 97.8%

Worked example: copper in brass (iodine–thiosulfate)

0.950 g of brass is dissolved in nitric acid, neutralised and made up to 250.0 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion is added to excess KI: 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I₂. The iodine needs 19.00 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ Na₂S₂O₃.

n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 0.0500 × 0.01900 = 9.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol

n(I₂) = 9.50 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 2 = 4.75 × 10⁻⁴ mol

n(Cu²⁺) = 2 × 4.75 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol in 25.0 cm³ (overall Cu²⁺ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 1)

in 250.0 cm³: 9.50 × 10⁻³ mol; mass = 9.50 × 10⁻³ × 63.5 = 0.603 g

% Cu = 0.603 ÷ 0.950 × 100 = 63.5%

Worked example: an unknown oxidation state (unstructured)

25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ V²⁺ needs 30.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. What is the final oxidation state of vanadium?

n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0500 × 0.0300 = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol; each takes 5e⁻, so electrons accepted = 7.50 × 10⁻³ mol

n(V²⁺) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol

electrons lost per V = 7.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2.50 × 10⁻³ = 3

V goes from +2 to +5 (as VO₂⁺).

Worked example: a back titration

0.200 g of an ore containing MnO₂ is warmed with 50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ Fe²⁺ in acid (excess): MnO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O. The leftover Fe²⁺ needs 20.00 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄.

n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 0.02000 = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol → leftover Fe²⁺ = 5 × 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol

Fe²⁺ added = 0.100 × 0.0500 = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol

Fe²⁺ that reacted with MnO₂ = 5.00 × 10⁻³ − 2.00 × 10⁻³ = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol

n(MnO₂) = 3.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol; mass = 1.50 × 10⁻³ × 86.9 = 0.130 g

% MnO₂ = 0.130 ÷ 0.200 × 100 = 65.2%

  • Marks are by process: "moles of MnO₄⁻ in the titre"; "use of 1:5 ratio"; "scaling to 250.0 cm³"; "conversion of moles to g"; "percentage … to 3 SF". "TE at each stage"; "ignore premature rounding if the final answer is correct".
  • "Correct answer with no working scores zero" on some papers: always show the route.
  • Use Ar from the periodic table in the Data Booklet (55.8 or 56 for Fe both accepted). Atomic numbers used as Ar score nothing (WCH15 Jun 2021).

Often asked as: % of iron in tablets, wire or moss killer (5–6 marks); Cu in a complex salt by iodine–thiosulfate; dissolved oxygen (Winkler); find an unknown oxidation state.

Using 25.0 cm³ where the titre belongs, or forgetting the × 10 scale-up (WCH15 Jan 2025 examiner report).

The wrong ratio, or none at all (WCH15 Jan 2024 examiner report). If you get a percentage over 100, recheck the ratio: don't fudge it (WCH15 Oct 2020 examiner report).

Rounding intermediate values, and quoting 4 or more SF (WCH15 Oct 2024 examiner report). Using the formula mass of VO₃⁻ when the question asks for mass of vanadium (WCH15 Oct 2022 examiner report).

Back titrations: excess reagent added, the leftover titrated, the amount used found by difference (WCH15 Jun 2023; Oct 2022). Many candidates lose marks by missing the back-titration step.

Winkler method for dissolved oxygen: moles of S₂O₃²⁻ ÷ 2 = moles of I₂; I₂ : MnO₂ = 1 : 1; MnO₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1 (WCH15 Oct 2025).

ppm and ppb. For dilute aqueous solutions 1 dm³ ≈ 1 kg, so 1 mg dm⁻³ ≈ 1 ppm and 1 μg dm⁻³ ≈ 1 ppb. Example: 2.15 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ Fe × 55.8 = 1.20 × 10⁻³ g dm⁻³ = 1.20 mg dm⁻³ = 1.20 ppm (WCH15 Jan 2025; Jun 2021).

A formula of a hydrate from titration data, e.g. KCr(SO₄)₂·12H₂O (WCH15 Jan 2025).

2.80 g of iron tablets is dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 250 cm3. 25.0 cm3 portions need 24.60 cm3 of 0.00500 mol dm−3 KMnO4. Find the percentage of iron by mass.

Moles of MnO4− = 0.02460 × 0.00500 = 1.23 × 10−4 mol.

The equation has 5 Fe2+ per MnO4−: moles of Fe2+ in 25.0 cm3 = 5 × 1.23 × 10−4 = 6.15 × 10−4 mol.

In the whole 250 cm3: 6.15 × 10−3 mol, so the mass of iron = 6.15 × 10−3 × 55.8 = 0.343 g.

Percentage of iron = 0.343 ÷ 2.80 × 100 = 12.3%.

1.50 g of brass is dissolved in nitric acid, neutralised and made up to 250 cm3. A 25.0 cm3 portion is added to excess KI, and the iodine released needs 15.70 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 sodium thiosulfate. Find the percentage of copper.

Moles of S2O32− = 0.01570 × 0.100 = 1.57 × 10−3 mol.

2Cu2+ make 1 I2, which reacts with 2 S2O32−, so Cu2+ : S2O32− = 1 : 1. Moles of Cu2+ in 25.0 cm3 = 1.57 × 10−3 mol.

In 250 cm3: 1.57 × 10−2 mol, so mass of copper = 1.57 × 10−2 × 63.5 = 0.997 g.

Percentage of copper = 0.997 ÷ 1.50 × 100 = 66.5%.

Spec16.16Uncertainty in titration results

Discuss the uncertainty of measurements and what it means for how valid the final result is.

Percentage uncertainty

% uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100

  • A bigger titre gives a smaller % uncertainty: ±0.10 on 5.00 cm³ is 2%, but on 25.00 cm³ it is only 0.4%. Choose concentrations so the titre is large (ideally 20–30 cm³).

Worked example: is 97.8% justified?

From the FeSO₄ titration in 16.15:

burette: 0.10 ÷ 17.60 × 100 = 0.57%

pipette: 0.06 ÷ 25.0 × 100 = 0.24%

flask: 0.3 ÷ 250.0 × 100 = 0.12%

balance: 2 × 0.005 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 0.20%

total ≈ 1.1% → 97.8% ± 1.1%, so the result lies between about 96.7% and 98.9%.

The third significant figure is uncertain: quote 98% (2 SF) or 97.8% (3 SF), never 97.82%.

  • "100 × 0.05 × 2 ÷ 27.35 = 0.37(%)" for the burette; "100 × 0.06 ÷ 25 = 0.24(%)" for the pipette (WCH15 Jun 2021).
  • "the total percentage uncertainty is much bigger than the rounding, so the answer should be to no more than 2 / 3 SF". IGNORE "data given to 3 SF".

Often asked as: calculate % uncertainties for several pieces of apparatus; decide how many SF are sensible; effect of an impurity or a damp sample on the result.

Too many significant figures: the examiners' rule is one fewer than the least precise datum, or the same number (WCH15 Jan 2021 examiner report).

Spec16.17Core Practicals 13a and 13b: redox titrations

Carry out redox titrations with (i) iron(II) and potassium manganate(VII) and (ii) sodium thiosulfate and iodine.

Core practical 13a: iron(II) with potassium manganate(VII)

  1. Weigh the iron(II) sample accurately (e.g. ground iron tablets or ammonium iron(II) sulfate) by difference.
  2. Dissolve in about 100 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid, transfer with washings to a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark with distilled water and invert to mix.
  3. Pipette 25.0 cm³ into a conical flask and add about 10 cm³ more dilute H₂SO₄ (excess acid).
  4. Fill the burette with KMnO₄(aq) (≈0.02 mol dm⁻³). Read the top of the meniscus, because the purple solution is too dark to see the bottom.
  5. Titrate until the first permanent pale pink colour. No indicator is needed: MnO₄⁻ is self-indicating.
  6. Do a rough titre, then accurate titres until two are concordant (within 0.10 cm³). Average the concordant titres.

Core practical 13b: iodine with sodium thiosulfate

  1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the oxidising agent (e.g. KIO₃ standard, bleach, or a Cu²⁺ solution) into a conical flask.
  2. Add excess potassium iodide (and acid where needed). Iodine is released: the solution turns brown.
  3. Titrate with Na₂S₂O₃(aq) from the burette until the brown fades to pale straw yellow.
  4. Add about 1 cm³ of starch solution: it turns blue-black.
  5. Continue dropwise until the blue-black colour just disappears (colourless; with copper, an off-white CuI precipitate remains).
  6. Repeat to concordant titres.

Why each step matters

StepReason
Acidify MnO₄⁻ with dilute H₂SO₄MnO₄⁻ needs H⁺ to be reduced fully to Mn²⁺. Too little acid gives a brown MnO₂ precipitate and the ratio changes.

  • Iodine–thiosulfate: "blue-black to colourless" (WCH15 Jan 2022).
  • Self-indicating: advantage "no indicator needed"; disadvantage "the intense colour of manganate(VII) makes reading the burette difficult" (WCH15 Jan 2026).
  • Dichromate needs an indicator because "the colour change (orange to green) is not distinctive / sharp enough". Cr₂O₇²⁻ orange, Cr³⁺ green; DO NOT ACCEPT blue (WCH15 Jun 2022).

Spec16.18Fuel cells

Understand that fuel cells use the energy released when a fuel reacts with oxygen to generate a voltage; methanol and other hydrogen-rich fuels can be used.

How a fuel cell works

  • The fuel (H₂, methanol, ethanol…) is oxidised at the anode (negative electrode). Electrons flow round the external circuit.
  • Oxygen is reduced at the cathode (positive electrode).
  • The overall reaction is the same as burning the fuel, but the energy comes out as electricity instead of heat, so no flame is needed.
  • Fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously, so the cell keeps working as long as they are fed in. It doesn't need recharging, and it doesn't store energy.
  • Electrodes are porous and coated with a platinum catalyst.

Methanol and other hydrogen-rich fuels

Hydrogen is hard to store (a gas with low energy per volume) and flammable. A liquid hydrogen-rich fuel such as methanol is easier to handle:

  • Anode (acid): CH₃OH + H₂O → CO₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻
  • Cathode: 1½O₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 3H₂O
  • Overall: CH₃OH + 1½O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Advantages and disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
H₂ cell: only water made; no CO₂ at the point of use (combustion engines give CO₂, CO, NOₓ)making H₂ needs energy, often from fossil fuels (electrolysis or from methane)
more efficient than burning the fuel in an engineH₂ is flammable; storing and transporting it is difficult (high pressure or low temperature)
quiet, few moving partsexpensive platinum catalyst; fuel cells have a limited lifetime
  • How it works: fuel cells "use the energy released on the reaction of a fuel / hydrogen with oxygen to generate a voltage" (WCH15 Jun 2024).
  • Advantage: "only water is produced, whereas CO₂ is produced in an internal combustion engine". Disadvantage: "production / transport / storage of hydrogen is difficult". Another fuel: "e.g. methanol (as it is hydrogen-rich)".
  • Methanol oxidation: "CH₃OH + H₂O → 6H⁺ + CO₂ + 6e⁻". DO NOT ACCEPT the formula CH₂OH (WCH15 Oct 2024).

Often asked as: 6-mark "explain how a hydrogen fuel cell works, with an advantage, a disadvantage and another fuel"; MCQs on electrode half-equations.

Forgetting to explain how the cell works (the first indicative point was the least seen, WCH15 Jun 2024 examiner report). Not answering the last part of the question ("name another fuel").

Treating "flammable" and "explosive" as the same thing. Writing methanol as CH₂OH.

  • Check which medium the question gives: use H⁺ in acid and OH⁻ in alkali, never a mixture.
  • Acid: anode "H₂(g) ⇌ 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻"; cathode "O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O(l)" (WCH15 Jun 2024).
  • Alkaline: anode "H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻"; cathode "O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻" (WCH15 Jan 2021; Jun 2025).
  • Overall "H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O", E° = +1.23 V (WCH15 Jan 2024).

Often asked as: MCQs picking the anode or cathode half-equation in acid or alkali; writing the reduction half-equation for an unfamiliar cell.

Mixing acid and alkaline half-equations, or balancing with OH⁻ when H⁺ and H₂O were specified (WCH15 Oct 2024 examiner report).

Mixing up anode and cathode. In any cell, the anode is where oxidation happens (both start with a vowel); in a fuel cell it is the negative terminal.

Other cells built from the same ideas: zinc–air (Zn + 2OH⁻ → Zn(OH)₂ + 2e⁻, WCH15 Jan 2022) and the alkaline Zn/MnO₂ cell (2MnO₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → Mn₂O₃ + 2OH⁻, WCH15 Jan 2023). A Nernst-type expression applied to a fuel cell (WCH15 Oct 2024).

Quick checks

What is E⦵cell for Mg(s) | Mg2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)?

E⦵cell = Eright − Eleft = +0.34 − (−2.37) = +2.71 V. −2.03 V comes from adding the two values instead of subtracting; −2.71 V from subtracting the wrong way round.

Why is the salt bridge usually soaked in potassium nitrate rather than potassium chloride?

The salt bridge carries ions, not electrons, and its ions must not react with either half-cell.

Which statement about the standard hydrogen electrode is correct?

Its potential is defined as 0 V, not measured. The platinum black is an inert, conducting surface that catalyses the H2/H+ equilibrium.

Using the table, which species could oxidise Br− to Br2 under standard conditions?

The oxidising agent must have a more positive E⦵ than Br2/Br− (+1.09 V). Cl2/Cl− is +1.36 V, giving E⦵cell = +0.27 V; the others give negative values.

E⦵cell for a reaction is +0.45 V, but no reaction is seen. What is the best explanation?

A positive E⦵cell means the reaction is feasible and K > 1, but it says nothing about the rate.

Why is hydrochloric acid not used to acidify potassium manganate(VII) in titrations?

E⦵(MnO4−/Mn2+) = +1.51 V is more positive than E⦵(Cl2/Cl−) = +1.36 V, so the titre would be too large and toxic chlorine would form.

0.100 g of potassium iodate(V), KIO3 (Mr 214), is added to excess acidified KI. What volume of 0.100 mol dm−3 thiosulfate reacts with the iodine formed?

Moles of IO3− = 0.100 ÷ 214 = 4.67 × 10−4. Each makes 3I2, which need 6 S2O32−: 2.80 × 10−3 mol, which is 28.0 cm3. 14.0 cm3 forgets that each I2 needs two thiosulfate ions.

In an iodine–thiosulfate titration, when should the starch be added?

Added early, the starch–iodine complex forms in large amounts and does not release its iodine readily, giving an inaccurate end point.
