Kinetics

At AS you explained qualitatively why concentration, temperature and catalysts change the rate of a reaction. Here the treatment becomes quantitative: rate equations say exactly how the rate depends on each concentration, the orders they contain reveal which species take part in the slowest step of the mechanism, and the Arrhenius equation puts a number on the activation energy.

Spec11.1Key terms in kinetics

Know the meaning of: rate of reaction, rate equation (rate = k[A]m[B]n, with m and n equal to 0, 1 or 2), order with respect to a substance, overall order, rate constant, half-life, rate-determining step, activation energy, and homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts.

The rate equation

\[ \text{rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^m[\mathrm{B}]^n \]
\([\mathrm{A}]\): concentration of A / mol dm⁻³\(m\), \(n\): orders with respect to A and B\(k\): rate constant

The nine definitions

TermWhat it means
Rate of reactionThe change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. On a concentration–time graph it is the gradient (of a tangent) at that moment.
Rate equationrate = k[A]m[B]n. It links the rate to the concentrations of the species that affect it. It is found by experiment, never from the balanced equation.
Order with respect to a substanceThe power that substance's concentration is raised to in the rate equation (m for A, n for B).
Overall orderThe sum of the individual orders, m + n.
Rate constant, kThe constant of proportionality in the rate equation. It is fixed at a given temperature: change the temperature (or add a catalyst) and k changes; change a concentration and it doesn't. A bigger k means a faster reaction.
Half-life, t½The time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half of its starting value.
Rate-determining step (RDS)The slowest step in a multi-step mechanism. It limits the rate of the whole reaction.
Activation energy, EaThe minimum energy that colliding particles must have for the collision to lead to a reaction.
Homogeneous catalystA catalyst in the same phase (state) as the reactants, e.g. H⁺(aq) in the iodination of propanone (11.6).
Heterogeneous catalystA catalyst in a different phase from the reactants, usually a solid with gaseous or liquid reactants, e.g. iron in the Haber process (11.11).

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by giving an alternative route with a lower activation energy, and it is regenerated at the end.

  • A zero-order species is not in the rate equation at all, but it still reacts. It just doesn't take part in (or before) the slow step.
  • The rate equation can include a catalyst (such as H⁺) that isn't in the stoichiometric equation. It never includes a species that only appears after the slow step.

Units of k

Rearrange the rate equation, then cancel units: k = rate ÷ ([A]m[B]n).

Overall orderUnits of k
0mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ (the same as the rate)
1s⁻¹
2dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
3dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹
4dm⁹ mol⁻³ s⁻¹

Pattern: each extra order adds dm³ mol⁻¹ to the units. Learn the method, not just the table.

Rearrange the rate equation to make k the subject:

\[ k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]^2} \]

Substitute the units of each quantity:

\[ k = \frac{\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}}{(\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}})(\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}})^2} = \frac{\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}}{\mathrm{mol^{3}\,dm^{-9}}} \]

Cancel one mol dm−3 from top and bottom, then move what is left on the bottom to the top by changing the signs of its powers:

\[ k = \frac{\mathrm{s^{-1}}}{\mathrm{mol^{2}\,dm^{-6}}} = \mathrm{dm^{6}\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}} \]

The order of the units in the final answer doesn't matter: mol−2 dm6 s−1 is equally correct.

Worked example: units of k

rate = k[NO]²[O₂], so k = rate ÷ ([NO]²[O₂]).

Units = mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ ÷ (mol dm⁻³)³ = mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ ÷ (mol³ dm⁻⁹)

= mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹, written dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹ (any order of the units is fine).

  • Rate equation: "rate = k[NO]²[O₂]". k must be there, and concentrations go in square brackets. Round brackets are not credited.
  • Units of k: "dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹" (third order); "dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹" (second); "s⁻¹" (first). Any order of the units is allowed.
  • Effect of temperature on k: "as T increases, k increases"; "a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ the activation energy". "More successful collisions" on its own is ignored.
  • Homogeneous catalyst: "same state/phase as the reactants"; catalyst: "speeds up the reaction and is regenerated" (WCH14 Jan 2022). If both words are in bold in the question, explain both.

Often asked as: MCQ on units of k or on the effect of changing a concentration; write a rate equation from given orders; calculate k with units; name a catalyst type.

  • Rate equations with a fourth overall order, e.g. rate = k[BrO₃⁻][Br⁻][H⁺]², so k has units dm⁹ mol⁻³ s⁻¹ (WCH14 Jun 2021, Jan 2022).
  • Zero order overall: rate = k, so k has the same units as the rate (WCH14 Oct 2021).
  • The rate equation can contain a species that isn't in the balanced equation as written, e.g. CN⁻ rather than HCN (WCH14 Oct 2024).

Mixing up the rate constant k with the equilibrium constant K. Le Chatelier and ΔStotal = R ln K tell you nothing about k. Explain changes in k with particle energies and Ea (examiner report, Jan 2020).

Leaving k out of the rate equation, or quoting orders from the balanced equation's coefficients.

Answering "explain why the rate increases with temperature" by writing out the Arrhenius equation. Describe what the particles do.

Spec11.2Half-life from a graph: constant half-life means first order

Find the half-life of a reaction from a suitable graph, and recognise that a constant half-life means the reaction is first order.

Reading half-lives

  1. On the concentration–time curve, pick a starting concentration (say 0.80 mol dm⁻³) and read the time.
  2. Find when the concentration has fallen to half that value (0.40) and read the time. The difference is one half-life.
  3. Repeat from another concentration. It doesn't have to be consecutive: 0.60 → 0.30 works just as well as 0.40 → 0.20.
  4. Draw the construction lines on the graph. They earn the method mark.

Half-life and order

OrderHalf-life as the reaction goes on
Firstconstant (it doesn't depend on concentration)
Zerogets shorter
Secondgets longer

Only first order has a constant half-life, so "the half-life is constant" is the evidence the mark scheme wants for first order. Radioactive decay is the classic first-order process.

Worked example: half-life to k

From the graph above, t½ = 120 s (three half-lives all 120 s, so first order).

For a first-order reaction, k = ln 2 ÷ t½ = 0.693 ÷ 120 = 5.78 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

Initial rate = k[H₂O₂] = 5.78 × 10⁻³ × 0.80 = 4.6 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

If t½ is read in minutes (say 132 min), convert first: 132 × 60 = 7920 s, so k = 0.693 ÷ 7920 = 8.75 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹.

A first-order reaction has a half-life of 25 s. The initial concentration of the reactant is 0.64 mol dm−3. What is its concentration after 100 s?

The half-life is constant, so count half-lives: 100 s ÷ 25 s = 4 half-lives.

Halve the concentration four times:

\[ 0.64 \rightarrow 0.32 \rightarrow 0.16 \rightarrow 0.080 \rightarrow 0.040\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \]

Equivalently, \(0.64 \times (\tfrac{1}{2})^4 = 0.64/16 = 0.040\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\).

  • "working for at least one half-life shown on graph". Lines on the graph are required.
  • "two half-lives … within the range", then "(first order as) the half-life is constant". Stating the value alone does not score the conclusion.
  • k = ln 2 ÷ t½ = 0.693 ÷ 120 = 5.78 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ (IGNORE SF except 1 SF).

Often asked as: show two half-lives on a printed graph and deduce the order; read a half-life (MCQ); turn a half-life into k.

  • k = ln 2 ÷ t½ for a first-order reaction (WCH14 Oct 2021 Q17, Oct 2022 Q16).
  • The half-life is the time to reach half the starting concentration, not half-way to the final concentration. MCQ distractors use the second (WCH14 Jan 2025 Q15).

Showing only one half-life, or not writing "constant". Assuming the second half-life must start where the first ended: any halving works (examiner report, Jun 2024).

Leaving k per minute when the question wants s⁻¹.

Spec11.3Choosing a technique to follow the rate

Pick, and justify, a suitable method to get rate data for a given reaction: titration, colorimetry, mass change, volume of gas, or another suitable technique.

The principle

Find a property that changes as the reaction goes and is proportional to the concentration of one reactant or product. Then measure it against time. The justification must name what changes in this reaction.

TechniqueUse it when…How it worksWatch out for
Titration (sample and quench)an acid, alkali or iodine is used up or maderemove samples at timed intervals, quench them (stop the reaction), then titratequenching is needed; the reaction itself mustn't make the species you titrate

Quenching

To stop a sample reacting before you titrate it:

  • dilute it with a large volume of ice-cold water (slows it a lot), or
  • remove the catalyst: add sodium hydrogencarbonate to neutralise an acid catalyst, or filter off a solid catalyst.

Worked example: which method won't work?

Br₂(aq) + HCOOH(aq) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) + CO₂(g)

  • Colorimetry works: orange-brown bromine is used up.
  • Gas volume and mass loss work: CO₂ is given off.
  • Conductivity works: ions are produced.
  • Quench and titrate with acid does not: H⁺ is a product, so titrating with acid tells you nothing useful.
  • Colorimetry: "the reactants are colourless but the product NO₂ is reddish-brown" (WCH14 Oct 2023). Name the coloured species.
  • Manganate(VII) titration of H₂O₂: "the concentration (of H₂O₂ is) proportional to the volume of manganate(VII)"; "5:2 ratio" allowed (WCH14 Jun 2025).
  • Solid catalyst: quench by "separating the reaction mixture from the catalyst" (WCH14 Jun 2025).

Often asked as: MCQ "which technique is (not) suitable"; justify colorimetry for a named reaction; how to stop a sample reacting.

  • Colours you need from AS: NO₂ is brown (thermal decomposition of Group 2 nitrates), Br₂(aq) orange/yellow, I₂(aq) yellow-brown, MnO₄⁻ purple (WCH14 Oct 2023 Q15, Jan 2025 Q20).
  • Quench an acid-catalysed reaction with sodium hydrogencarbonate (WCH14 Oct 2025 Q11).

Writing "there must be a colour change" without saying which species is coloured. The commonest slip: saying NO is brown and NO₂ colourless (it's the other way round).

Spec11.4Initial-rate and continuous-monitoring methods

Understand two ways of getting rate data: (i) the initial-rate method, with separate experiments at different starting concentrations of one reagent (a clock reaction is an acceptable approximation); (ii) continuous monitoring, giving a concentration–time or volume–time graph.

Initial-rate method

  • Run several separate experiments. In each one change the starting concentration of one reagent and keep all the others (and the temperature) the same.
  • Measure the rate at the very start, t = 0, when the concentrations are exactly the ones you made up.
  • Make up each mixture to the same total volume with water, so that changing one volume doesn't dilute the others differently.
  • From a concentration–time (or volume–time) curve, the initial rate is the gradient of the tangent drawn at t = 0.

Clock reactions

A clock reaction gives one time, t: the time for a sudden colour change. That time measures the initial rate because:

  • A small, fixed amount of a product has to form before the colour appears.
  • So little reactant has been used by then that the concentrations have hardly changed: the rate is still the initial rate.
  • Rate ∝ 1/t (shorter time, faster reaction).

In the iodine clock (Core Practical 9b, 11.12): iodine is made slowly; a small amount of thiosulfate turns it straight back into iodide. When all the thiosulfate is used up, free iodine meets the starch, and the mixture suddenly turns blue-black.

Continuous monitoring

  • One experiment. Measure the concentration (or a property proportional to it) at many times as the reaction goes.
  • Plot concentration (or volume, or absorbance) against time. Then:
    • read half-lives (11.2), or
    • draw tangents at several points to get rates at different concentrations, then plot rate against concentration (11.5).
  • Examples: gas syringe readings every 10 s; a colorimeter logging absorbance; sampling and titrating (11.6).

Worked example: rate from a clock time

5.0 cm³ of 0.010 mol dm⁻³ Na₂S₂O₃ is in a total volume of 50.0 cm³. The blue-black colour appears after 40 s. Find the rate of formation of iodine.

Moles of S₂O₃²⁻ = 0.0050 × 0.010 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol

I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻, so moles of I₂ formed before the colour = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 2 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol

Concentration of I₂ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.0500 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³

Rate = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 40 = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

  • Clock chemistry: "sodium thiosulfate reacts with the iodine formed"; "when (all) the thiosulfate is used up the iodine reacts with the starch giving a blue-black colour"; "the reciprocal of the time taken … is a measure of the rate".
  • Why it's an initial rate: "the thiosulfate is used up before the concentrations of the other reagents change significantly".
  • Colour change: "colourless to blue-black" (blue or black allowed). "Clear" is not a colour.
  • Initial rate from a graph: "tangent drawn at t = 0" and its gradient, in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

Often asked as: explain how a clock reaction measures an initial rate; state the colour change; calculate a rate from a clock time; draw a tangent at t = 0.

  • The iodine clock used in WCH14 is often hydrogen peroxide + iodide + acid, not propanone + iodine (WCH14 Jan 2023 Q17, Jan 2024 Q16).
  • Moles of I₂ = ½ × moles of thiosulfate; divide by the mixture volume and by the time (WCH14 Jan 2024 Q16).

Dividing moles of iodine by volume but forgetting to divide by time. Using a single point instead of a tangent. Giving the rate per minute when it should be per second.

Saying starch reacts with iodide ions. Starch detects iodine, I₂.

Spec11.5Deducing orders from graphs and initial-rate data

Deduce the order (0, 1 or 2) with respect to a substance from (i) a concentration–time graph, (ii) a rate–concentration graph, (iii) initial-rate data.

(i) Concentration–time graphs

  • Zero order: a straight line going down. The gradient (the rate) is constant and equals −k.
  • First order: a curve with a constant half-life.
  • Second order: a curve that starts steeper and levels off more slowly; half-lives increase.

(ii) Rate–concentration graphs

  • Zero order: horizontal line. Changing [A] doesn't change the rate.
  • First order: straight line through the origin. Rate ∝ [A]; the gradient is k.
  • Second order: upward curve through the origin. To prove it, plot rate against [A]²: you get a straight line through the origin.
  • You get the points by drawing tangents on one concentration–time curve, or from separate initial-rate experiments.

Two graphs are straight lines and easy to mix up: zero order on a concentration–time graph and first order on a rate–concentration graph. Always read the axis labels first.

(iii) Initial-rate tables

  1. Find two experiments where only one concentration changes.
  2. Work out the factor the concentration changes by and the factor the rate changes by.
  3. Same factor → first order; factor squared → second order; no change → zero order.
  4. Repeat for each reactant. If no pair changes only one concentration, allow for the order you already know (see below).
  5. Write the rate equation, then substitute one experiment's values to find k, with units.

Worked example: orders, rate equation and k

BrO₃⁻(aq) + 5Br⁻(aq) + 6H⁺(aq) → 3Br₂(aq) + 3H₂O(l)

  • Exp 1: [BrO₃⁻] 0.10, [Br⁻] 0.10, [H⁺] 0.10; initial rate 1.2 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  • Exp 2: [BrO₃⁻] 0.20, [Br⁻] 0.10, [H⁺] 0.10; rate 2.4 × 10⁻³
  • Exp 3: [BrO₃⁻] 0.20, [Br⁻] 0.20, [H⁺] 0.10; rate 4.8 × 10⁻³
  • Exp 4: [BrO₃⁻] 0.10, [Br⁻] 0.10, [H⁺] 0.20; rate 4.8 × 10⁻³

BrO₃⁻: Exp 1 → 2, [BrO₃⁻] × 2 with the others constant; rate × 2. First order.

Br⁻: Exp 2 → 3, [Br⁻] × 2 with the others constant; rate × 2. First order.

(Exp 1 → 3 would also work: both double and the rate goes × 4. BrO₃⁻ accounts for × 2, so Br⁻ must account for the other × 2.)

H⁺: Exp 1 → 4, [H⁺] × 2; rate × 4 = 2². Second order.

rate = k[BrO₃⁻][Br⁻][H⁺]² (fourth order overall)

From Exp 1: k = 1.2 × 10⁻³ ÷ (0.10 × 0.10 × 0.10²) = 12 dm⁹ mol⁻³ s⁻¹

Order with respect to NO: compare experiments 1 and 2, where only [NO] changes. [NO] doubles and the rate goes from 1.25 × 10−5 to 5.0 × 10−5, a factor of 4 = 22.

So the reaction is second order with respect to NO.

So the reaction is first order with respect to H2.

Write the rate equation. The overall order is 2 + 1 = 3.

\[ \text{rate} = k[\mathrm{NO}]^2[\mathrm{H_2}] \]

Find k by substituting the values from any one experiment, here experiment 1:

\[ k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\mathrm{NO}]^2[\mathrm{H_2}]} = \frac{1.25\times10^{-5}}{(5.0\times10^{-3})^2 \times 2.0\times10^{-3}} = 250\ \mathrm{dm^6\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}} \]

Check with another experiment: \(250 \times (1.0\times10^{-2})^2 \times 4.0\times10^{-3} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\), matching experiment 3.

Experiments 1 and 3: both change. The rate rises by a factor of \(1.08\times10^{-2} / 4.0\times10^{-4} = 27\).

Calculations or annotations on the table are accepted as the explanation.
  • Graphs you plot: "labelled axes with units", "points cover over half the graph paper", points plotted to ±½ square, then the line.
  • First order, rate against concentration: "straight line … beginning at the origin". Concentration–time data: a "smooth curve of best fit", not a ruler.

Spec11.6The acid-catalysed iodination of propanone

Know how to get the data that give the orders with respect to propanone, iodine and hydrogen ions; use the orders to say which species are in the rate-determining step; and suggest a mechanism.

The reaction

CH₃COCH₃(aq) + I₂(aq) → CH₃COCH₂I(aq) + H⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) (catalysed by H⁺)

  • The brown iodine colour fades as iodine is used up.
  • H⁺ is a catalyst: it is used in the mechanism and given back. (It is also a product, but with acid in large excess from the start its concentration hardly changes.)

Getting the orders (Core Practical 9a, see 11.12)

  • Iodine: use propanone and acid in large excess, so their concentrations stay almost constant and only [I₂] changes. Follow [I₂] by sampling, quenching with NaHCO₃ and titrating with thiosulfate (or with a colorimeter). The plot of [I₂] (or titre) against time is a straight line, so the rate doesn't depend on [I₂]: zero order in iodine.
  • Propanone: repeat with double the propanone concentration (same acid and iodine). The gradient doubles: first order.
  • H⁺: repeat with double the acid concentration. The gradient doubles: first order.

rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]

What the rate equation says about the mechanism

  • Propanone and H⁺ are in the rate equation, so they take part in or before the rate-determining step.
  • Iodine isn't in the rate equation, so it reacts in a fast step after the RDS. Changing [I₂] can't speed up a step it isn't part of.
  • Overall order 2 → the RDS involves one propanone and one H⁺ (directly, or via an intermediate formed from them in a fast step before it).

A mechanism that fits

  1. Fast, reversible: a lone pair on the carbonyl oxygen accepts H⁺.
    CH₃COCH₃ + H⁺ ⇌ CH₃C(OH⁺)CH₃
  2. Slow (RDS): a C–H bond on a CH₃ group breaks; H⁺ is lost and the enol forms (C=C with an –OH on it).
    CH₃C(OH⁺)CH₃ → CH₂=C(OH)CH₃ + H⁺
  3. Fast: the electron-rich C=C of the enol attacks I₂; the O–H loses its H⁺ and the C=O re-forms.
    CH₂=C(OH)CH₃ + I₂ → CH₃COCH₂I + H⁺ + I⁻

Check it: the steps add up to the overall equation (H⁺ cancels out as a catalyst); propanone and H⁺ appear up to the slow step; I₂ appears only after it. Step 2 is slow because it breaks a strong C–H bond, so it has a high activation energy.

Worked example: from titre gradient to rate

10.0 cm³ samples are titrated with 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ Na₂S₂O₃. The titre falls by 0.60 cm³ every minute (graph above).

Thiosulfate per minute = 0.60 × 10⁻³ × 0.0100 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol

I₂ per minute = 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 = 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol (in 10.0 cm³)

Change in [I₂] per minute = 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.0100 = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ min⁻¹

Rate = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 60 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

  • Large excess: "so only changes in iodine concentration affect the rate" / "the concentrations of propanone and acid remain (almost) constant".
  • Zero order in iodine: "[I₂] (or volume of thiosulfate) against time gives a straight line", or "rate against [I₂] is a horizontal line".
  • Quench: HCO₃⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l), with state symbols.
  • Mechanism: the step with iodine isn't in the rate equation; the steps with propanone and H⁺ are. The slow step "involves breaking a (strong) bond / has a high activation energy".

Often asked as: why the excess; which graph shows zero order; quench equation; which steps of a given mechanism fit the rate equation, and which is the RDS.

  • Why not quench with NaOH: base also catalyses the reaction (so it wouldn't stop), and in alkali the iodoform reaction / multiple substitution happens (WCH14 Oct 2025 Q11).
  • Showing the acid's anion (Cl⁻) plays no part: change [Cl⁻] with NaCl, or use a different strong acid, and show the rate doesn't change (WCH14 Oct 2025 Q11).
  • Mechanism MCQ: protonation of the C=O oxygen is fast; loss of H⁺ to form the enol is the slow step (WCH14 Jan 2021 Q7).

Concluding that iodine "doesn't react" because it is zero order. It reacts, but after the slow step.

Learning a mechanism by rote and not checking it against the rate equation and the overall equation.

Spec11.7The rate-determining step from a rate equation, and back again

Deduce the rate-determining step from a rate equation, and the rate equation from a given rate-determining step.

The rules

  • The rate equation shows the species (and how many of each) that take part in the rate-determining step or in fast steps before it.
  • Order = number of particles of that species that go into the RDS (directly or via an intermediate made from them before the RDS).
  • Species that only react after the RDS are zero order and not in the rate equation.
  • An intermediate is never written in the rate equation. Replace it with the species that made it.

Rate equation → slow step

Rate equationWhat the slow step contains
rate = k[A]one A only (A breaks up, e.g. a C–Br bond ionises)
rate = k[A][B]one A and one B
rate = k[A]²two A
rate = k[A]²[B]two A and one B. A three-particle collision is unlikely, so expect 2A ⇌ A₂ (fast) then A₂ + B (slow)

Slow step → rate equation

Write the reactants of the slow step. If one is an intermediate, swap it for whatever formed it earlier.

Worked example: is the mechanism consistent?

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is found to be first order in H₂O₂.

Mechanism X: slow step H₂O₂ + H₂O₂ → … (two H₂O₂). This would give rate = k[H₂O₂]², so it isn't consistent.

Mechanism Y: slow step H₂O₂ → H₂O + O (one H₂O₂), then fast O + H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂. This gives rate = k[H₂O₂] and the steps add up to the overall equation, so it is consistent.

For 2NO(g) + Br2(g) → 2NOBr(g) the rate equation is rate = k[NO]2[Br2]. A two-step mechanism is proposed:

Step 1: NO + Br2 → NOBr2
Step 2: NOBr2 + NO → 2NOBr

Check the steps add up: NO + Br2 + NOBr2 + NO → NOBr2 + 2NOBr. Cancelling NOBr2 leaves 2NO + Br2 → 2NOBr, so NOBr2 is an intermediate.

If step 1 were the slow step, it would contain one NO and one Br2, giving rate = k[NO][Br2]. That doesn't match the experiment.

If step 2 is slow, it uses one NOBr2 (made from one NO and one Br2) and one more NO: two NO and one Br2 in total. That gives rate = k[NO]2[Br2], matching the experiment.

So step 2 is the rate-determining step, and step 1 must be fast.

  • "the slow step … involves two molecules of H₂O₂ so the mechanism is not consistent with being a first-order reaction" (WCH14 Jun 2025).
  • Two checks for "justify": "the steps add up to the overall equation" and "the rate equation matches the species in the slow step". Both are needed (WCH14 Oct 2023: only 14% gave both).

Often asked as: MCQ which step fits the rate equation; complete a two-step mechanism; justify whether a proposed mechanism agrees.

  • An intermediate (e.g. N₂O₂) can appear in the slow step; the rate equation then shows the species that formed it (WCH14 Jan 2026 Q13).

Checking only one thing (just the overall equation, or just the rate equation) in a "justify" question.

Giving a stock answer without looking at the steps you've been given.

Spec11.8Deducing a mechanism from the rate equation and the overall equation

Use the rate equation and the balanced (stoichiometric) equation together to suggest a reaction mechanism.

Method

  1. From the rate equation, write the slow step: it contains the species (and numbers) in the rate equation.
  2. Use fast steps before or after it to account for everything else in the overall equation.
  3. Check that the steps add up to the overall equation (intermediates and catalysts cancel).
  4. Check that each step involves no more than two particles colliding.

Why most reactions have several steps

A balanced equation like BrO₃⁻ + 5Br⁻ + 6H⁺ has 12 reactant particles. They can't all collide at once: a collision of even three particles at the same moment, in the right orientation and with enough energy, is very unlikely. So the reaction must happen in a series of steps involving one or two particles each.

Worked example: three mechanisms

2NO₂(g) + F₂(g) → 2NO₂F(g), rate = k[NO₂][F₂]

Slow: NO₂ + F₂ → NO₂F + F

Fast: NO₂ + F → NO₂F

Sum: 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F ✓ (F is an intermediate)

NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g), rate = k[NO₂]² (zero order in CO)

Slow: NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO

Fast: NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂

Sum: NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ ✓ (CO only reacts after the slow step)

2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g), rate = k[NO]²[O₂]

Fast, reversible: 2NO ⇌ N₂O₂

Slow: N₂O₂ + O₂ → 2NO₂

Sum ✓. The slow step contains N₂O₂ (made from 2NO) and O₂, which matches [NO]²[O₂] without needing a three-particle collision.

Worked example: hydrogen peroxide and iodide

H₂O₂ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → I₂ + 2H₂O, rate = k[H₂O₂][I⁻] (zero order in H⁺)

Slow: H₂O₂ + I⁻ → IO⁻ + H₂O

Fast: IO⁻ + H⁺ → HIO

Fast: HIO + I⁻ + H⁺ → I₂ + H₂O

Sum: H₂O₂ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → I₂ + 2H₂O ✓

  • Must be multi-step: "there are only 4 particles in the rate equation and 12 in the equation for the reaction", or "collisions with more than two particles are unlikely" (WCH14 Jan 2022).
  • "a three-particle collision is unlikely" (hard / difficult / impossible are allowed) (WCH14 Oct 2023).
  • 2NO + O₂: "two moles of NO" in step 1 forming N₂O₂, and "O₂ in the rate-determining step": N₂O₂ + O₂ → 2NO₂. A reversible arrow in step 1 is allowed (WCH14 Jan 2026).

Often asked as: explain why the reaction can't be one step; propose steps that fit a rate equation; say which step is slow.

Linking "number of particles" questions to SN1/SN2 when the reaction has nothing to do with halogenoalkanes (examiner report, Jan 2022).

Pasting the stock "five particles can't collide at once" into a question about something else (e.g. the activation energy of a solid reaction).

Spec11.9Rate equations as evidence for SN1 and SN2

Understand how the rate equations for the hydrolysis of primary and tertiary halogenoalkanes give evidence for the SN2 and SN1 mechanisms.

The evidence

Primary, e.g. 1-bromobutaneTertiary, e.g. 2-bromo-2-methylpropane
Rate equation (with OH⁻)rate = k[RBr][OH⁻]rate = k[RBr]
Overall order21
What's in the RDSthe halogenoalkane and OH⁻the halogenoalkane only
MechanismSN2: one stepSN1: two steps, first one slow
Energy profileone hump (transition state)two humps (carbocation intermediate)

S = substitution, N = nucleophilic, 1 or 2 = the number of species in the rate-determining step.

SN2 (primary): one step

  • The C–Br bond is polar: C is δ+, Br is δ−.
  • A lone pair on the oxygen of OH⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon, from the side opposite the bromine.
  • At the same time the C–Br bond breaks and both electrons go to Br, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • Halfway, there is a transition state [HO···C···Br]⁻: five groups round the carbon, two partial bonds, an overall negative charge (written outside square brackets).
  • Both the halogenoalkane and OH⁻ are in this one slow step, so the reaction is first order in each.

SN1 (tertiary): two steps

  1. Slow: the C–Br bond breaks heterolytically on its own. Both electrons go to Br; a tertiary carbocation (CH₃)₃C⁺ and Br⁻ form. Only the halogenoalkane is involved, so rate = k[RBr].
  2. Fast: a lone pair on OH⁻ (or on a water molecule) attacks the positive carbon to give the alcohol (CH₃)₃COH.

OH⁻ only reacts in the fast second step, so its concentration doesn't affect the rate.

Why primary and tertiary go different ways

  • Tertiary → SN1: three alkyl groups push electrons towards the positive carbon, so the tertiary carbocation is stabilised. The three bulky groups also block an OH⁻ attacking from the back (steric hindrance), so SN2 is difficult.
  • Primary → SN2: a primary carbocation would be very unstable, but the carbon is easy to reach (little steric hindrance).
  • Secondary halogenoalkanes can react by both.

Worked example: data to mechanism

Hydrolysis of 2-bromobutane with OH⁻: doubling [2-bromobutane] doubles the rate; doubling [OH⁻] doubles the rate.

rate = k[C₄H₉Br][OH⁻], second order overall. Both species are in the slow step, so the mechanism is SN2: one step through a transition state.

If instead doubling [OH⁻] had no effect, rate = k[C₄H₉Br], and the mechanism would be SN1: slow ionisation to a carbocation, then fast attack by OH⁻.

  • "rate = k[RX][OH⁻]" for primary, "rate = k[RX]" for tertiary; "primary SN2, tertiary SN1".
  • Primary: the slow step is the halogenoalkane reacting with OH⁻ "via a transition state". DO NOT AWARD "intermediate" for transition state.
  • Tertiary: the slow step forms "a carbocation (in a step that only involves the halogenoalkane)", then "the hydroxide ion attacks the carbocation in a (fast) second step".
  • SN2 diagram: lone pair on O of OH⁻; dipole on C–Br; arrow from lone pair to the δ+ carbon; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br; transition state in square brackets with the negative charge outside. A mechanism that doesn't match the given rate equation scores at most 2.
  • SN1 profile: "two activation energies imply two steps / an intermediate"; "the first activation energy is larger so the first step is slower".

Often asked as: 6-mark answer comparing a primary and a tertiary bromoalkane (WCH14 Oct 2021 Q18, Jan 2025 Q18); deduce SN1 or SN2 from orders and draw it; MCQ; annotate an energy profile.

  • Steric hindrance and carbocation stability as the reasons (WCH14 Oct 2025 Q6, Jan 2026 Q3).
  • Optical activity: SN2 on one enantiomer gives inversion (one optically active product); SN1 goes through a planar carbocation that is attacked equally from both sides, giving a racemic mixture (WCH14 Oct 2024 Q19, Oct 2025 Q6). See 15.5.

Drawing SN2 when the rate equation has no OH⁻ in it. Drawing SN2 without the transition state, or with arrows that don't start at a lone pair or a bond.

Writing SN1 without the Br⁻ leaving group, or without saying "slow" then "fast".

Using the "planar carbocation, attacked from above and below" answer in an SN2 question.

Spec11.10Finding the activation energy from experimental data

Use calculations and graphs to find the activation energy of a reaction from experimental data. (The Arrhenius equation is given if you need it.)

Why k increases with temperature

  • At a higher temperature the particles have more kinetic energy on average, so they collide more often, but that is a small effect.
  • The big effect: a much larger proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea (the shaded area in the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution grows a lot).
  • So more collisions are successful per second and k increases. Roughly, a 10 K rise often doubles the rate.

The Arrhenius equation

k = A e−Ea/RT

  • k = rate constant; A = the pre-exponential (Arrhenius) constant, linked to collision frequency and orientation; Ea in J mol⁻¹; R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; T in kelvin.
  • Take natural logs: ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT), which is the straight-line form ln k = (−Ea/R) × (1/T) + ln A.

The Arrhenius equation and its logarithmic form

\[ k = A\,e^{-E_\mathrm{a}/RT} \qquad\qquad \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{T} \]
\(k\): rate constant\(A\): Arrhenius constant (same units as k)\(E_\mathrm{a}\): activation energy / J mol⁻¹\(R\) = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹\(T\): temperature / K

Graphical method

  1. For each temperature, find k (or the rate, or 1/t for a clock reaction: at fixed concentrations all three are proportional to k).
  2. Calculate 1/T (T in K) and ln k.
  3. Plot ln k (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis). You get a straight line with a negative gradient.
  4. Gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R. The y-intercept is ln A.

Worked example: Ea from a graph

  • T = 293 K: k = 5.49 × 10⁻² s⁻¹; 1/T = 3.413 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; ln k = −2.90
  • T = 303 K: k = 0.105 s⁻¹; 1/T = 3.300 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; ln k = −2.25
  • T = 313 K: k = 0.193 s⁻¹; 1/T = 3.195 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; ln k = −1.64
  • T = 323 K: k = 0.342 s⁻¹; 1/T = 3.096 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; ln k = −1.07
  • T = 333 K: k = 0.586 s⁻¹; 1/T = 3.003 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; ln k = −0.53

Gradient (from the line, between 1/T = 3.00 × 10⁻³ and 3.40 × 10⁻³) = −2.31 ÷ (0.40 × 10⁻³) = −5780 K

Ea = −(−5780) × 8.31 = 48 000 J mol⁻¹ = +48.0 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 SF)

Worked example: two temperatures, no graph

The rate constant doubles between 298 K and 308 K. Find Ea.

Write ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT) for each temperature and subtract (ln A cancels):

ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)

ln 2 = (Ea ÷ 8.31) × (1/298 − 1/308)

0.693 = (Ea ÷ 8.31) × 1.0895 × 10⁻⁴

Ea = 0.693 × 8.31 ÷ (1.0895 × 10⁻⁴) = 52 900 J mol⁻¹ = +52.9 kJ mol⁻¹

Worked example: one point, A given

k = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at 500 K and A = 1.0 × 10¹³ s⁻¹. Find Ea.

ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT), so Ea = RT(ln A − ln k) = 8.31 × 500 × (29.93 − (−8.05))

= 8.31 × 500 × 37.98 = 157 800 J mol⁻¹ = +158 kJ mol⁻¹

A first-order reaction has k = 2.5 × 10−4 s−1 at 320 K and Ea = 85.0 kJ mol−1. Calculate A.

Work out the exponent, with Ea in J mol−1:

\[ \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{RT} = \frac{85\,000}{8.31\times320} = 31.96 \qquad e^{-31.96} = 1.31\times10^{-14} \]

Rearrange k = A e−Ea/RT:

\[ A = \frac{k}{e^{-E_\mathrm{a}/RT}} = \frac{2.5\times10^{-4}}{1.31\times10^{-14}} = 1.9\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

A has the same units as k, here s−1.

A catalyst lowers the activation energy of a reaction from 75 kJ mol−1 to 50 kJ mol−1. Assuming A is unchanged, by what factor does k increase at 298 K?

Divide the two Arrhenius expressions. A cancels:

\[ \frac{k_\mathrm{cat}}{k} = \frac{e^{-50\,000/RT}}{e^{-75\,000/RT}} = e^{25\,000/(8.31\times298)} = e^{10.1} \approx 2.4\times10^{4} \]

Lowering Ea by a third makes the reaction about 24 000 times faster, which is why catalysts matter so much in industry.

  • "Ea = −gradient × R"; positive answer; units kJ mol⁻¹ or J mol⁻¹; usually 3 SF.
  • Large Ea → "kinetically stable / rate is low".

Spec11.11Solid (heterogeneous) catalysts in industry

Understand how a solid catalyst works for an industrial gas-phase reaction, by providing a surface for the reaction.

Three steps at the surface

  1. Adsorption: gas molecules form weak bonds to active sites on the catalyst surface. This weakens the bonds inside the reactant molecules and holds them close together in a favourable orientation.
  2. Reaction on the surface, by a route with a lower activation energy.
  3. Desorption: the product molecules break away from the surface, leaving the site free for more reactant.

Making a good industrial catalyst

  • Large surface area: finely divided powder, pellets, or a thin coat on a ceramic honeycomb (catalytic converters). More surface → more active sites → faster reaction, and less of an expensive metal is needed.
  • The right strength of adsorption: strong enough to weaken bonds, weak enough for products to leave.
  • Poisoning: impurities (e.g. sulfur compounds; lead in old petrol on catalytic converters) adsorb strongly and block active sites. Feed gases are purified to prevent this.

Spec11.12Core Practicals 9a and 9b: iodine–propanone by titration, and an iodine clock

Core Practical 9a: follow the rate of the iodine–propanone reaction by a titration method. Core Practical 9b: investigate a clock reaction (Harcourt–Esson iodine clock).

Core practical 9a: iodine and propanone by titration

Aim: find the order with respect to iodine (and, with extra runs, propanone and H⁺).

  1. Mix known volumes of propanone and sulfuric (or hydrochloric) acid in a flask; both are in large excess over iodine.
  2. Add a known volume of iodine solution and start the clock at the moment of mixing. Swirl.
  3. Every few minutes, withdraw a 10.0 cm³ sample with a pipette and run it into a flask of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution. This quenches the reaction by neutralising the acid catalyst. Note the exact time.
  4. Titrate the iodine left in each sample with standard sodium thiosulfate. Add starch near the end point (when the solution is pale yellow): end point blue-black → colourless.
  5. Plot titre (∝ [I₂]) against time. A straight line shows zero order in iodine; the gradient gives the rate (worked example in 11.6).
  6. Repeat with a different starting [propanone] (then a different [H⁺]), everything else the same, and compare gradients to find those orders.

I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻

Control: temperature (water bath), total volume, the volume of each sample, the concentrations of the excess reagents.

Safety: propanone is highly flammable (no flames); iodine solution stains and irritates; wear eye protection.

Core practical 9b: an iodine clock

Aim: find the order with respect to one reagent (e.g. iodide) by timing a colour change.

Reaction (one common version): H₂O₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → I₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l), slow

Clock step: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻, very fast

  1. In a beaker, mix potassium iodide, sulfuric acid, a small fixed volume of sodium thiosulfate, starch and water.
  2. Add the hydrogen peroxide, start the clock and stir.
  3. Stop the clock when the mixture suddenly turns blue-black. Record t.
  4. Repeat with different volumes of KI, making up the same total volume with water so only [I⁻] changes. Keep the temperature constant.
  5. Use 1/t as the rate. Plot 1/t against [I⁻] (or volume of KI): a straight line through the origin shows first order.

Why it works: the thiosulfate removes iodine as fast as it forms, so no blue colour appears. When the thiosulfate runs out, the next iodine formed reacts with starch. The thiosulfate is used up before the other concentrations have changed much, so 1/t measures the initial rate.

Another version uses peroxodisulfate: S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂.

Making the results reliable

  • Same total volume in every run, measured with burettes or graduated pipettes.
  • Thermostat the solutions before mixing; record the temperature.
  • Start timing at the moment of mixing; judge the colour change against a white tile. Human reaction time matters most for short times, so choose concentrations that give times of tens of seconds at least.
  • Repeat each run and take a mean.
  • Clock: "sodium thiosulfate reacts with the iodine formed"; "when the thiosulfate is used up the iodine reacts with starch giving a blue-black colour"; "1/time is a measure of rate".
  • Large excess: "so the concentrations of propanone and acid remain (almost) constant".
  • Colour change in the titration end point and in the clock: give both colours in the right order.

Often asked as: describe how a clock reaction measures an initial rate; calculate rates from clock times; use titration data to show zero order in iodine; explain each step of the method (WCH14 Jan 2023 Q17, Jan 2024 Q16, Oct 2025 Q11).

Saying the blue-black colour appears "when the iodine is used up". It appears when the thiosulfate is used up.

Changing the volume of one reagent without making up the total volume with water: then every concentration changes.

Spec11.13Core Practical 10: finding the activation energy

Core Practical 10: find the activation energy of a reaction.

Principle

Keep every concentration the same and change only the temperature. Then the rate (or 1/t for a clock reaction) is proportional to k, so ln(1/t) against 1/T gives a line of gradient −Ea/R.

Core practical 10: method in brief

A common version uses a clock reaction between bromide and bromate(V) ions in acid:

5Br⁻ + BrO₃⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3Br₂ + 3H₂O

A small fixed amount of phenol removes the bromine as it forms. When the phenol is used up, bromine bleaches methyl red indicator: time how long the red colour takes to disappear.

  1. Put the bromide/bromate mixture, phenol and methyl red in one boiling tube and the sulfuric acid in another.
  2. Stand both tubes in a water bath until they reach the bath temperature. Record it with a thermometer in the mixture.
  3. Mix, start the clock, and stop it when the red colour disappears. Record t.
  4. Repeat at about five temperatures between room temperature and about 60 °C, with identical volumes each time.
  5. Calculate 1/T (T in K) and ln(1/t). Plot ln(1/t) against 1/T; Ea = −gradient × R.

Other versions: the peroxodisulfate/iodide clock at different temperatures; magnesium ribbon in hydrochloric acid (time for the ribbon to disappear).

Safety: phenol is toxic and corrosive (gloves, eye protection); bromine fumes; hot water.

Worked example: from clock times to Ea

At 20 °C, t = 125 s; at 40 °C, t = 34.6 s.

T₁ = 293 K, 1/T₁ = 3.413 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, ln(1/t₁) = ln(0.00800) = −4.83

T₂ = 313 K, 1/T₂ = 3.195 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, ln(1/t₂) = ln(0.0289) = −3.54

Gradient = (−3.54 − (−4.83)) ÷ (3.195 × 10⁻³ − 3.413 × 10⁻³) = 1.29 ÷ (−0.218 × 10⁻³) = −5920 K

Ea = 5920 × 8.31 = 49 200 J mol⁻¹ ≈ +49 kJ mol⁻¹

(In the exam you'd plot all five points and take the gradient from the line.)

Errors and improvements

  • Temperature drifts while the reaction runs: use a thermostatted bath; record the temperature at the start and end and use the mean.
  • Judging the end point: use a white tile; a colorimeter removes the judgement.
  • Mixing time is significant at high temperatures (short times): choose concentrations that give longer times.
  • The practical procedure is rarely examined; the data analysis is (see 11.10): axes, scale, best-fit line, gradient with sign and unit (K), Ea = −gradient × R, sign, units, SF.

Often asked as: plot ln k or ln(rate) against 1/T from given data and find Ea (WCH14 Jan 2024 Q16, Oct 2025 Q2).

Changing a concentration as well as the temperature: then the rate isn't proportional to k.

Using °C for T, or forgetting that ln(1/t) is negative when t > 1 s.

Quick checks

The rate equation for a reaction is rate = k[X]2[Y]. What are the units of k?

The overall order is 3. k = rate/([X]2[Y]) = (mol dm−3 s−1)/(mol dm−3)3 = mol−2 dm6 s−1. The second option is for a second-order reaction.

A reaction is first order with respect to A and second order with respect to B. Both concentrations are doubled at the same time. By what factor does the rate increase?

Doubling [A] multiplies the rate by 2; doubling [B] multiplies it by 22 = 4. The effects multiply: 2 × 4 = 8. Adding the orders (to get 3) or adding the factors (to get 6) are the usual slips.

A first-order reaction has a half-life of 1400 s. Starting from 1.0 mol dm−3, what is the concentration after 4200 s?

4200 s is three half-lives, because a first-order half-life is constant: 1.0 → 0.50 → 0.25 → 0.125 mol dm−3. Dividing by 3 instead of halving three times gives the wrong answer of 0.33.

The iodination of propanone has rate = k[CH3COCH3][H+]. Which statement must be true?

Iodine is a reactant, but it is zero order, so it is not involved in the slow step and must react afterwards. A zero-order reactant means there are at least two steps. Changing [I2] has no effect on the rate.

A graph of ln k against 1/T for a reaction has a gradient of −7500 K. What is the activation energy? (R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1)

Gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = 7500 × 8.31 = 62 300 J mol−1 = 62.3 kJ mol−1. Activation energy is always positive; dividing by R instead of multiplying gives 0.90.

In a clock reaction, why can 1/t be used as a measure of the initial rate?

Rate = amount formed ÷ time. The amount (fixed by the thiosulfate) is the same in every run, so the rate is proportional to 1/t. Keeping that amount small means the concentrations are still close to their starting values, so it is an initial rate.

Increasing the pressure of a gas reacting on a solid catalyst has no effect on the rate. What is the best explanation?

Reaction only happens at active sites. Once they are saturated, extra gas molecules have nowhere to adsorb, so the rate stays the same and the reaction is zero order in that gas.
