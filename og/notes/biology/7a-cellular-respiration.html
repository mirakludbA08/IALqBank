Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is how a cell releases the chemical energy stored in food and captures it as ATP. This page follows the route of glucose through glycolysis, the link reaction, the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain, then looks at what changes when oxygen runs out, the respiratory quotient and the two respiration core practicals.

Spec7.1Aerobic respiration: the overall reaction

(i) Know that aerobic respiration splits the respiratory substrate, releasing CO₂ as a waste product, and joins the hydrogen from it to oxygen, releasing a large amount of energy. (ii) Know that respiration happens in many small steps, each catalysed by its own intracellular enzyme. You don't need enzyme names.

The overall equation

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (about 2870 kJ released per mole of glucose)

Read it the way the spec does:

  • Splitting the substrate. The carbon skeleton of glucose is broken down step by step. Its carbon atoms leave as CO₂, a waste product, in the decarboxylation reactions of the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
  • Reuniting hydrogen with oxygen. Hydrogen atoms are removed from the substrate (dehydrogenation) and carried by the coenzymes NAD and FAD to the electron transport chain. There they finally combine with oxygen from the air to make water. This is where most of the energy is released.
  • The energy isn't "produced". It is released from the substrate and transferred to make ATP from ADP + Pi. Much of it (about 60%) is released as heat, which helps keep mammals warm.

Oxidation and reduction

OxidationReduction
Hydrogenlostgained
Electronslostgained
Oxygengainedlost
  • Glucose is oxidised (it loses hydrogen). Oxygen is reduced (it gains hydrogen and electrons) to water.
  • NAD + 2H → reduced NAD. Reduced NAD is the carrier "loaded" with hydrogen; it becomes NAD again when it hands the hydrogen on.

Why respiration happens in steps

  • Respiration is a series of reactions: the product of one step is the substrate of the next. Each step is catalysed by a specific intracellular enzyme, because each enzyme's active site fits only one substrate.
  • Releasing the energy in small amounts:
    • lets much of it be captured in ATP at several points, instead of all being lost as heat at once (as when glucose burns),
    • avoids a sudden temperature rise that would denature enzymes,
    • allows control: the rate of a key step can be slowed when ATP is plentiful,
    • provides intermediates that other pathways use, and entry points for other substrates (fatty acids, amino acids, glycerol).

ATP: the energy currency

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) = adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups.
  • Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP + Pi, catalysed by ATPase, releases energy in small, usable amounts exactly where it's needed: active transport, muscle contraction, protein synthesis, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in neurones.
  • ATP synthase makes ATP from ADP + Pi (a condensation reaction) using energy released in respiration.
  • ATP is soluble, moves easily around the cell and is remade very quickly. It is an immediate energy source, not a long-term store.

Spec7.2Glycolysis

Understand what glycolysis does in both aerobic and anaerobic respiration: phosphorylation of a hexose, ATP made by substrate-level phosphorylation, reduced NAD, pyruvate and (in animals without oxygen) lactate. You don't need the intermediate stages or compounds.

Where and when

  • Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm. It doesn't need oxygen, so it is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration.
  • Glyco-lysis means "sugar splitting": one 6C glucose becomes two 3C pyruvate.

The steps (per glucose)

  1. Phosphorylation. Two ATP are hydrolysed and their phosphate groups are added to glucose, making a phosphorylated hexose (hexose bisphosphate). This makes the sugar more reactive (lowers the activation energy for splitting it), and the charged phosphates stop it leaving the cell.
  2. Splitting. The phosphorylated hexose splits into two triose phosphate molecules (3C).
  3. Oxidation. Hydrogen is removed from each triose phosphate (dehydrogenation). NAD picks it up, making 2 reduced NAD.
  4. ATP formation. Phosphate groups are transferred from the intermediates directly to ADP, making 4 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. The end product is 2 pyruvate (3C).

Net yield per glucose: 2 ATP (4 made − 2 used), 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate. All of this happens without oxygen.

  • Substrate-level phosphorylation: ATP made by transferring a phosphate group directly from a substrate molecule to ADP. It happens in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle and needs no electron transport chain. (Compare oxidative phosphorylation, 7.4.)

What happens to pyruvate next

Oxygen availableNo oxygen (anaerobic)
Animalsactively transported into the mitochondrial matrix → link reactionreduced to lactate by reduced NAD (one step, no CO₂)
Yeast and plantsas in animalsdecarboxylated to ethanal (CO₂ released), then ethanal reduced to ethanol by reduced NAD

Why anaerobic respiration makes lactate (or ethanol)

  • Without oxygen, the electron transport chain stops, so reduced NAD can't be reoxidised there.
  • A cell has only a little NAD. Once it is all reduced, step 3 of glycolysis can't happen and glycolysis would stop.
  • Turning pyruvate into lactate uses the hydrogen from reduced NAD, so NAD is regenerated. Glycolysis can keep going and keep making 2 ATP per glucose.
  • That is far less ATP than aerobic respiration, because pyruvate isn't oxidised any further: most of the energy stays locked in lactate (or ethanol).
  • Pyruvate in anaerobic conditions: "reduced / converted to lactate"; "reduced NAD is oxidised", so NAD is regenerated and "glycolysis can continue"; "lactate transported to the liver". ACCEPT "H atoms added to pyruvate".
  • Products of glycolysis: "pyruvate", "reduced NAD", "ATP" (net 2).
  • Why cyanide doesn't stop anaerobic ATP production: "anaerobic respiration involves just glycolysis", "ATP made in glycolysis (by substrate-level phosphorylation)", "in the cytoplasm, so mitochondria / the ETC aren't needed".
  • Less ATP: anaerobic respiration must be linked to less ATP per glucose. "Anaerobic respiration" on its own is not credited.

Often asked as: MCQs on where glycolysis happens and on its first step (phosphorylation of hexose); identify ADP, ATP, NAD and reduced NAD on a pathway diagram; why cyanide doesn't affect anaerobic ATP production; why a cheetah can sprint only briefly.

  • "Anaerobic respiration produces no ATP." It makes a net 2 ATP per glucose.
  • "Lactate is made to produce ATP." Its job is to regenerate NAD; the ATP comes from glycolysis.
  • Saying CO₂ is released when lactate forms. Only the ethanol pathway releases CO₂.
  • Writing NADP or NADPH (those belong to photosynthesis). Write "reduced NAD" to match the spec.
  • "The phosphate comes from pyruvate." It comes from the phosphorylated intermediates.
  • ATP yields are supplied in questions, e.g. anaerobic 2 v aerobic 36 per glucose (WBI15 Jun 2023). Glucose released from glycogen is already phosphorylated without using ATP, so it gives 3 ATP and 2 lactate (WBI15 Oct 2021).
  • Cheetah sprints: fast-twitch fibres, anaerobic respiration, lactate build-up and oxygen debt; also heat builds up faster than homeostasis can remove it (WBI15 Oct 2022).
  • Log-scale graphs (lactate against body mass in crocodiles). Say aerobic respiration "cannot keep up" with ATP demand in vigorous exercise, and link every trend to aerobic or anaerobic respiration (WBI15 Oct 2021, 6-mark).

Spec7.3Link reaction and Krebs cycle

Understand how the link reaction and Krebs cycle complete the oxidation of glucose: CO₂ formed by decarboxylation, ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and reduced NAD and reduced FAD by dehydrogenation. Both happen in the mitochondria (matrix), while glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm. You don't need the names of other compounds.

Mitochondrion structure

PartFeatureRole
Outer membranesmoothpyruvate is actively transported across it
Inner membranefolded into cristae (large surface area)holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase: oxidative phosphorylation
Intermembrane spacenarrowH⁺ builds up here
Matrixfluid containing enzymes, mitochondrial DNA and ribosomeslink reaction and Krebs cycle

Three kinds of reaction to recognise

  • Decarboxylation: a carboxyl group is removed as CO₂.
  • Dehydrogenation: hydrogen is removed (an oxidation) and picked up by NAD or FAD, which are reduced.
  • Substrate-level phosphorylation: a phosphate group is transferred directly to ADP, making ATP.

Link reaction (once per pyruvate, so twice per glucose)

  1. Pyruvate (3C) is actively transported into the matrix.
  2. It is decarboxylated (CO₂ removed) and dehydrogenated (hydrogen to NAD → reduced NAD). What's left is a 2C acetyl group.
  3. The acetyl group combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl CoA, which carries it to the Krebs cycle.
  • No ATP is made in the link reaction.

Krebs cycle (once per acetyl group, so twice per glucose)

  1. The 2C acetyl group joins a 4C compound to make a 6C compound. Coenzyme A is released to collect another acetyl group.
  2. The 6C compound is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated: a 5C compound, CO₂ and reduced NAD are made.
  3. The 5C compound is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated: a 4C compound, CO₂ and reduced NAD.
  4. The 4C compound is converted back into the original 4C acceptor. On the way, 1 ATP is made by substrate-level phosphorylation, and 1 reduced FAD and 1 more reduced NAD are formed.
  • It's a cycle because the 4C acceptor is regenerated, ready for the next acetyl group.
  • Per turn: 2 CO₂, 1 ATP, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD. Per glucose, double it.

Follow the carbon

6C glucose → 2 × 3C pyruvate → 2 × 2C acetyl + 2 CO₂ (link reaction) → 4 CO₂ (two turns of the Krebs cycle). That's 6 CO₂: every carbon atom of glucose has left as CO₂.

  • Fate of pyruvate in aerobic respiration (any 3): "moves into the matrix"; "dehydrogenated / oxidised by NAD"; "decarboxylated / CO₂ removed" to "a 2C acetyl group"; "combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl CoA"; "enters the Krebs cycle".
  • Krebs cycle products (only the first three you list are marked): "carbon dioxide", "ATP", "reduced NAD", "reduced FAD". Numbers like "6 ATP" are ignored. Reduced NADP is not accepted.
  • Drawing a mitochondrion: "double membrane with cristae", "matrix labelled as the site of the Krebs cycle".
  • Data on isolated mitochondria: pyruvate is "used in the link reaction / aerobic respiration"; "ADP is needed to make ATP" (respiration slows if ADP runs out); an inhibitor of pyruvate transport "stops pyruvate entering the matrix". Refer to each sample by name (WBI15 Jun 2022).

Often asked as: MCQs on locations and on how substrates enter the Krebs cycle; name three products of the Krebs cycle; describe the fate of pyruvate; label the site of the Krebs cycle; magnification or width of a mitochondrion from an electron micrograph.

  • Putting the Krebs cycle on the cristae, or just "in the mitochondria". The link reaction and Krebs cycle are in the matrix; the electron transport chain is on the inner membrane.
  • Mixing up decarboxylation (CO₂ removed) and dehydrogenation (hydrogen removed).
  • Saying the link reaction makes ATP.
  • Reciting the Krebs cycle in a data question without using the data.
  • Names the spec says you don't need are still credited: acetyl CoA, coenzyme A, oxaloacetate (the 4C acceptor), citrate (the 6C compound), "TCA cycle" (WBI15 Jan 2025).
  • Other substrates: fatty acids are broken into 2C units that enter as acetyl CoA; amino acids enter as pyruvate, acetyl CoA or Krebs intermediates; glycerol enters glycolysis as triose phosphate.
  • Magnification of a mitochondrion in standard form: convert mm to nm before dividing; watch the sign of the power of 10 (WBI15 Jan 2022, Jun 2022).

Spec7.4Oxidative phosphorylation: the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis

Understand how ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation, linked to the electron transport chain in the mitochondria, including the roles of chemiosmosis and ATP synthase.

Step by step

  1. Reduced NAD and reduced FAD deliver hydrogen atoms to the electron transport chain (ETC): a series of electron carrier proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae). The coenzymes are reoxidised, so NAD and FAD can return to the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
  2. Each hydrogen atom splits into a proton (H⁺) and an electron (e⁻).
  3. The electrons pass from carrier to carrier in a series of redox reactions (each carrier is reduced, then oxidised). Each carrier is at a lower energy level than the one before, so energy is released at each step.
  4. That energy is used to pump (actively transport) H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  5. H⁺ builds up in the intermembrane space: a proton gradient (an electrochemical gradient) forms across the inner membrane, which is impermeable to H⁺.
  6. H⁺ diffuses back into the matrix down the gradient, but only through the channel in ATP synthase. This flow drives ATP synthase to join ADP + Pi → ATP. Making ATP using this flow of protons is chemiosmosis.
  7. At the end of the chain, oxygen is the final electron acceptor. It combines with electrons and H⁺ to form water: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O.

The first interactive diagram at the foot of the page runs the chain for you, so you can see what changes when oxygen runs out.

Why oxygen matters

  • No oxygen → no final electron acceptor → electrons can't leave the last carrier → the chain stops → no H⁺ is pumped → no chemiosmosis → very little ATP.
  • Reduced NAD and reduced FAD can't be reoxidised, so the cell runs out of NAD and FAD. The link reaction and Krebs cycle stop too. Only glycolysis continues (anaerobic respiration).

  • Edexcel questions give you the numbers: 38 ATP and 30.51 kJ per ATP (WBI15 Jun 2021); 2 anaerobic v 36 aerobic (WBI15 Jun 2023). Use the values in the question.

  • Chemiosmosis (any 5): "hydrogen (atoms) carried by reduced NAD / reduced FAD to the ETC"; "split into protons and electrons"; "electrons pass along the chain, releasing energy"; "energy used to pump protons into the intermembrane space"; "protons diffuse back (down the electrochemical gradient) into the matrix through ATP synthase"; "forming ATP from ADP and Pi".
  • Movement words: protons are "pumped / actively transported" out and "diffuse" back. "Stalked particle" is accepted for ATP synthase; "ATPase" is not. "ADP + Pi → ATP" with no mention of ATP synthase is ignored.
  • Oxygen: "terminal / final electron acceptor", "accepts electrons and H⁺ to form water", "no oxygen means electrons cannot pass along the chain".

Often asked as: describe the role of chemiosmosis in ATP synthesis (2–5 marks); describe oxidative phosphorylation; the role of reduced NAD or of oxygen; effects of inhibitors (cyanide, carbon monoxide, amytal, hydramethylnon); 6-mark interpretation of oxygen-consumption data for ETC defects.

  • Mixing up the active and passive steps: protons are pumped into the intermembrane space and diffuse back through ATP synthase.
  • Confusing the inner membrane with the intermembrane space.
  • Starting a sentence with "It…" so the examiner can't tell whether you mean NAD or reduced NAD. Name the molecule.
  • "Affects ATP production." Say increases or decreases.
  • Stopping at "anaerobic respiration" in an inhibitor question. Carry on: less ATP, so less muscle contraction (or whatever the context needs).
  • Named ETC complexes (I to IV), coenzyme Q and cytochrome c appear in data questions. You only need to follow where electrons enter and where a blocker stops them (WBI15 Jan 2021, 6-mark).
  • Carbon monoxide binds to the haem in haemoglobin more strongly than O₂, so less O₂ reaches the cells to act as the final electron acceptor, and less ATP is made (WBI15 Jun 2023).
  • An ETC inhibitor such as amytal reduces oxygen use and Krebs cycle activity, because NAD is no longer regenerated (WBI15 Jan 2025).
  • Mitochondrial DNA codes for some ETC proteins, so mutations in it reduce ATP production (WBI15 Jan 2023 article).

Spec7.5What happens to lactate after anaerobic respiration

Understand what happens to the lactate made in animals after a period of anaerobic respiration.

During hard exercise

  • Muscles need ATP faster than oxygen can be delivered, so extra ATP comes from anaerobic respiration (glycolysis → lactate) and from creatine phosphate.
  • Lactate builds up in the muscle and passes into the blood. Lactic acid releases H⁺, so blood pH falls. Low pH inhibits enzymes and contributes to muscle fatigue.

After exercise

  1. Lactate diffuses into the blood and is carried to the liver (heart muscle also takes some up and uses it).
  2. Once oxygen is available, lactate is oxidised back to pyruvate (its hydrogen goes to NAD).
  3. The pyruvate is then either:
    • respired aerobically: link reaction, Krebs cycle and ETC, giving CO₂, water and ATP; or
    • converted to glucose in the liver, which goes into the blood or is stored as glycogen.
  • The extra oxygen needed for this (and to remake ATP and creatine phosphate and reload myoglobin with O₂) is the oxygen debt. That's why breathing rate and heart rate stay high for a while after exercise stops.

Worked example: explaining a lactate graph

A cyclist's blood lactate rises over the first 15 minutes of a ride, then stays roughly constant from 15 to 60 minutes.

The question asks about 15 to 60 minutes only. Lactate is still being made by anaerobic respiration, but it is being removed at the same rate (converted back to pyruvate, or taken to the liver), so its concentration stays about the same (WBI15 Oct 2024). Don't explain the first 15 minutes too.

  • Fate of lactate (any 2–3): "lactate diffuses into the blood"; "transported to the liver"; "converted / oxidised back to pyruvate" (ACCEPT dehydrogenation); "pyruvate then used in the link reaction / aerobic respiration"; "converted to glucose or glycogen in the liver".
  • Lactate must become pyruvate first. "Lactate used in the link reaction / Krebs cycle" is ignored.
  • "Aerobic respiration after the exercise repays the oxygen debt."
  • Lactate concentration rises, so blood pH falls.

Often asked as: describe what happens to lactate at the end of a race; explain lactate graphs (cyclists, diving seals); compare the relative changes in lactate and heart rate; explain why breathing stays fast after exercise.

  • Leaving out the liver.
  • Going down an "oxygen debt" route ("breathing removes lactate") instead of describing the conversion of lactate.
  • Explaining the whole graph when the question names a time window.
  • "An unfit person has more lactate." Compare rates: makes lactate sooner, or removes it more slowly.
  • Comparing two lines on different y-axes: use percentage change. Lactate rose by 308.5% while heart rate rose by only 22.5% (WBI15 Jan 2022).
  • Seals diving: muscle lactate falls at the end of a dive as it moves into the blood and to the liver (WBI15 Oct 2025, 6-mark).

Spec7.6Respiratory quotient (RQ)

Understand what is meant by the respiratory quotient (RQ).

Definition

RQ = volume of CO₂ produced ÷ volume of O₂ used (over the same time). Moles can replace volumes, since equal numbers of moles of gas take up equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure.

Typical values (questions usually supply them)

Respiratory substrateRQ
Carbohydrate (e.g. glucose)1.0
Proteinabout 0.8–0.9
Lipidabout 0.7
Mixture of carbohydrate and lipidbetween 0.7 and 1.0
Anaerobic respiration that releases CO₂ (yeast, plants)above 1

Why lipids give a lower RQ

  • Lipids contain proportionally more hydrogen and much less oxygen than carbohydrates.
  • More O₂ is needed to turn all that hydrogen into water, so more O₂ is used for every CO₂ made: RQ is below 1.
  • Glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O, so RQ = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0.

Equation for aerobic respiration of oleic acid: C18H34O2 + 25.5 O2 → 18 CO2 + 17 H2O + energy.

RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 used = 18 ÷ 25.5.

RQ = 0.7, which fits the value expected for a fat.

A fat needs far more oxygen per carbon than glucose, so less CO2 is released for each O2 used.

Reading RQ values

  • Above 1: some anaerobic respiration that releases CO₂ (yeast, plants), or carbohydrate being converted into fat. Lactate fermentation in animals releases no CO₂ itself.
  • Falling over time: the substrate is switching from carbohydrate to fat (e.g. a long cycle ride as glycogen stores run down).
  • Measuring RQ needs aerobic respiration: the CO₂ comes from the link reaction and Krebs cycle, which only keep going when oxygen is there to act as the final electron acceptor (WBI15 Jan 2026).

Worked example: RQ from an equation and from data

From an equation: 2C₁₈H₃₄O₂ + 51O₂ → 36CO₂ + 34H₂O.

RQ = 36 ÷ 51 = 0.71, so a lipid is being respired (WBI15 Jan 2024).

From measurements: a cockroach uses 1.7 cm³ O₂ h⁻¹ and produces 1.3 cm³ CO₂ h⁻¹.

RQ = 1.3 ÷ 1.7 = 0.76 → 0.8 to 1 d.p. (WBI15 Oct 2021). Likely protein or a mixture of substrates.

Working backwards: O₂ uptake is 2.20 dm³ min⁻¹ and the RQ is 0.88.

CO₂ produced = RQ × O₂ used = 0.88 × 2.20 = 1.94 dm³ min⁻¹ (WBI15 Jan 2023).

  • Definition (2 marks): "carbon dioxide produced ÷ oxygen used / consumed", as volumes or moles. "Amount" and "number of molecules" are ignored.
  • Trend with a mixture: "as the proportion of lipid increases, the RQ decreases", supported by a calculation from the table.
  • Cyclist: "RQ decreased" + "switching to lipid / fat rather than carbohydrate". Anaerobic respiration is ignored here.
  • Why oxygen is needed to calculate RQ: "CO₂ is produced in the link reaction / Krebs cycle" and "oxygen is the final electron acceptor / needed for oxidative phosphorylation".

Often asked as: calculate RQ from an equation or gas volumes; work out CO₂ from the RQ and O₂ uptake; identify the substrate; interpret a changing RQ (a cyclist, germinating barley over 30 days).

  • Inverting it (O₂ ÷ CO₂).
  • Describing the numbers but never naming the substrate.
  • Rounding too early, or giving too many decimal places.
  • "An RQ above 1 always means anaerobic respiration." It does only where anaerobic respiration releases CO₂ (yeast, plants); it can also mean fat is being made from carbohydrate.
  • Germinating barley over 30 days: carbohydrate is respired at first, lipid from about day 5 to day 10 (RQ falls), then the proportion of carbohydrate rises again. Describe each stage separately (WBI15 Jan 2026).
  • A falling RQ during exercise means a gradual change of substrate; few candidates said this (WBI15 Jan 2023).

Spec7.7Core Practical 15: a redox indicator and respiration in yeast

Use an artificial hydrogen carrier (a redox indicator) to investigate respiration in yeast.

Principle

  • Dehydrogenase enzymes in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle remove hydrogen from respiratory substrates. Normally NAD (or FAD) accepts it.
  • A redox indicator is an artificial hydrogen acceptor: it takes the hydrogen instead, and changes colour when it is reduced.
    • methylene blue: blue → colourless
    • DCPIP: blue → colourless
    • TTC (triphenyltetrazolium chloride): colourless → red
  • The faster the yeast respires, the faster the colour changes. Rate = 1 ÷ time for the colour change.

Core practical 15: effect of temperature on respiration in yeast

  1. Make a suspension of yeast in glucose solution (activated in warm glucose beforehand). Use the same volume and concentration in every tube.
  2. Put tubes of yeast suspension, and separate tubes of indicator (e.g. 1 cm³ of methylene blue), in thermostatically controlled water baths at a range of temperatures, e.g. 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 °C. Leave them 10 minutes to equilibrate.
  3. Add the indicator to the yeast, mix once by inverting, and start the timer.
  4. Don't shake again: oxygen from the air would re-oxidise the indicator. (A thin layer of oil on top keeps air out.)
  5. Stop the timer when the blue colour has gone, judged against a colour standard (yeast suspension with water in place of the indicator).
  6. Repeat three times at each temperature. Calculate the mean time and the rate (1 ÷ mean time).

Controls: boiled yeast + indicator (no colour change: shows living yeast and active enzymes are needed); yeast with no glucose (much slower).

Variables

  • Independent: temperature (or glucose concentration, or type of sugar).
  • Dependent: time taken for the indicator to change colour, converted to a rate (s⁻¹).
  • Control: volume and concentration of yeast suspension; volume and concentration of glucose; volume and concentration of indicator; pH (use a buffer); how the tube is mixed; same person judging the end point.

Worked example: rate from time

Mean time for methylene blue to lose its colour: 250 s at 25 °C and 125 s at 35 °C.

Rate at 25 °C = 1 ÷ 250 = 4.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Rate at 35 °C = 1 ÷ 125 = 8.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

The rate doubles for a 10 °C rise: molecules have more kinetic energy, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form. Above the optimum, enzymes denature (active sites change shape) and the rate falls.

Errors and improvements

  • The end point is subjective (yeast is cream-coloured) → use a colour standard and the same observer, or a colorimeter reading absorbance at set times.
  • Indicator re-oxidised by air → don't shake; oil layer.
  • Yeast settles → stir the stock suspension before taking each sample.
  • Safety: methylene blue stains (gloves); take care with hot water baths.
  • Applied version, the MTT assay (MTT turns yellow → purple when reduced): "use living / viable cells"; "reduced NAD is produced in respiration and reduces the dye"; "same concentration of each drug; same number of cells and conditions"; "record the time for the colour to change (or use a colorimeter)"; "the longer it takes to turn purple, the more toxic the drug" (WBI15 Jan 2022 article).
  • Planning language (from Unit 6): "range of five temperatures", "thermostatic water bath", "equilibrate yeast and indicator separately", "named indicator", "add glucose (respiratory substrate)", "repeats and means".

Often asked as: not set as a method question in WBI15 from 2020 to 2026; its logic appears in unfamiliar contexts such as the MTT toxicity assay. Unit 6 asks for the full plan.

  • Forgetting the glucose, or "thermostatically controlled" for the water bath. Shaking the tube while timing.
  • Reversing the conclusion: a drug that slows respiration slows the colour change, so a longer time means a more toxic drug. Few candidates mentioned using living cells.

Spec7.8Core Practical 16: respirometer, rate of respiration and RQ

Use a simple respirometer to find the rate of respiration and the RQ of a suitable material, such as germinating seeds or small invertebrates.

Principle

  • Organisms in a sealed tube take in O₂ and give out CO₂.
  • Soda lime (or potassium hydroxide) absorbs the CO₂, so the only change in gas volume is the O₂ taken in. The gas volume and pressure fall, and the coloured liquid in the capillary tube moves towards the organisms.
  • Volume of O₂ = distance moved × cross-sectional area of the capillary = πr²l (r = radius of the bore, l = distance moved).
  • A control tube contains glass beads of the same volume as the organisms (and the same soda lime). Any movement in it is caused by changes in temperature or pressure, so it can be used to correct the readings.

Core practical 16: rate of respiration and RQ

  1. Weigh the organisms (e.g. 2.0 g of germinating seeds, or woodlice or maggots). Put soda lime in the tube, a wire gauze above it, then the organisms on the gauze.
  2. Set up the control tube with glass beads of equal volume.
  3. Stand both tubes in a thermostatically controlled water bath (e.g. 25 °C) with the tap open for 10 minutes to equilibrate.
  4. Close the tap. Mark the liquid's position and measure the distance it moves in a set time (e.g. 5 minutes). Reset it with the syringe.
  5. Repeat at least three times and calculate the mean. Rate = πr²l ÷ time (÷ mass for a rate per gram).
  6. For the RQ: repeat with the same organisms, but with the soda lime replaced by the same volume of glass beads, so CO₂ isn't absorbed.

The second interactive diagram at the foot of the page lets you do both runs and work out the RQ.

Getting the RQ from the two runs

  • With soda lime, movement x = O₂ taken in.
  • Without soda lime, movement y = O₂ taken in − CO₂ given out (the net change).
  • So CO₂ given out = x − y, and RQ = (x − y) ÷ x.
  • If the liquid doesn't move without soda lime, CO₂ out = O₂ in, so RQ = 1 (carbohydrate).
  • If it moves away from the organisms without soda lime, more CO₂ is given out than O₂ taken in: add that distance instead of subtracting it (RQ above 1).

Worked example: maggots

Capillary radius 0.5 mm; 1.5 g of maggots; 25 °C; 5 minutes per reading.

With soda lime, the liquid moves 25 mm towards the maggots. Without soda lime, it moves 5 mm towards them.

O₂ used = πr²l = 3.14 × 0.5² × 25 = 19.6 mm³ in 5 min → 19.6 ÷ 5 = 3.9 mm³ min⁻¹ → 3.9 ÷ 1.5 = 2.6 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.

CO₂ produced = 25 − 5 = 20 mm of capillary. RQ = 20 ÷ 25 = 0.80, so protein (or a mixture of substrates) is being respired.

The same capillary is used for both runs, so πr² cancels in the RQ and you can use the distances directly.

Control variables, safety and errors

  • Control: mass and type of organism; temperature; volume of soda lime; capillary bore; equilibration time; age of the seeds or stage of the larvae.
  • Safety and ethics: soda lime and KOH are corrosive (gloves, eye protection); the gauze stops organisms touching them; handle invertebrates gently, keep runs short and return them to their habitat.
  • Errors: leaks at the bung (seal with petroleum jelly); temperature changes (water bath and control tube); soda lime becoming saturated (use fresh); small movements are hard to read (finer capillary, longer time, or a gas syringe or pressure sensor); seeds may switch to anaerobic respiration as O₂ runs low (keep runs short).
  • "CO₂ absorbed by soda lime / KOH, so movement shows O₂ uptake". Sodium hydrogencarbonate is not a CO₂ absorber.
  • "Control tube with glass beads of the same volume" corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Glass beads replace soda lime in the second run "so carbon dioxide is not absorbed" (and the volume stays the same).
  • RQ = "volume of CO₂ produced ÷ volume of O₂ used".
  • Rate answers need the unit conversions done (cm³ to dm³, minutes to hours) and the s.f. or standard form asked for.

Often asked as: not set as a method question in WBI15 from 2020 to 2026; instead, RQ and rate calculations from given values (WBI15 Oct 2021, Jan 2023, Jan 2026). The full plan is a favourite in Unit 6.

  • Saying a respirometer measures CO₂ output. It measures O₂ uptake; the CO₂ comes from the difference between the two runs.
  • Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
  • A control tube with soda lime only. It needs inert glass beads of the same volume as the organisms.

Quick checks

In which part of the cell does the Krebs cycle take place?

The Krebs cycle runs in the matrix. The cytoplasm is where glycolysis happens, which is the commonest mix-up. The cristae hold the electron transport chain, not the cycle.

What is the net number of ATP produced by glycolysis per glucose molecule?

Glycolysis makes 4 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation but uses 2 at the start to phosphorylate glucose, so the net gain is 2. Quoting 4 is the common error of forgetting the ATP spent.

Why does the electron transport chain stop when oxygen is absent?

Oxygen accepts electrons at the end of the chain and combines with H+ to form water. Without it, the carriers stay reduced and reduced NAD cannot be reoxidised, so the chain and chemiosmosis stop.

Why is lactate formed in muscle during anaerobic respiration?

Pyruvate accepts hydrogen from reduced NAD, which regenerates NAD+ for glycolysis. Lactate itself makes no extra ATP, so the first option is wrong.
