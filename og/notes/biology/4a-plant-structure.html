Plant structure and function

Plants are built differently from animals: their cells sit inside walls of cellulose, they store energy as starch, and they move water and food through specialised tissues. This page covers plant cell ultrastructure, starch and cellulose, the tissues of the stem and the fibres people make from them, the water and mineral ions plants need, and how drugs from plants are discovered and tested.

Spec4.1Plant cell structure and how it compares with animal cells

(i) Know the structure and ultrastructure of a plant cell (cell wall, chloroplast, amyloplast, vacuole, tonoplast, plasmodesmata, pits, middle lamella) and compare it with an animal cell. (ii) Know what each of these structures does.

What plant and animal cells share

Both are eukaryotic cells, so both have a nucleus with a nucleolus, rough and smooth ER, a Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm and a cell surface membrane (Topic 3). Plant cells add the structures below.

For "compare" questions, write paired sentences that name both cells: "a plant cell has a cellulose cell wall whereas an animal cell has none". A list of features with no cell named isn't credited.

  • Animal cells only: "centrioles", "glycogen granules", "cilia", "flagella"; lysosomes accepted. Vesicles and temporary vacuoles are ignored.
  • Chloroplast: "(site of) photosynthesis" / "converts light energy to chemical energy". "Makes food" is ignored.
  • Starch / amyloplast: "store of {glucose / energy}". "Produces energy" and "stores carbohydrates" are ignored.
  • Vacuole: "provides support (turgor)", "stores" + a named substance (water, sugars, mineral ions, pigments, enzymes). "Cell sap" alone and "gives the cell shape" are ignored.
  • Plasmodesmata: "connect cells / allow communication between cells", "signalling substances pass through the cytoplasm (symplast)", or named molecules passing through.
  • Pits: "allow (lateral) movement of water / mineral ions" between adjoining cells or out of the xylem.
  • Walls with secondary thickening compared with walls without: "thicker / more layers", "contain lignin", "stronger / less flexible", "waterproof / impermeable".
  • Drawings: amyloplasts and chloroplasts drawn in the cytoplasm and smaller than the nucleus; the tonoplast drawn as a single membrane, with the label line touching the membrane, not the middle of the vacuole.

Often asked as: MCQ on which structure stores starch (very common), name two structures found in animal but not plant cells, state the function of a named structure, draw and label amyloplast / chloroplast / tonoplast, compare a plant cell with a fungal cell.

  • Saying plant cells have no Golgi apparatus: they do (WBI12 Oct 2024 report).
  • Putting chloroplasts in a root cell. Roots are underground, so their cells have none.
  • Writing "cell sap" or "nutrients" as the vacuole's contents, or "nutrients" for what passes through plasmodesmata. Name the substances.
  • Mixing up pits (thin areas of the wall) with plasmodesmata (cytoplasmic channels through the wall).
  • Calling the vacuole the tonoplast. The tonoplast is the membrane around it.
  • Fungal cells compared with plant cells: fungal walls are made of chitin, not cellulose; fungi store glycogen, not starch; they have no chloroplasts and no plasmodesmata (WBI12 Jan 2021).
  • How a plant cell gets bigger after division: it takes up water into the vacuole and makes more cytoplasm, organelles and proteins, plus new membrane and wall. Describing division instead loses the marks (WBI12 Oct 2021).
  • Sphere and hemisphere volume in plant contexts (4/3πr³, halved for a bowl shape) (WBI12 Jun 2022).

Worked example: actual size from a micrograph

A chloroplast measures 42 mm long on a micrograph at × 6000.

Convert to µm: 42 mm × 1000 = 42 000 µm.

Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 42 000 ÷ 6000 = 7.0 µm.

Spec4.3Starch and cellulose

Know the structure and function of starch and cellulose, including how hydrogen bonds between β-glucose molecules form cellulose microfibrils.

Starch: the plant's energy store

  • Starch is a mixture of two polymers of α-glucose (Unit 1, 1.2):
    • Amylose: 1,4-glycosidic bonds only, so an unbranched chain that coils into a helix. Very compact.
    • Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds and 1,6-glycosidic bonds at the branch points, so branched.
  • It is stored as grains in amyloplasts and in chloroplasts.
  • Why it is a good store, as feature → consequence pairs:
    • Made of glucose → hydrolysed to glucose for respiration.
    • Compact (coiled, branched) → a lot of glucose stored in a small space.
    • Insoluble → doesn't affect the water potential of the cell, so no osmotic water uptake; and it can't diffuse out.
    • Large → can't cross the membrane, so it stays in the cell.
    • Branched (amylopectin) → many chain ends where enzymes can work at once → rapid hydrolysis to glucose.

Cellulose: the structural polysaccharide

  • Cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose. In β-glucose the –OH on carbon 1 points up (above the ring); in α-glucose it points down.
  • To join carbon 1 of one β-glucose to carbon 4 of the next by a 1,4-glycosidic bond, every other molecule is turned over (rotated 180°).
  • That flipping makes each chain long, straight and unbranched, with –OH groups sticking out on both sides.
  • The –OH groups of neighbouring parallel chains form hydrogen bonds with each other (δ+ H of one –OH attracted to the δ− O of another). One hydrogen bond is weak, but there are huge numbers of them, so together they hold the chains firmly side by side.
  • Bundles of many chains held together like this form a microfibril. Microfibrils are very strong in tension (high tensile strength), insoluble, and hard to break down.
  • In the cell wall, microfibrils lie in a matrix of other polysaccharides and are laid down in layers (4.4).

  • Writing about pectin, hemicellulose or lignin when the question asks only about cellulose and microfibrils.

  • Cellulose is in the walls of xylem, phloem and sclerenchyma (WBI12 Oct 2020).
  • Picking out the branched (amylopectin) drawing and naming the 1,6 bond (WBI12 Jun 2023).

Spec4.4Microfibrils, secondary thickening and the strength of plant fibres

Explain how the way cellulose microfibrils are arranged, plus secondary thickening, gives xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres the physical properties that make plant fibres useful to humans.

How a cell wall is built

  • Primary wall: laid down first, while the cell is still growing. Thin and flexible; microfibrils form a loose mesh in a matrix of pectins and hemicelluloses, so the wall can stretch.
  • Secondary wall: added inside the primary wall once the cell stops growing (only in some cells, e.g. xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres). Microfibrils are laid down in several layers, and the microfibrils in each layer run at a different angle from the layer next to it.
  • Secondary thickening = this extra secondary wall, usually impregnated with lignin.
  • Lignin is a complex polymer that fills the spaces between microfibrils. It makes the wall rigid (resists compression and stops the cell collapsing) and waterproof (impermeable). The cell contents die, leaving a hollow tube.
  • Where lignin is missing, pits remain, so water can still move sideways.
  • In xylem, lignin is often laid down as rings, spirals or a net. This lets young xylem stretch as the stem grows and the stem bend without the tube collapsing.

  • Xylem wall properties: "cellulose molecules organised into microfibrils"; "layers of microfibrils at different angles / a mesh"; "{hydrogen bonds / lignin / secondary thickening} give {strength / support}"; "microfibrils held together by {pectin / pectate}"; gaps let water and ions through.
  • Lignin: "makes the wall impermeable to water (waterproof)", "provides strength", laid down as rings or spirals.
  • Xylem for support: "walls have secondary thickening / lignin" + strength. For transport: "hollow tubes with no {end walls / cytoplasm}" + "no obstruction to the water column".
  • Climbing plant v sunflower: the climber "is supported by the pole" and "needs to be flexible to bend round it", so needs less lignin; the sunflower must hold itself up.

Often asked as: explain the physical properties of a xylem vessel wall; role of secondary thickening in sclerenchyma; why a fibre such as jute is strong; interpret cellulose and lignin data (bamboo with age).

  • Leaving out "lignified walls / secondary thickening" in support answers (WBI12 Jan 2020 report).
  • Writing "hollow" without saying "no end walls / no cytoplasm".
  • Giving functions when physical properties are asked (WBI12 Oct 2023), or treating lignin as something that carries water rather than giving strength and waterproofing (WBI12 Jan 2024).
  • Saying cellulose fibres resist compression. Cellulose gives tensile strength; lignin resists compression.
  • Saying lignin strengthens the cell membrane. It is in the wall (WBI12 Oct 2019).
  • Bamboo: as the stem ages, the % cellulose falls and the % lignin rises; ratio cellulose : lignin about 2 : 1 at one year (WBI12 Jun 2025).
  • Jute has high tensile strength because of its high cellulose and lignin content, and because its fibres are twisted or woven (WBI12 Oct 2025).
  • Tracheids (long xylem cells with tapering ends) are accepted as support cells like sclerenchyma (WBI12 Jun 2021).
  • Capillary action in the narrow xylem lumen helps water rise (WBI12 Jan 2020).

Spec4.5Sclerenchyma, xylem and phloem

Know how sclerenchyma fibres (support), xylem vessels (support, and transport of water and mineral ions) and phloem (translocation of organic solutes) are alike and different in structure, where they sit in the stem, and what they do.

Xylem vessels

  • Formed from a column of cells joined end to end. Their end walls break down and their contents die, leaving a continuous hollow tube with no cytoplasm.
  • Walls are lignified (secondary thickening in rings, spirals or a net) → support, waterproof, don't collapse under tension.
  • Pits in the walls let water move sideways between vessels.
  • Carry water and mineral ions from the roots up to the leaves (one direction) in the transpiration stream; also support the plant.

Phloem

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells joined end to end. The end walls are perforated to form sieve plates. Each has no nucleus, very few organelles and only a thin layer of cytoplasm, so sap can flow through.
  • Walls are cellulose only, not lignified.
  • Companion cells lie beside each sieve tube element, linked to it by plasmodesmata. They have a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria: they supply the ATP for active loading of sucrose and keep the sieve tube element alive.
  • Phloem carries organic solutes, mainly sucrose and amino acids, from sources (e.g. leaves) to sinks (growing points, roots, storage organs, fruits). This is translocation, and it goes both up and down the plant.

Sclerenchyma fibres

  • Long, narrow cells with tapering, closed ends (end walls), overlapping in bundles.
  • Very thick lignified secondary walls, a small empty lumen, some simple pits. Dead when mature.
  • Support only: they don't transport anything.

Similarities to remember: all three are long, narrow cells with cellulose walls. Xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres are both dead, hollow, lignified and give support, and both have pits. Xylem and phloem are both tubes for transport in the vascular bundles, and neither a mature xylem vessel nor a sieve tube element has a nucleus.

Where they are

  • Stem: vascular bundles in a ring near the outside. In each bundle, from outside to inside: sclerenchyma → phloem → cambium → xylem. Pith in the centre.
  • Root: xylem in the centre, often star-shaped, with phloem in the gaps between its arms (all inside the endodermis). A central core resists pulling forces as the root anchors the plant.
  • Leaf: in each vein and the midrib, xylem towards the upper surface and phloem towards the lower surface.
  • Phloem: "{transport / translocation} of {sucrose / amino acids}" "from {source / leaves} to {sink / site of use or storage}". "Glucose", "nutrients" and "sugar" are ignored.
  • Xylem: "transport of water and mineral ions" and support. Sclerenchyma: "support" ("strength" and "rigidity" alone are ignored).
  • Xylem v sclerenchyma: similarities "both contain {cellulose / pits / lignin / dead cells}", "both hollow / no cytoplasm". Difference: "sclerenchyma has end walls (closed, pointed ends) whereas xylem does not".
  • Phloem v xylem (structure): similarities "both have cellulose walls", "both tubular", "both have no nucleus". Differences: "phloem has sieve plates", "phloem contains cytoplasm, so not hollow", "phloem has no lignin / secondary thickening", "phloem has plasmodesmata whereas xylem has pits"; living v dead accepted.
  • Phloem adapted: "continuous tube / sieve plates let the solution flow", "plasmodesmata", "companion cells provide ATP for active loading of sucrose".
  • Root v stem: "in the stem xylem and phloem are in separate vascular bundles arranged in a ring; in the root they are in a central group". Use comparative language and say which tissue.

Often asked as: label a stem section (sclerenchyma, phloem, cambium, xylem); compare xylem with sclerenchyma or with phloem; describe tissue positions in root, stem and leaf; how phloem structure suits its job; dye taken up by a celery stalk; a parasitic plant or a millipede feeding from the vascular tissue.

  • Swapping sclerenchyma and phloem when labelling, or letting a label line touch the wrong tissue (WBI12 Jan 2023, Jan 2020 reports).
  • Thinking both xylem and phloem are lignified, or that xylem has end walls or sieve plates (WBI12 Oct 2019, Jun 2022 reports).
  • Writing "glucose" or "nutrients" for what phloem carries. It is sucrose (and amino acids).
  • Giving functions when the question asks for structure (WBI12 Jun 2022 report).
  • Phloem moves sap both up and down; xylem moves water upwards only (WBI12 Jan 2021).
  • Calcium ions travel up the plant in the xylem (WBI12 Jan 2021).
  • A parasitic plant grows into the host's phloem and absorbs sucrose by diffusion, facilitated diffusion or active transport (not osmosis) (WBI12 Jun 2023).
  • A dye placed in water travels up a celery stalk in the xylem, so only the xylem is stained (WBI12 Jun 2023).
  • Recognising parenchyma (thin-walled living packing cells) in a stem section (WBI12 Jan 2021).

Spec4.6Core Practical 7: plan diagrams and cell drawings of plant tissues

Use a light microscope to observe transverse sections of roots, stems and leaves; draw and label plan diagrams of them; draw and label cells of plant tissues; and identify sclerenchyma fibres, phloem, sieve tubes and xylem vessels and where they are.

Core practical 7: method in brief

  1. Wet a razor blade or scalpel and cut very thin transverse sections of a stem (or root) on a white tile, cutting away from your fingers. For a leaf, hold it between two pieces of polystyrene or carrot and shave thin slices.
  2. Move the sections with a soft paintbrush into water in a watch glass. Choose the thinnest one: a thin partial section is better than a thick whole one (thick sections let little light through and cells overlap).
  3. Put it on a slide and add a drop or two of stain, e.g. toluidine blue O for 1–2 minutes, then rinse or blot off the excess.
  4. Lower a coverslip at an angle from one side to avoid air bubbles.
  5. Focus on low power first and draw a plan diagram of the tissue layout. Then switch to high power and draw a few cells of one tissue.
  6. Use an eyepiece graticule calibrated with a stage micrometer to measure sizes, and add a magnification or scale.

Prepared permanent slides are an acceptable alternative.

Cut several very thin transverse sections with a sharp scalpel or razor blade, cutting away from your fingers, and keep them in water.

Thin sections are only one or two cells thick, so light passes through and the cells do not overlap. Water stops them drying out.

Stain the sections, for example with toluidine blue O, which turns lignified walls a green-blue and other walls a pink-purple, or with phloroglucinol and acid, which stains lignin red.

Staining lignin picks out the xylem and sclerenchyma, the two tissues with thick lignified walls.

Rinse, mount the thinnest section in a drop of water under a coverslip, and find a vascular bundle at low power.

Identify the tissues by their position and appearance: large empty lignified vessels on the inner side (xylem), small thin-walled cells outside them (phloem), and a group of small, very thick-walled lignified cells at the outer edge (sclerenchyma).

Draw a low-power plan showing the outlines of the tissues only, with no individual cells, using clear continuous lines and ruled label lines. State the magnification.

Stains and what they show

  • Toluidine blue O stains lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) blue-green and unlignified walls (phloem, parenchyma) pink-purple. Staining adds contrast, because thin unstained sections absorb very little light.
  • (Other stains you may meet: phloroglucinol with acid stains lignin pink-red; iodine stains starch grains blue-black.)

Identifying the tissues

  • Xylem vessels: the largest, rounded, empty cells with thick (lignified) walls, on the inner side of each bundle in a stem and in the centre of a root.
  • Sclerenchyma fibres: small cells with very thick walls and tiny lumens, in a cap on the outside of each stem bundle.
  • Phloem: a patch of small, thin-walled cells between the xylem and the sclerenchyma; in high power you may see sieve plates and small, densely stained companion cells.

Drawing rules (both kinds of drawing)

  • Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading or colouring.
  • Draw large (use at least half the space) and keep the proportions right.
  • Plan diagrams: tissue boundaries only, no cells.
  • Label lines drawn with a ruler, ending exactly on the structure, not crossing each other, no arrowheads. Labels outside the drawing.
  • Add a title and the magnification (or a scale bar).
  • Draw what you see, not what the textbook shows.

Safety

  • Razor blades and scalpels: cut away from your fingers onto a tile; hand back or put in a sharps container.
  • Toluidine blue stains skin and clothing: gloves, wipe up spills.
  • Never point a microscope mirror at direct sunlight.

Worked example: calibrating a graticule

Eyepiece graticule: 40 divisions line up with 0.1 mm (100 µm) on the stage micrometer.

One division = 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 µm at this objective.

A xylem vessel spans 18 divisions: diameter = 18 × 2.5 = 45 µm.

Recalibrate if you change the objective lens.

  • Label lines must not touch the cambium line or the wrong tissue (WBI12 Jan 2020 report).
  • A labelled diagram of the stem is accepted as part of an answer comparing root and stem arrangements.

Often asked as: the full plan diagram has not been set in WBI12 papers from 2019 to 2026, but its skills have: labelling xylem, phloem and sclerenchyma on a stem micrograph, comparing tissue arrangement in a root and a stem, and a stained celery section.

  • Drawing individual cells in a plan diagram, or shading.
  • Few candidates knew where the tissues are in a root or a stem (WBI12 Oct 2021 report): learn the stem order (sclerenchyma, phloem, cambium, xylem) and the central root core.
  • Air bubbles mistaken for structures: lower the coverslip at an angle.

Spec4.7Plant fibres, starch and sustainability

Explain how using plant fibres and starch can help sustainability, including plant-based products that replace oil-based plastics.

What "sustainable" means

A resource is used sustainably if we can meet today's needs without using it up, so it is still available to future generations. Plants are renewable: more can be grown. Crude oil is non-renewable (finite): once used, it is gone.

Plant products that can replace oil-based ones

  • Plant fibres (hemp, jute, flax, sisal, cotton) for rope, cloth, sacks and building materials, instead of nylon and polyester made from oil.
  • Bioplastics made from starch (e.g. from maize, potatoes, cassava) or cellulose (wood pulp), instead of oil-based plastics such as polythene and PVC. Examples: thermoplastic starch, PLA (polylactic acid, made by fermenting sugars from maize starch or sugarcane), cellulose films.
  • Bioethanol made by fermenting sugars from starch or sugarcane, used as fuel instead of petrol.
  • Waste plant material (sugarcane bagasse, wheat bran, seed husks, avocado seeds) can be turned into plates, cutlery or packaging.

The drawbacks (needed for "discuss" or "evaluate")

  • Crops for fibres, plastics and fuel compete with food crops for land, water and fertiliser.
  • Growing them needs land (risk of deforestation, monocultures that lower biodiversity), fertilisers (eutrophication) and pesticides, and machinery that burns fuel, so they are not fully carbon neutral.
  • Bioplastics may be weaker, less water-resistant or more expensive. Many only break down in industrial compost, not in soil or the sea.
  • Plant ropes are often weaker than synthetic ones and can rot.

Every "more sustainable" point needs a reason: "renewable, so more can be grown and it won't run out for future generations"; "biodegradable, so decomposers break it down"; "carbon neutral, so no net increase in atmospheric CO₂". The word "sustainable" on its own scores nothing.

  • Sustainable: "renewable / more can be grown / will not run out / available to future generations"; "biodegradable / broken down by decomposers"; "carbon neutral".
  • Oil-based not sustainable: "non-renewable (finite)", "takes a long time to decompose", "adds to CO₂ / not carbon neutral".
  • Ignored: "recyclable", "less pollution" with no detail, "sustainable" with no explanation, "more easily broken down" with no detail. "Not recyclable" is not credited for oil-based plastics.
  • Packaging with antimicrobial plant extracts (e.g. oregano): "antimicrobial property reduces bacteria on the food", so a longer shelf life and fewer food-poisoning cases.

Often asked as: explain why a named plant product (hemp, wheat bran, avocado seed, a calabash bowl, wood packaging) is more sustainable than an oil-based one; a 6-mark comparison of bioethanol crops; percentage calculations from product data.

  • Giving "more sustainable" with no reason (WBI12 Jun 2023 report), or leaving out the future generations idea (WBI12 Oct 2019 report).
  • Writing "less pollution" or "recyclable". Neither earns a mark.
  • In a 6-mark evaluation, listing pros and cons without linking them to sustainability (capped at Level 1).
  • Bioethanol crops 6-mark: compare water, fertiliser and pesticide needs and CO₂ output of crops such as hemp, rapeseed and soybean; consider the farmer, the population and the ecosystem; reach a supported conclusion (WBI12 Oct 2019).
  • Starch bioplastic strength: more starch → higher tensile strength (more hydrogen bonds between starch molecules); more glycerol (a plasticiser) → lower tensile strength (WBI12 Jan 2026).
  • Percentage of a percentage: e.g. 14% of 48 mg, then 43% of that answer, to 2 s.f. (WBI12 Oct 2020).

Spec4.8Water and inorganic ions in plants

Explain why plants need water and the inorganic ions nitrate, calcium and magnesium.

Roots take in water by osmosis and mineral ions mostly by active transport (using ATP) from the soil. Both travel up the plant in the xylem.

Water

  • Reactant in photosynthesis (split in the light-dependent stage) and in hydrolysis reactions (e.g. starch → glucose).
  • Solvent: substances dissolve in it so they can react and be transported (mineral ions in the xylem, sucrose in the phloem).
  • Turgor: water entering vacuoles by osmosis makes cells turgid, which supports non-woody plants and leaves. Without enough water, cells become flaccid and the plant wilts. Turgor also opens stomata (guard cells).
  • Cooling: evaporation of water from leaves (transpiration) cools them.

Inorganic ions

IonUsed forDeficiency symptoms
Nitrate (NO₃⁻)source of nitrogen to make amino acids → proteins (enzymes, membrane proteins), nucleotides → DNA, RNA, ATP, NADP, and chlorophyllstunted growth; leaves turn yellow (chlorosis), starting with the older leaves
Calcium (Ca²⁺)forms calcium pectate in the middle lamella, which holds neighbouring cells together, and in the wall matrix; also needed for membrane functiongrowing tips and young leaves die back or are deformed; weak walls; soft fruit
Magnesium (Mg²⁺)part of every chlorophyll molecule, so needed for light absorption and photosynthesis; also activates some enzymes (ATP works bound to Mg²⁺)yellowing between the veins, starting with older leaves; less photosynthesis, so slower growth

Older leaves show nitrogen and magnesium shortage first because the plant moves these ions out of old leaves into new growth. Calcium can't be moved once it is in a wall, so shortage shows in the youngest tissue.

Follow the chain to the end: "magnesium → chlorophyll → light absorbed → photosynthesis → glucose made → growth". "Magnesium is needed for photosynthesis" stops too early, and "calcium pectate" with no job scores nothing. Say what it is for: "calcium pectate holds cells together in the middle lamella".

  2. Keep everything else the same: volume of solution, light, temperature, aeration of the solution, time.
  3. Cover containers so that algae don't grow, and top up with distilled water.
  4. After a few weeks, record leaf colour, number of leaves, shoot length, and dry mass (dry at 80–100 °C to constant mass) or chlorophyll content.
  5. Use several plants per solution and compare means (with error bars or standard deviation).

Set up identical containers of mineral solution: one complete, and others each lacking one ion (no nitrate, no calcium, no magnesium). A container of distilled water can be added for comparison.

The complete solution is the control: any difference in the other plants must be caused by the missing ion.

Spec4.9Core Practical 8: tensile strength of plant fibres

Measure the tensile strength of plant fibres.

Principle

Tensile strength is the stretching force a material can withstand before it breaks, per unit cross-sectional area:

tensile strength = breaking force (N) ÷ cross-sectional area (m²)

Units: N m⁻² (= Pa), or N mm⁻². A hanging mass gives a force: force (N) = mass (kg) × 9.81. Dividing by area lets you compare thick and thin fibres fairly.

Core practical 8: method in brief

  1. Extract fibres (e.g. from celery, nettle, flax or banana stems) by scraping or by retting (soaking so microbes break down the soft tissue). Choose fibres with no visible damage.
  2. Cut each fibre to the same length (e.g. 10 cm). Measure its diameter at several points with a micrometer or a microscope with a graticule, and take the mean.
  3. Clamp the top of the fibre to a retort stand (or tie it round a bar), and hang a mass hanger from the bottom. Put a tray with padding underneath.
  4. Add masses one at a time (e.g. 10 g), waiting a few seconds after each, until the fibre breaks. Record the total mass.
  5. Repeat with at least three fibres of each type and calculate the mean breaking mass.
  6. Convert to force (× 9.81, with mass in kg) and divide by the cross-sectional area (πr²) to get tensile strength.

Extract fibres from the plant, for example by retting nettle or flax stems, or by pulling the vascular strands out of a celery stalk. Use fibres of the same length.

Clamp one end of the fibre firmly to a stand and tie a mass hanger to the other end. Place a tray of sand or a cushion underneath, and wear eye protection.

When the fibre snaps the masses fall; the tray protects feet and bench, and goggles protect against a whipping fibre.

Add masses one at a time, gently and without dropping them, until the fibre breaks. Record the total mass, including the hanger, at the moment it breaks.

Dropping a mass on would add a sudden extra force and give a falsely low value.

Repeat with several fibres of the same type and calculate a mean. Then repeat with other types of fibre.

Natural fibres vary a lot, so repeats are needed to give a reliable mean and show the spread.

Convert to force: F = m × g, with m in kg and g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Measure the fibre's diameter with a micrometer or calibrated eyepiece graticule, find the area A = πr², and calculate tensile strength = F ÷ A.

Variables

  • Independent: type of fibre (or plant species, age, treatment).
  • Dependent: breaking mass / force, then tensile strength.
  • Control: fibre length, diameter (or correct for it with area), the part of the plant it came from, its age and how fresh or dry it is, the size of each mass added, the time between additions.

Safety

Wear eye protection (a snapping fibre can whip up). Keep feet clear of falling masses and use the padded tray. Take care with knives or scalpels when extracting fibres.

Worked example: tensile strength from breaking mass

A fibre breaks when the total mass is 450 g. Its mean diameter is 0.80 mm.

Force = 0.450 kg × 9.81 = 4.41 N.

Radius = 0.80 ÷ 2 = 0.40 mm = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m.

Area = πr² = π × (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)² = 5.03 × 10⁻⁷ m².

Tensile strength = 4.41 ÷ 5.03 × 10⁻⁷ = 8.8 × 10⁶ N m⁻² (2 s.f.).

Use the radius, not the diameter, and convert mm to m before squaring.

A fibre of diameter 0.10 mm breaks when the total mass is 400 g.

Force: F = 0.400 kg × 9.81 N kg⁻¹ = 3.92 N.

Area: r = 0.05 mm = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ m, so A = π × (5.0 × 10⁻⁵)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁹ m².

Tensile strength = 3.92 ÷ 7.85 × 10⁻⁹ = 5.0 × 10⁸ Pa = 500 MPa.

Dividing by area lets fibres of different thickness be compared fairly: a thick strand can hold more mass yet still be a weaker material.

The simulation at the bottom of this page lets you run the practical. The breaking loads are simulated but behave like real fibres, which vary from one sample to the next.

Errors and improvements

  • Fibre slips in the clamp or breaks at the clamp → wrap the end or tie it round a smooth bar; ignore results where it breaks at the clamp.
  • Mass steps too large → the true breaking load lies between two values; use smaller masses near the expected break.
  • Masses added with a jerk add extra force → lower them gently.
  • Fibres vary in diameter and have weak spots → measure diameter at several points, use more fibres, calculate means.
  • Fibres dry out over time → test them all fresh, or all at the same time after extraction.
  • Reporting the breaking mass as "tensile strength". It is only a breaking load until you convert to force and divide by area. Longer celery strings may seem "stronger" simply because they were thicker.
  • Assuming celery "strings" are xylem. They are mainly strands of thick-walled supporting cells (collenchyma) running alongside the vascular bundles, so a dye test that stains the xylem doesn't show that the whole string is xylem.

Not examined as a practical in WBI12 papers from 2019 to 2026. Tensile strength appears in explanations instead: why a fibre is strong (cellulose, hydrogen bonds, microfibrils at different angles, lignin), bioplastic strength data (starch and glycerol), and water stress reducing cellulose and therefore wall strength.

Often asked as: link tensile strength to microfibril structure; interpret strength data with error bars.

Spec4.10Conditions bacteria need to grow

Know what conditions bacteria need for growth.

Bacteria reproduce by binary fission; in good conditions some divide every 20–30 minutes. Each condition below matters because it affects the enzymes and the processes the cell needs to grow and divide.

ConditionWhy it is needed
Nutrientsa carbon source (e.g. glucose) for respiration and to build molecules; a nitrogen source (amino acids, nitrate) to make proteins and nucleic acids; mineral ions (e.g. phosphate for DNA and ATP). Agar or broth provides these.
Watersolvent for reactions and transport; needed for hydrolysis reactions; bacteria in a dry place stop growing.
Suitable temperaturean optimum temperature for their enzymes. Too cold → slow reactions (little kinetic energy); too hot → enzymes denature.
Suitable pHeach bacterium's enzymes have an optimum pH; far from it the active sites change shape (ionic and hydrogen bonds disrupted).

  • Photosynthetic bacteria (e.g. cyanobacteria) need light, CO₂ and mineral ions instead of a supply of glucose.
  • In school labs cultures are grown at 25 °C or below, not 37 °C, so that bacteria which grow well at body temperature (likely human pathogens) are not encouraged.
  • Each condition needs a reason: "oxygen for (aerobic) respiration" (or "no oxygen" for obligate anaerobes); "glucose for respiration / amino acids for protein synthesis"; "optimum temperature for {enzyme / metabolic} reactions" (or "so enzymes are not denatured"); "optimum pH for … reactions"; "water for {hydrolysis / as a solvent}".
  • Photosynthetic bacterium: mineral ions (nitrate, phosphate), pH, light. Glucose, temperature, "nutrients" and "moisture" are ignored there.
  • Oxygen graph: "as oxygen increases the growth rate of A decreases whereas B increases"; "B is an (obligate) aerobe"; "B levels off because another factor is limiting"; "high oxygen inhibits A (an anaerobe)".

Often asked as: explain the conditions needed (4 marks); describe and explain growth-rate graphs against oxygen, salt (NaCl) concentration or temperature; bacterial survival on wooden and plastic chopping boards.

  • Listing conditions with no reasons: the commonest reason marks are lost (WBI12 Oct 2020, Jan 2021, Jun 2023 reports).
  • Saying high temperature "denatures the bacteria". It denatures their enzymes (WBI12 Jan 2021 report).
  • Giving plant growth conditions, or glucose for a photosynthetic bacterium.
  • Describing each bar separately instead of giving the overall trend first (WBI12 Oct 2022 report).
  • Thermophiles have heat-stable enzymes with a high optimum temperature; psychrophiles grow best in the cold (WBI12 Jan 2023).
  • High salt lowers the water potential outside the cell, so water leaves the bacterium by osmosis and growth slows (WBI12 Jun 2022).

Spec4.11Plants as sources of antimicrobial and other medicinal substances

Know that substances made by plants can have antimicrobial and other therapeutic (medicinal) properties.

Why plants make these chemicals

Plants can't move away from pathogens or herbivores. Many make chemicals that kill or slow the growth of microorganisms (antimicrobials: antibacterial and antifungal) or deter animals (e.g. bitter tannins). Humans can extract, purify and use these, or copy their structure.

Examples

From plant to medicine

  1. Find a plant used in traditional medicine or one that resists disease.
  2. Extract the active substance and purify it, so the dose can be measured and controlled.
  3. Work out its structure, so it can be synthesised in a lab: no need to harvest huge amounts of a wild plant, and chemists can modify it to work better or have fewer side effects.
  4. Test it for safety and effectiveness (4.13).

This is one reason to conserve biodiversity: plants not yet studied, especially endemic ones in threatened habitats, may contain useful drugs.

  • Zone of inhibition data: "extract {was effective / had antimicrobial properties} against all three bacteria"; "prevented growth of / killed bacteria around the disc"; "higher concentration → larger zone".
  • Comparing two extracts: say which is better against which bacterium, then whether the SDs or error bars overlap (no significant difference) or not, then a method point (control disc, same volume and concentration of extract).
  • Largest possible difference between two zones: upper limit of the bigger (mean + range or SD) minus lower limit of the smaller.
  • A gel with no extract used in a wound trial acts as a control (like a placebo) for comparison. This is a control, not a controlled variable.

Often asked as: interpret zone-of-inhibition data for plant extracts; 6-mark data on ulcer treatments with antimicrobial plant extracts; wound-healing trials; calculate a standard deviation from zone diameters.

  • Calling penicillin a plant product. It comes from a fungus.
  • Confusing a control (a group or disc without the active substance) with a controlled variable (WBI12 Jun 2023 report).
  • Saying a standard deviation "proves reliability", or that overlapping SDs show "accuracy". Overlap means no significant difference (WBI12 Jun 2023, Jan 2024 reports).
  • Standard deviation by hand, s = √(Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)) (formula given). SD shows how spread out the values are about the mean, not a correlation (WBI12 Oct 2024).
  • Named plant chemicals in stems: tannins (bitter, deter herbivores), flavonoids (antibacterial), fucoxanthin from seaweed (WBI12 Jun 2021, Jan 2022).

Spec4.12Core Practical 9: antimicrobial properties of plants and aseptic technique

Investigate whether plant extracts have antimicrobial properties, using aseptic techniques to handle bacteria safely.

Principle

Paper discs soaked in a plant extract are placed on agar covered evenly with bacteria. If the extract kills bacteria or stops them growing, it diffuses into the agar and leaves a clear zone (zone of inhibition) with no growth around the disc. A bigger zone suggests a more effective antimicrobial.

Core practical 9: method in brief

  1. Wash hands and disinfect the bench (e.g. 1% Virkon left for 10 minutes). Close windows and doors.
  2. Grind each plant material (e.g. garlic, mint) with a pestle and mortar in the same volume of ethanol (to dissolve the active substances). Leave, then filter or decant the extract.
  3. Soak sterile paper discs (all the same size) in each extract for the same time; let the ethanol evaporate on a sterile surface.
  4. Mark the base of an agar plate already seeded with a lawn of bacteria (e.g. Bacillus subtilis, Micrococcus luteus) into quarters.
  5. Lift the lid only slightly and use sterile forceps (flamed and cooled) to place one disc per quarter. Include a control disc soaked in ethanol only (evaporated).
  6. Tape the lid at two points (not all the way round), invert the plate and incubate at 25 °C for 24–48 hours.
  7. Measure the diameter of each clear zone across two directions at right angles, take the mean, and calculate the area. Repeat with more plates and find mean values.

Crush a known mass of plant material (for example garlic, mint or ginger) with ethanol using a pestle and mortar, then filter. Each extract should be made the same way.

Many antimicrobial substances in plants dissolve better in ethanol than in water.

Using aseptic technique, spread a known volume of a bacterial culture evenly over an agar plate to make a lawn.

Soak sterile paper discs of the same size in each extract, and one disc in ethanol only as a control. Let the discs dry, then place them on the agar with sterile forceps, well spaced apart.

The ethanol must evaporate, otherwise it would kill bacteria itself. The control disc shows whether any killing is due to the solvent rather than the plant.

Tape the lid on with two short strips (not all the way round), label the base, and incubate upside down at 25 °C for 24 to 48 hours.

Partial taping stops the lid coming off but lets oxygen in, which discourages anaerobic bacteria, many of which are pathogens. Upside down stops condensation dripping onto the agar.

Measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc, across two diameters at right angles, and calculate the area of the zone with πr². A larger clear zone means a more effective antimicrobial at that concentration.

Aseptic technique protects you from the bacteria and protects the culture from contamination: disinfect the bench, flame the inoculating loop until red hot and let it cool, flame the neck of culture bottles, lift the lid of a plate only a little, work close to a lit Bunsen burner, and sterilise all used cultures and equipment in an autoclave (about 121 °C for 15 minutes) before disposal.

Variables and controls

  • Independent: plant extract (or its concentration). Dependent: diameter (or area) of the clear zone.
  • Control: same bacterium and volume of culture, same agar depth, same disc size, same volume and concentration of extract, same soaking time, same incubation temperature and time.
  • The ethanol-only disc shows the solvent and the disc themselves don't stop growth. An antibiotic disc can be added as a positive control for comparison.

Worked example: area of a clear zone

Zone diameters measured across two directions: 18 mm and 20 mm. Mean = 19 mm.

r = 19 ÷ 2 = 9.5 mm.

Area = πr² = π × 9.5² = 284 mm² (3 s.f.).

If the question asks for the area of clear agar only, subtract the disc: a 6 mm disc has area π × 3² = 28.3 mm², leaving 283.5 − 28.3 = 255 mm².

Worked example: standard deviation of zone diameters

Three plates give zone diameters of 7, 9 and 11 mm. Mean x̄ = 27 ÷ 3 = 9 mm.

(x − x̄): −2, 0, +2. Squared: 4, 0, 4. Σ(x − x̄)² = 8.

s = √(8 ÷ (3 − 1)) = √4 = 2.0 mm. Remember the n − 1 and the square root.

Limitations

  • Different compounds have different-sized molecules, so they diffuse through agar at different rates: a small zone doesn't always mean a weak antimicrobial.
  • The concentration of active substance in each extract is unknown and may differ.
  • Zones may be irregular (measure two diameters, or use area); discs placed too close overlap.
  • An agar plate is not skin or a gut: an extract that works here may be broken down in the body or be toxic.
  • The method appears in question stems: agar plate spread with bacteria, discs soaked in extract, incubated at 25 °C for 24 h, zones measured.
  • Planning answers credit: sterile equipment / aseptic technique; a range of concentrations; discs soaked in extract; a bacterial lawn; incubation (at least 24 hours); the dependent variable as "the diameter of the clear zone" (not just "the clear zone").

Often asked as: aseptic technique itself was not asked in WBI12 papers from 2019 to 2026, but zone-of-inhibition data from this practical appear often (see 4.11).

  • "Aseptic technique means wearing gloves." It means preventing contamination of the culture and of people.
  • Sealing the lid all the way round (encourages anaerobes), or incubating at 37 °C.
  • Forgetting the solvent-only control disc.

Spec4.13How drug testing developed: Withering to modern trials

Know how drug testing changed from historical methods to today's protocols, including William Withering's digitalis soup, double-blind trials, placebos and the three phases of clinical trials.

William Withering and digitalis

  • In the 1770s–80s, Withering, an English doctor, heard of a traditional herbal remedy (a "soup" of many plant ingredients) that helped patients with dropsy (swelling caused by fluid build-up, often from heart failure).
  • He worked out that foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) was the active ingredient.
  • Over about ten years he gave foxglove preparations (e.g. dried, powdered leaves) to more than 150 patients, changing the dose and recording the results. Too little did nothing; too much was toxic (sickness, slowed or irregular heartbeat); the right dose strengthened the heartbeat and removed the fluid.
  • Today the drug from foxglove is digoxin, still used for some heart conditions.

What was missing from Withering's method

  • No pre-clinical tests (cells, tissues, animals) before treating people.
  • Tested straight on ill patients, not healthy volunteers first.
  • No control group or placebo; not randomised or blind.
  • The amount of active substance in each leaf varied, so doses were not standardised.
  • Small numbers, no statistics, no independent checking, no formal consent.

Modern drug testing

  1. Pre-clinical testing: computer modelling, then tests on human cells and tissues (in vitro), then on animals, to see whether the drug works, how toxic it is, and how it is absorbed, broken down and excreted.
  2. Clinical trials in three phases (table below).
  3. Data are reviewed independently between phases. If the results are good, the drug is licensed, and its use is still monitored afterwards.
PhaseWhoHow manyWhat it tests
1healthy volunteerssmall group (tens)safety, side effects, how the body handles the drug; a range of doses starting low
2patients with the diseasea few hundred (about 100–500)whether it works; the best dose; more on side effects; often against a placebo
3patients with the diseasethousands (about 1000–3000)whether it is more effective than the existing treatment (or a placebo); how common side effects are; usually randomised, double-blind

Pre-clinical testing: the drug is tested on cells and tissues grown in the laboratory, then on animals such as rodents.

This checks the drug acts as expected in living tissue, shows how it is absorbed and broken down in a whole organism, and screens out toxic compounds before any human takes them.

Phase 1: a small number of healthy volunteers are given the drug, starting with very low doses.

The aim is safety: side effects, a safe dose range, and how the body absorbs, distributes and excretes the drug.

Phase 2: a few hundred patients who have the disease are treated.

This tests whether the drug actually works against the disease, finds the best dose and records side effects in people who are ill.

Phase 3: a large trial with thousands of patients, usually randomised and double-blind, comparing the new drug with a placebo or with the best existing treatment.

Large numbers allow statistical tests to show whether any improvement is significant, and reveal rarer side effects.

If all goes well the results are reviewed by a regulatory body, which may license the drug. Its safety continues to be monitored after it is in use.

Placebos and blinding

  • A placebo looks identical to the drug (same colour, shape, taste) but has no active ingredient. It is given to the control group so that any improvement caused by the drug can be separated from the placebo effect (patients improving because they believe they are being treated).
  • When an effective treatment already exists, the control group gets the existing drug instead, because withholding treatment would be unethical (e.g. for burns or cancer).
  • Single-blind: only the patients don't know which treatment they get.
  • Double-blind: neither the patients nor the doctors/scientists know who gets the drug and who gets the placebo (or existing drug) until the end. This removes bias from both sides: doctors can't treat or assess patients differently, and patients' expectations can't affect the results.
  • Patients are randomly assigned to groups, and groups are matched for age, sex, health and severity of disease.

Answer in context. If the question names a disease or a drug (plague, burns, headaches), use it in every phase: "Phase 2: a few hundred patients with burns…". Generic trial essays are capped at Level 1–2.

  • Phases: pre-clinical on {human cells / animals}; Phase 1 on healthy volunteers (side effects, safety); Phase 2 on a small group (100–500) of patients (dose, and whether it works); Phase 3 on a large group (1000–3000) of patients, compared with a placebo or the current drug; double-blind; a statistical test; review between phases.
  • Phase roles: "to test for side effects", "to find the {safe / minimum / effective} dose", "to see if it is more effective than {existing treatments / a placebo}". "Check for safety" or "see if it works" on their own are ignored. Side effects are not credited for cell or animal tests.
  • Double-blind: "neither the {patient} nor the {doctor / scientist} knows whether they are receiving the {drug / placebo / existing treatment}".
  • Finding a dose: "give a range of doses / increase the dose", "the lowest effective dose / highest dose without serious side effects".
  • Withering: the active ingredient was digitalis (digoxin accepted). Modern trials know the active ingredient and its dose, test first on healthy volunteers, start with the lowest dose, and use three phases.
  • For a burns treatment, a placebo group is ignored: compare with the current hospital treatment.

Often asked as: describe a full trial for a named drug (6-mark); roles of phases 1 and 2; compare phases 2 and 3; explain double-blind; compare Withering's method with modern testing; evaluate trial data (sample size, missing data, no information on age or sex, subjective measures such as pain).

  • Describing the three phases when the question asks how a double-blind trial works, or describing a single-blind trial (WBI12 Oct 2020, Oct 2021 reports).
  • Wrong group sizes, or putting patients in Phase 1.
  • Writing "safe" or "patients" unqualified; calling an existing treatment a "placebo".
  • Saying Withering tested garlic or another plant. He used foxglove.
  • Patient numbers per phase (about 100–500 in Phase 2; 1000–3000 in Phase 3) are credited (WBI12 Jun 2019).
  • In a trial, the current drug is the control in a later stage, not the placebo (WBI12 Oct 2023).
  • A plague-treatment 6-mark reached Level 3 only with the disease context, three phases, double-blind, a placebo and either a significance test or an in-vitro test on the bacterium (WBI12 Jun 2019).
  • Spotting missing data (no results for one drug at some doses) as an evaluation point (WBI12 Jan 2023).

Quick checks

Which structure links the cytoplasm of two neighbouring plant cells?

Plasmodesmata are channels filled with cytoplasm passing through both cell walls. A pit is only a thin area of wall that water can cross, and the middle lamella is the calcium pectate layer that glues the walls together, so neither connects the cytoplasm.

Why can cellulose form microfibrils but starch cannot?

Alternate β-glucose units are inverted, giving straight chains whose –OH groups form hydrogen bonds with neighbouring chains. Cellulose has only 1,4 bonds, and it is starch, not cellulose, that is made of α-glucose and coils into a helix.

Which feature is shared by xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres but not by phloem sieve tubes?

Both xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres have thick lignified walls and are dead. Phloem walls are cellulose only. Transporting water is a job for xylem alone, so that option is the commonest trap.

A plant shows yellowing between the veins of its older leaves. Which ion is most likely to be lacking?

Magnesium is part of chlorophyll, and a shortage causes yellowing between the veins of older leaves first. Calcium shortage shows at the growing tips and young leaves. Nitrate shortage causes general yellowing of older leaves with stunted growth rather than yellowing confined to the areas between the veins.

A fibre with a cross-sectional area of 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² breaks under a force of 8.0 N. What is its tensile strength?

Tensile strength = force ÷ area = 8.0 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ = 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa (400 MPa). Multiplying instead of dividing gives 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ Pa, and slipping a power of ten gives 4.0 × 10⁷ Pa.

Why is a phase 3 drug trial carried out double-blind?

If either the patients or the doctors know who has the real drug, their expectations can affect what is reported and recorded. Blinding does not prevent side effects, and phase 3 trials still need thousands of patients.

In an antimicrobial investigation, why is one disc soaked in ethanol only?

The ethanol-only disc is the control. If it also produced a clear zone, the solvent had not evaporated and the results could not be credited to the plant substances.
