Mammalian transport

Mammals are large and very active, so diffusion alone cannot supply their cells. This page explains why a heart and double circulation are needed, how arteries, veins and capillaries are built, how the heart works through the cardiac cycle, and how haemoglobin carries oxygen and carbon dioxide.

Spec1.6Why many animals need a heart and circulation

Understand why many animals have a heart and circulation: mass transport overcomes the limitations of diffusion in meeting an organism's needs.

The problem: diffusion is only fast over short distances

All cells need a steady supply of O₂ and glucose, and must get rid of CO₂ and urea. A small organism can rely on diffusion. A large one can't, for two reasons:

  1. Distance: many cell layers mean long diffusion distances, and the time diffusion takes rises steeply with distance.
  2. Surface area : volume ratio falls as size increases, so the surface is too small to supply the whole volume. Large, active animals also have a high metabolic rate (high demand).

Worked example: SA : V

A 1 cm cube: SA = 6 cm², V = 1 cm³ → 6 : 1.

A 3 cm cube: SA = 6 × 9 = 54 cm², V = 27 cm³ → 2 : 1.

Tripling the length cut the SA:V ratio to a third, so there is far less surface per unit of volume to supply.

Other shapes: cylinder (a vessel, a worm) V = πr²l, curved SA = 2πrl; sphere V = ⁴⁄₃πr³, SA = 4πr².

Use the radius (half the diameter): using the diameter is the commonest error. Give the ratio as x : 1.

The solution: mass transport

  • Mass transport (mass flow) is the bulk movement of a fluid in one direction, driven by a pressure difference made by a pump (the heart), inside vessels.
  • It carries substances quickly between exchange surfaces (lungs, gut) and every tissue, so the final step into or out of each cell is only a short diffusion distance.
  • It also maintains concentration gradients at exchange surfaces and in tissues, by constantly bringing new blood and carrying away what has diffused in.

Single vs double circulation

Advantages of a double circulation: low pressure to the lungs (doesn't damage the delicate capillaries; slower flow allows time for gas exchange) and high pressure to the body (fast delivery for an active, endothermic animal). The septum keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.

Other animals' circulations. Fish: pressure falls after the gill capillaries, so flow to the body is slow; that is enough because fish aren't endothermic and have a lower metabolic rate. Amphibians (WBI11 June 2025): a three-chambered heart (two atria, one ventricle, no septum), so some mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. That is enough for a small animal with a low O₂ demand, which also exchanges gases through its moist skin. As in mammals, oxygenated blood from the lungs returns to the left atrium. Insects (WBI11 Oct 2021): an open circulation, with blood in body cavities rather than vessels; O₂ reaches cells through tracheoles, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood don't need to be kept apart.

  • Mass transport: "all the components (of the blood) flow together", "down a pressure gradient", "to overcome the limitations of diffusion".
  • Diffusion is "too slow" over large distances. Quote data if given (e.g. 0.5 ms over 1 µm, but 50 ms over 10 µm).
  • Organisms with no circulation: every cell is "not far from" the environment, so "diffusion can supply" enough; "low metabolic rate / oxygen demand"; large SA:V.
  • A big heart in context (e.g. a giraffe): blood "pumped under high pressure" to reach the head "against gravity".

Often asked as: explain in a named organism (giraffe, butterfly, yak, hydra, frog), define mass transport, calculate SA:V.

Name both reasons: diffusion distance too long and SA:V too small for a high demand. Say what is transported and where, e.g. "O₂ and glucose to respiring cells".

Writing about SA:V when the question is about distance or pressure (e.g. a giraffe's heart). Answer the factor the question points to.

Vague "nutrients and waste": name O₂, glucose and CO₂, and say where the concentration gradient is.

In a comparison, describe both animals, not just the mammal.

Memory aid: Arteries go Away; veins go in.

Why each feature matters

  • Elastic tissue (artery): stretches when the ventricle forces blood in, then recoils in diastole. This smooths out pressure surges and keeps pressure up between beats.
  • Smooth muscle: contracts or relaxes to narrow or widen the lumen, controlling blood flow to different organs.
  • Collagen: strength, so the wall withstands high pressure and doesn't overstretch or burst.
  • Smooth, folded endothelium: reduces friction; folds let the artery stretch.
  • Wide lumen in veins: less resistance at low pressure. Valves stop backflow. Contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins and push blood towards the heart.
  • Capillary wall one cell thick → short diffusion distance. Narrow lumen → red cells pass in single file, pressed against the wall (short distance) and moving slowly (more time for exchange). Huge branched network → large surface area, and every cell is close to a capillary. Gaps between cells let plasma out to form tissue fluid and let white cells squeeze through.
  • Elastic fibres "stretch" when pressure is high, "widening the lumen", then "elastic recoil" "maintains the high blood pressure" and smooths the flow. In "why doesn't it burst?" questions, recoil is ignored: use stretch + "collagen for (tensile) strength".
  • Endothelium: "smooth / flattened", "to reduce friction". Smooth muscle: contracts to control flow (vasoconstriction).
  • Capillaries: one cell thick → short diffusion distance for gases; pores let plasma and white cells out; narrow, so red cells are close to the wall.
  • Veins: valves "prevent backflow" because pressure is low.
  • Artery definition: "carries blood away from the heart". Not "carries oxygenated blood" (the pulmonary artery doesn't).
  • Drawing an artery TS: lumen + three layers, labelled tunica intima (endothelium), tunica media (smooth muscle + elastic fibres), tunica externa (collagen). Descriptions instead of names are fine. Label lines must touch the layer, and a wrong label cancels a right one.
  • Velocity graphs: flow "decreases through the arterioles", is "low in the capillaries" (huge total cross-sectional area), "increases in the venules". Explain velocity, not pressure.

Often asked as: draw and label a TS of an artery or vein, explain a named feature, read velocity or pressure graphs, calculate a lumen area or mean arterial pressure.

"Arteries have thick walls to pump blood." Arteries don't pump; the heart does. The wall withstands high pressure and the elastic tissue recoils to maintain it. Don't swap the verbs: muscle contracts, elastic tissue stretches and recoils. Capillary walls are made of cells; they don't have "cell walls".

Writing "walls" when you mean "elastic fibres", or mixing up elastic fibres and smooth muscle.

Vein valves drawn the wrong way round: they open in the direction of flow, towards the heart.

"Glucose diffuses out through the pores": examiners only credit diffusion across the wall for gases. Plasma (carrying glucose) is forced out through the gaps.

Forgetting to multiply by the number of vessels in a surface-area calculation.

Tissue fluid: at the arterial end of a capillary, hydrostatic pressure is higher than the opposing pull of the blood's water potential, so plasma (minus large proteins and cells) is forced out. At the venous end, hydrostatic pressure has fallen, so water moves back in by osmosis (plasma proteins keep the blood's water potential low). Excess fluid drains into lymph vessels. High blood pressure → more tissue fluid forms (oedema).

Mean arterial pressure (formula given): MAP = diastolic + ⅓(systolic − diastolic). For 120/80 mmHg: 80 + 40 ÷ 3 = 93 mmHg (WBI11 Jun 2025).

Lumen area: lumen diameter = outer diameter − 2 × wall thickness; halve it for the radius, then πr². Convert units first (e.g. mm → µm) (WBI11 Jun 2025).

At the arterial end, hydrostatic pressure is greater than the oncotic pressure.

The blood has just been pumped out of the heart, so it is at high pressure.

The net pressure is outwards, so fluid is forced into the tissues.

Along the capillary, hydrostatic pressure falls.

Friction and fluid loss lower the pressure as the blood moves along.

At the venous end, oncotic pressure is greater than hydrostatic pressure, so the net pressure is inwards.

Fluid returns to the capillary, and the excess drains into lymph vessels.

Spec1.8The cardiac cycle and the heart

Know the cardiac cycle (atrial systole, ventricular systole and cardiac diastole) and relate the structure and operation of the mammalian heart, including its major blood vessels, to its function. (Myogenic stimulation isn't needed at AS.)

Heart structure

Diagrams show the heart as if it's facing you, so its right side is on the left of the page.

  • Right side: deoxygenated blood in from the body (vena cava) → out to the lungs (pulmonary artery).
  • Left side: oxygenated blood in from the lungs (pulmonary vein) → out to the body (aorta).
  • Atrioventricular (AV) valves between atria and ventricles: tricuspid on the right, bicuspid (mitral) on the left. Tendinous cords stop them turning inside out.
  • Semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
  • Left ventricle wall thicker than the right: it generates a higher pressure to pump blood all round the body. The right only pumps to the nearby lungs, and low pressure there protects the lung capillaries.
  • Atria have thin walls: they only push blood a short distance, into the ventricles.
  • Septum: keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate.
  • Coronary arteries: supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.

The three stages (one cycle ≈ 0.8 s at 75 beats per minute)

The pressure rule: contraction → smaller volume → higher pressure. Blood always flows from higher to lower pressure.

  1. Atrial systole: atria contract → atrial pressure rises above ventricular pressure → blood is pushed through the open AV valves into the ventricles. Semilunar valves are closed.
  2. Ventricular systole: ventricles contract (from the apex, at the bottom, upwards) → ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure → AV valves shut (the "lub" sound). When ventricular pressure rises above the pressure in the arteries, the semilunar valves open and blood is ejected into the aorta and pulmonary artery.
  3. Cardiac diastole: atria and ventricles relax → ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure → semilunar valves shut (the "dub" sound). Blood flows from the veins into the atria and, once atrial pressure is above ventricular pressure, the AV valves open and the ventricles fill passively.

Valves open when pressure behind them is higher than pressure in front, and close when that difference reverses. They are moved by pressure; they don't contract.

Atrial systole: the atria contract and push blood through the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles.

Extra filling gives the ventricles a little more blood before they contract.

Ventricular systole begins: pressure in the ventricles rises above the atrial pressure.

The rise in pressure shuts the atrioventricular valves, which stops backflow into the atria.

Pressure rises further, until it exceeds the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery.

The semilunar valves are forced open, so blood leaves the heart.

Cardiac diastole: the ventricles relax, and pressure in them falls below the pressure in the arteries.

The semilunar valves snap shut, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricles.

When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the atrioventricular valves open and the ventricles fill passively from the atria.

The filling is driven by the pressure difference, not by muscle contraction.

Reading the pressure graph

  • Each point where two lines cross is a valve opening or closing.
  • From 2 to 3 blood is being ejected into the aorta.
  • From 1 to 2 and from 3 to 4, all valves are shut and the ventricle volume isn't changing.
  • The small notch in aortic pressure just after 3 is the semilunar valve snapping shut. Aortic pressure stays high in diastole because the elastic wall recoils.
  • The right ventricle's curve has the same shape but a much lower peak pressure.

Worked example: heart rate from a graph

One complete cycle (peak to the same point on the next peak) takes 0.8 s.

Beats per second = 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25; beats per minute = 1.25 × 60 = 75 bpm.

(Shortcut: 60 ÷ cycle time in seconds. Reverse: cycle time = 60 ÷ heart rate.)

Follow the s.f. instruction exactly: to 3 s.f. the answer is 75.0, and "75" loses the mark.

  • Valve states: atrial systole AV open, SL closed; ventricular systole AV closed, SL open; cardiac diastole AV open, SL closed. The AV valves close at the start of ventricular systole.
  • Call it "(cardiac) diastole", not "ventricular diastole" or "diasystole".
  • Coronary artery: brings "oxygenated blood to the heart muscle" "for (aerobic) respiration". It branches off the aorta because that's the nearest vessel carrying oxygenated blood. Glucose / nutrients are ignored.
  • Exercise: "the cardiac cycle is shorter / completed more frequently", "ventricles contract more forcefully". "Heart rate increases" alone is ignored.
  • Right v left ventricle: same timing (both sides "beat simultaneously"); the right peaks lower because it pumps only to the lungs, so the alveoli aren't damaged.
  • Delay between atrial and ventricular contraction: so the "atria can finish emptying" into the ventricles before the AV valves close.
  • Volume graphs: left ventricle volume increases in atrial systole, decreases in ventricular systole, increases in diastole.

Often asked as: valve-state MCQs and cloze passages, label vessels or draw flow arrows, read pressure or volume graphs, calculate heart rate or cycle length.

Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate (e.g. 70 cm³ × 75 min⁻¹ = 5250 cm³ min⁻¹). Formally later content, but Unit 1 data questions use it.

Delay at the AV node: the impulse is held up briefly before it passes down the septum, so the atria empty before the ventricles contract. The spec leaves this out at AS, but it has been rewarded (WBI11 Oct 2022).

Pulmonary embolism: a clot lodged in a pulmonary artery stops blood being oxygenated → breathlessness and a raised heart rate (WBI11 Jan 2025).

Mixing up pressure and volume, or forgetting to compare. Every valve explanation needs a comparison: "pressure in the ventricle is higher than in the atrium, so the AV valve closes."

Swapping the pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein. Thinking the right ventricle contracts after the left: they contract together.

Writing "heart rate increases" when the question asks about events in the cycle. Working out 73 ÷ 60 when you need 60 ÷ 73.

Spec1.9Haemoglobin: carrying O₂ and CO₂

(i) Understand the role of haemoglobin in transporting oxygen and carbon dioxide. (ii) Understand the oxygen dissociation curve, the Bohr effect, and why fetal haemoglobin's higher affinity for oxygen matters.

Haemoglobin and oxygen

  • Haemoglobin (Hb) is a globular protein with four polypeptide chains (2 α, 2 β). Each chain holds a haem group containing Fe²⁺, which binds one O₂ molecule. So one Hb carries 4 O₂ (8 oxygen atoms).
  • Hb + 4O₂ ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin. Loading (association) happens in the lungs; unloading (dissociation) in respiring tissues.
  • Cooperative binding: the first O₂ binds with difficulty. Once it binds, Hb undergoes a conformational change (its tertiary/quaternary structure changes), making it easier for further O₂ molecules to bind. This is what makes the curve S-shaped (sigmoid).

Reading the curve, in both directions

  • Left → right (loading): at low pO₂ the curve is shallow: the first O₂ binds with difficulty. In the steep middle, binding is easy after the shape change, so a small rise in pO₂ gives a big rise in saturation. At high pO₂ (lungs) it levels off: Hb is nearly saturated, with few free sites.
  • Right → left (unloading): in the lungs there's little dissociation. In the steep part, at the pO₂ of respiring tissues, a small drop in pO₂ releases a lot of O₂, exactly where cells need it.
  • Affinity = how readily Hb takes up O₂. High affinity: loads easily, unloads reluctantly. Low affinity: the opposite.

The Bohr effect (shift to the right)

  1. Respiring tissue produces CO₂.
  2. In red blood cells, CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid), catalysed by carbonic anhydrase. H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
  3. The H⁺ ions (lower pH) bind to Hb and change its shape, lowering its affinity for O₂.
  4. So more O₂ is released at the same pO₂: the curve shifts right. Active tissues make the most CO₂, so they get the most O₂.

Carbon dioxide transport

  • About 5% dissolved in the plasma.
  • About 10–20% bound to Hb as carbaminohaemoglobin. The CO₂ joins the amino groups of the polypeptide chains, not the haem.
  • Most (about 70–85%) as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻): formed in red blood cells as above, then diffuse out into the plasma. Hb picks up the H⁺, buffering the blood pH. The reaction happens in red cells rather than plasma because that's where the carbonic anhydrase is.

Carbon dioxide diffuses from the tissue fluid into the red blood cell.

The concentration of carbon dioxide is higher outside the cell, so it moves in down the gradient.

Carbon dioxide combines with water to form carbonic acid, H₂CO₃.

The reaction is catalysed by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase inside the red blood cell, making it very fast.

Carbonic acid dissociates into hydrogen ions and hydrogencarbonate ions.

Hydrogencarbonate ions diffuse out into the plasma, and chloride ions move into the cell to balance the charge.

This exchange is called the chloride shift, and it keeps the electrical charge balanced.

The hydrogen ions bind to haemoglobin, which helps release oxygen (the Bohr effect).

Fetal haemoglobin (shift to the left)

  • Fetal Hb has a higher affinity for O₂ than adult Hb.
  • At the placenta the pO₂ is low. At the same pO₂, the mother's Hb unloads O₂ and the fetal Hb, with its higher affinity, loads it, so oxygen diffuses from mother to fetus.
  • After birth, adult Hb gradually replaces fetal Hb, because the baby now needs Hb that unloads O₂ readily in its own respiring tissues.

For any curve that's shifted left or right, give three steps: (1) at the same pO₂, saturation is higher or lower; (2) so affinity is higher or lower; (3) so O₂ is loaded or unloaded more readily: say where and why that helps.

  • Sigmoid curve: "four subunits"; "binding of the first oxygen molecule is difficult"; then "a conformational change" makes "binding of the other molecules easier" (cooperative binding accepted); the curve levels off as Hb becomes saturated. Plain "change of shape" is ignored.
  • Structure: 4 polypeptide subunits (2α, 2β), four haem groups, each with an "iron ion / Fe²⁺" (not "iron") that binds "an oxygen molecule / O₂" (not "O"). "Stores oxygen" is rejected. Hydrophilic R groups on the outside make Hb soluble in the red cell's cytoplasm.
  • Partial pressure: "the pressure exerted by one gas in a mixture of gases". Not amount or %.
  • Fetal Hb: "higher affinity", so "at the same partial pressure" oxygen "leaves the adult haemoglobin and binds to fetal haemoglobin". Write fetus, not "baby".
  • Left shift: "higher affinity", "higher % saturation at a lower pO₂", enough O₂ for respiration. Right shift: "lower affinity", so O₂ is unloaded more readily to respiring tissues.
  • Altitude: lower pO₂ in the alveoli → smaller gradient → slower rate of diffusion → Hb less saturated. "Less diffusion" is ignored.

Often asked as: explain the sigmoid shape, link Hb's structure to its function, explain a shifted curve in an unfamiliar animal (often a 6-mark), read values off a curve.

"CO₂ binds to the haem and pushes the oxygen off." It's the H⁺ ions (from carbonic acid) binding to Hb that lower its affinity. Say "partial pressure of oxygen", not "amount of oxygen".

Thinking fetal Hb works because fetal blood has a lower pO₂. At the placenta both bloods are at about the same pO₂: the point is fetal Hb's higher affinity.

Writing "iron" for iron ion (Fe²⁺), "speed of dissociation" for affinity, or "moves" for diffuses. Confusing Hb (the protein) with the red blood cell. Quote values read off the curve.

In multi-animal 6-mark questions, comparing traits across the animals scores; describing one species at a time stays at Level 1.

Fetal Hb has γ (gamma) chains in place of the β chains (WBI11 Oct 2024).

Data questions use other animals. Animals living where O₂ is scarce (high altitude, burrows, mud such as lugworms) have curves left of adult human Hb (higher affinity). Small, very active mammals have curves to the right (unload readily). Myoglobin (one chain, an O₂ store in muscle) has a curve far to the left: it releases O₂ only when pO₂ is very low (WBI11 Jun 2024).

Quick checks

Why is the wall of the left ventricle thicker than the wall of the right ventricle?

The left ventricle pumps blood through the high-resistance systemic circuit, so it needs to generate high pressure. The right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs, where lower pressure protects the capillaries. The commonest wrong answer is about volume; both ventricles pump roughly the same volume per beat.

During ventricular systole, which valves are open?

In systole, pressure in the ventricles rises above the pressure in the arteries, which forces the semilunar valves open. The atrioventricular valves are shut to stop backflow into the atria. Students often assume the valves that open during filling are open at the same time as the ones pushing blood out.

In active muscle, the oxygen dissociation curve shifts to the right. What does this mean for oxygen delivery?

A right shift means lower affinity: at any oxygen partial pressure less oxygen is bound, so more is released where it is needed. The commonest error is to read a right shift as higher affinity, which is the opposite of what happens.

What is the main role of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells?

Carbonic anhydrase catalyses the formation of carbonic acid from carbon dioxide and water in the tissues, and the reverse reaction in the lungs. Chloride shift is a passive exchange, not carried out by this enzyme.
