Gene expression and genetics

This page follows a gene from code to consequence: how the genetic code is read, how transcription and translation build a protein, how mutations change it, how alleles are inherited, and how cystic fibrosis and genetic screening put these ideas to use.

Spec2.11The nature of the genetic code

Understand the nature of the genetic code: triplet, non-overlapping and degenerate.

  • Triplet: a sequence of three bases (a codon on mRNA) codes for one amino acid. 4³ = 64 possible triplets, which is plenty for 20 amino acids (a doublet code would give only 4² = 16).
  • Non-overlapping: each base is read once, as part of only one triplet.
  • Degenerate: most amino acids are coded for by more than one triplet (e.g. glycine by GGU, GGC, GGA, GGG). This means some substitution mutations change no amino acid (2.14).
  • Some triplets are start (AUG) or stop (UAA, UAG, UGA) signals.
  • (The code is also universal: the same triplets code for the same amino acids in almost all organisms.)

Non-overlapping vs overlapping

mRNA: A U G G C A U U C

Read non-overlapping: AUG · GCA · UUC → 3 codons → 3 amino acids.

If it overlapped, AUG, UGG, GGC, GCA… would all be read, so one base change would alter several amino acids.

Non-overlapping: "a base in one triplet does not code for any other amino acid" · Degenerate: "most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon" (accept: only Met and Trp have one codon) · "a sequence of 12 bases codes for 4 amino acids, because each amino acid needs 3 bases" · Universal: "the same triplet codes for the same amino acid in (almost) all organisms"

Triplet: "three bases code for one amino acid", with an example from the table (e.g. AAA = Lys). Each feature needs a table example, and don't define a term with the term itself.

Why a triplet: "two bases would only give 16 (4²) combinations, not enough for 20 amino acids" (plus start and stop); three give 64 (4³).

Degenerate also "minimises the effect of mutations".

Stop codons: "no more amino acids can be added". 3 of the 64 codons are stop codons, so 61 ÷ 64 = 95.31% code for amino acids.

Often asked as: describe each feature using a codon table (6-mark); why a triplet and not a doublet; count bases, codons or amino acids; explain why a substitution had no effect.

  • The start codon AUG also codes for methionine, so Met appears inside polypeptides as well as at the start (WBI11 Jun 2024).
  • Reading a codon wheel: start at the centre with the first base and work outwards (WBI11 Oct 2025).

Check whether a table is written as mRNA codons or DNA triplets. A DNA template triplet CAG gives the mRNA codon GUC. Always convert to mRNA first before using a codon table.

Swapping degenerate (several codons for one amino acid) and non-overlapping (each base read once). It's the code that is degenerate, not DNA or the bases.

Spec2.12What a gene is

Know that a gene is a sequence of bases on a DNA molecule that codes for a sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain.

  • A gene is a sequence of bases on DNA that codes for the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Its position on a chromosome is its locus. One DNA molecule (chromosome) carries many genes.
  • The chain of cause and effect: base sequence → amino acid sequence (primary structure) → 3-D shape → function of the protein.

Counting

A polypeptide of 150 amino acids needs at least 150 × 3 = 450 bases in the coding part of the gene (plus 3 for the stop codon).

Gene: "a sequence of (DNA) bases that codes for a (poly)peptide" (accept "primary structure").

Allele: "a version / form of a gene". Gene v allele questions need named examples from the context, or they score 1.

mRNA "carries a copy of the gene to the ribosomes". "mRNA copies DNA" or "a copy of DNA" is too vague.

Mixing up gene and allele. The gene is the section of DNA for one polypeptide (e.g. the CFTR gene); alleles are its versions (e.g. the normal and the faulty CFTR allele).

Spec2.13Protein synthesis: transcription and translation

(i) Understand transcription and translation, including RNA polymerase, mRNA, tRNA, ribosomes, and start and stop codons. (ii) Understand the roles of the DNA template (antisense) strand, codons on mRNA and anticodons on tRNA.

Transcription (in the nucleus)

  1. The DNA double helix unwinds and hydrogen bonds break in the region of the gene, separating the strands.
  2. Only one strand, the template (antisense) strand, is used.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides pair with the exposed DNA bases by complementary base pairing (A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C).
  4. RNA polymerase joins the RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds, forming mRNA.
  5. mRNA detaches; the DNA re-forms its helix. mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.

The mRNA has the same sequence as the coding (sense) strand, except U replaces T.

Translation (on ribosomes in the cytoplasm)

  1. The mRNA attaches to a ribosome.
  2. tRNA molecules each carry a specific amino acid and have an anticodon (three bases).
  3. The tRNA whose anticodon is complementary to the start codon (AUG) binds first.
  4. The next tRNA binds to the next codon. The ribosome holds two tRNAs at a time.
  5. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids (condensation), using ATP.
  6. The ribosome moves along one codon; the first tRNA leaves (to collect another amino acid).
  7. This repeats until a stop codon, which has no tRNA; the polypeptide is released.

Worked conversion (a very common question)

The coding (sense) strand would read ATG CCA TGA, the same as mRNA with T for U.

Shortcut: the anticodon is the template triplet with U in place of T (TAC → UAC).

Exact names: "transcription", "translation", "RNA polymerase" (not DNA polymerase, helicase or just "polymerase"). Hedging between two names scores 0.

mRNA: "carries a copy of the gene out of the nucleus to the ribosomes"; its codons set the order of the amino acids.

tRNA: "carries a specific amino acid"; anticodon "complementary to the codon"; "holds the amino acid in place until the peptide bond forms".

Ribosome: site of translation; "holds (two) tRNAs on the mRNA" while the peptide bond forms.

Start codon: "starts translation" and codes for methionine. Stop codons have no tRNA.

Effect of a mutation: "the change is copied into the mRNA" → a different codon → "a tRNA carrying a different amino acid binds" → it's joined into the polypeptide by a peptide bond.

Often asked as: DNA → mRNA → anticodon → amino acids from a table; roles of mRNA, tRNA, RNA polymerase or ribosomes; compare start and stop codons.

  • RNA polymerase binds to the promoter (a base sequence just before the gene), separates the strands and forms phosphodiester bonds (WBI11 Jun 2024).
  • Start v stop codons: 1 start codon (AUG, methionine) and 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA). No tRNA has an anticodon for a stop codon, so the polypeptide is released (WBI11 Jun 2024).
  • mRNA medicines: lipid nanoparticles protect the mRNA from hydrolytic enzymes and fuse with the cell surface membrane; the mRNA is then translated (WBI11 Oct 2019).
  • RNA interference and antisense drugs stop the mRNA being translated; transcription carries on (WBI11 Jan 2025).
  • Codominance at the molecular level: both alleles are transcribed and translated, so both proteins are made (WBI11 Jan 2023).

Mixing up DNA polymerase (replication) with RNA polymerase (transcription). Writing T in mRNA or tRNA (it's U). The anticodon is on tRNA, the codon on mRNA.

Saying peptide bonds form in transcription: they form in translation.

Thinking stop codons have a tRNA. Giving the mRNA sequence when the question asks for the amino acids.

Naming an enzyme or molecule when the question asks for its role.

Spec2.14Mutations

(i) Understand how errors in DNA replication cause mutations (substitution, insertion, deletion). (ii) Know that some mutations cause cancer or genetic disorders, but many have no observable effect.

A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA, usually from an error during replication.

TypeWhat happensEffect
Substitutionone base replaced by anotherchanges one codon only: may give the same amino acid (degenerate code: silent), one different amino acid, or a stop codon (shortened protein)
Insertionan extra base addedframeshift: every codon after it is read differently → many amino acids change; often a stop codon appears
Deletiona base lostframeshift, as for insertion

Worked example

Original mRNA: AUG GCA UUC → Met – Ala – Phe

Substitution (G→C): AUG CCA UUC → Met – Pro – Phe (one amino acid changes)

Deletion of that G: AUG CAU UC… → Met – His – … (everything after the mutation changes)

Why a mutation may or may not matter

  • No effect: the new codon codes for the same amino acid; or the change is in non-coding DNA; or the new amino acid has similar properties and doesn't change the shape.
  • Harmful: a different amino acid changes the primary structure → different bonds between R groups → different tertiary structure → the protein doesn't function (e.g. an enzyme's active site no longer complementary). Examples: cystic fibrosis (2.16), sickle cell anaemia.
  • Cancer: mutations in genes that control cell division can lead to uncontrolled division and a tumour.
  • Mutations in gametes can be inherited; mutations in body cells are not.

Explain the chain fully: base sequence → codon/triplet → amino acid sequence (primary structure) → bonds / 3-D (tertiary) shape → function.

Mutation: "a change in the base sequence of DNA".

Use the spec's names: substitution, insertion, deletion. "Point", "frameshift", "addition", "nonsense" and "duplication" are ignored as type names.

Substitution: affects one triplet; may be silent (degenerate code, or a non-coding region); may change one amino acid → different bonds between R groups → different structure and activity; may make a stop codon → shorter polypeptide, or none.

Insertion / deletion: shifts the reading frame, so every later amino acid may change; the earlier in the gene, the bigger the effect.

Cancer: "cells dividing out of control" as a result of mutation; they may spread.

Often asked as: name the mutation type from two sequences; use a codon table to explain the effect on the protein (6-mark); explain why a mutation had no effect.

  • Chromosome mutations appear in school assessments and some data questions: whole chromosomes gained or lost through non-disjunction in meiosis (e.g. Down's syndrome, trisomy 21: three copies of chromosome 21), and changes to chromosome structure (deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation of a section). These change many genes at once.
  • Why mutation rates differ (WBI11 Oct 2024): faster cell division (more replication), less DNA repair or proofreading, more exposure to mutagens such as UV, more DNA. Mutations also build up with age.
  • Proto-oncogenes: normal genes that control cell division; mutated, they can cause cancer (WBI11 Jan 2025).

"Substitution of bases" just repeats the name: say which base changed and what that does to the codon and the amino acid.

Generic answers: apply the mutation to the table or sequence given and link it to the protein's function.

Always lay out: parental phenotypes → genotypes → gametes → Punnett square → offspring genotypes → phenotypes and ratio / probability. Choose letters whose capitals look different from the lower case (F/f, not S/s or C/c).

Reading pedigree diagrams

  • Squares = male, circles = female; shaded = affected; horizontal line = partners; vertical line down to children; generations numbered I, II, III.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the condition is recessive, and both parents are carriers (heterozygous).
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → dominant.
  • Start with the affected (homozygous recessive) people, whose genotypes you know for certain, and work outwards.
  • An unaffected child of two carriers has a 2/3 chance of being a carrier (FF : Ff = 1 : 2 among the unaffected).

Sex linkage

  • Sex is determined by the sex chromosomes: female XX, male XY. The Y is much smaller and lacks many genes found on the X.
  • A sex-linked gene is carried on the X chromosome. Males have only one X, so a single recessive allele is expressed in a male. Males can't be carriers.
  • Fathers pass their X only to daughters, so a son can't inherit an X-linked condition from his father.
  • Red-green colour blindness: recessive allele Xb; normal XB.

Probability of a colour-blind child = ¼. Probability a son is colour-blind = ½. No daughters are affected (but half are carriers).

Genotype: "the combination of alleles" (not "genes"). Phenotype: "observable characteristic".

Codominance: "both alleles are expressed" in the heterozygote, whose phenotype differs from both homozygotes. Not "both dominant".

Genetic diagram marks: parental genotypes → gametes → offspring genotypes → phenotypes linked to genotypes → ratio or number. The phenotype mark is the one most often lost. Label ratios ("3 orange : 1 white"), and give numbers if numbers are asked for: 284 offspring in a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio → 71 : 142 : 71.

Sex linkage: allele "on the X chromosome", recessive; "males need only one copy, females need two"; "an affected father can't pass it to his sons, because they get his Y".

Pedigrees: one reasoned sentence per individual, e.g. "dominant because 1 and 2 both have the trait but have a child without it"; "not X-linked because female 6 shows the recessive trait but her father doesn't".

Probability of an affected child of a given sex = probability affected × ½, e.g. Ff × Ff → affected boy = ¼ × ½ = ⅛.

Often asked as: genetic diagrams; probability MCQs; prove from a pedigree that a condition is dominant / recessive, or not X-linked; sex-linked crosses in new contexts (haemophilia, fruit flies).

  • ABO blood groups: three alleles (IA, IB, IO). IA and IB are codominant; IO is recessive. IAIB = group AB; IOIO = group O. Used in "which baby belongs to which parents" questions.
  • Test cross: cross an individual with the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive. All dominant offspring → it was homozygous; about half recessive offspring → it was heterozygous.
  • Haemophilia is another X-linked recessive condition (a missing clotting factor, so blood doesn't clot properly: link to 1.11). It is set up and solved exactly like colour blindness, and explains why far more males are affected.
  • Recessive alleles often are expressed (they make a non-functional protein), but one dominant allele makes enough working protein, so the phenotype is normal.
  • ZW sex determination (birds, Komodo dragons): the female is ZW (heterogametic) and the male ZZ (homogametic), the reverse of humans (WBI11 Jan 2026).
  • Chi-squared test to see if offspring numbers fit an expected ratio: work out (O − E)²/E for each class, add them up, and compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 (WBI11 Oct 2021).

Writing sex-linked genotypes without the X and Y (always write XB, Xb, Y), or giving the Y an allele. Also read the question carefully: "probability that a child is affected" differs from "probability that a boy is affected".

"Recessive gene" or "recessive disease": it's a recessive allele.

Using X and Y symbols for an autosomal cross: it scores 0 or is capped.

Giving the genotype ratio when the phenotype ratio was asked for, or an unlabelled "1 : 3". Forgetting that females can be affected by X-linked conditions.

Spec2.16Cystic fibrosis

Understand how a gene mutation in cystic fibrosis affects the gas exchange, digestive and reproductive systems.

The cause

  • CF is caused by a recessive allele of the CFTR gene. CFTR is a chloride ion channel protein in the membranes of epithelial cells.
  • The commonest mutation, ΔF508, is a deletion of three bases (one codon), so one amino acid (phenylalanine) is missing. The protein folds wrongly and doesn't work, or isn't inserted in the membrane.

From faulty protein to sticky mucus (learn this chain)

  1. Normally, CFTR lets Cl⁻ ions move out of epithelial cells into the mucus; Na⁺ follows; this lowers the water potential of the mucus, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis, keeping the mucus runny.
  2. In CF, Cl⁻ isn't moved out of the cells, and Na⁺ ions are absorbed into the cells from the mucus. The water potential inside the cells falls below that of the mucus, so water moves out of the mucus into the cells by osmosis → the mucus is dehydrated: thick and sticky.

Inheritance: two carrier parents (Ff × Ff) → 1 in 4 chance each child has CF.

Examiners reward the full chain: Cl⁻ not transported → water potential → osmosis → thick mucus → then the effect on the specific system. Link gas exchange answers back to Fick's law (2.1).

The chain: "mutation in the CFTR gene" → "CFTR (channel) protein not functioning" → "chloride ions do not move out of the cells" → Na⁺ moves in → water potential inside the cells falls → "water leaves the mucus" by osmosis → "mucus is dehydrated", thick and sticky. "Water concentration" is ignored.

Lungs: "cilia cannot move the mucus away"; mucus "traps bacteria" → infections, coughing, tissue damage; blocked airways → lower concentration gradient, smaller surface area, longer distance → slower diffusion.

Digestion: mucus blocks the pancreatic duct → enzymes don't reach the small intestine → food not fully digested or absorbed.

Reproduction: "mucus prevents sperm passing through the cervix"; mucus may block the oviduct.

ΔF508: "deletion removing the three bases / codon that code for phenylalanine". Not accepted: insertion, or a stop codon.

Often asked as: explain why the mucus is sticky; explain the effect on one system; interpret drug-trial data (FEV1); CF probabilities from carrier parents.

  • Sweat test: CFTR also moves Cl⁻ in sweat glands, so people with CF have salty sweat. Different CFTR mutations leave different amounts of working protein, so chloride levels and symptoms vary (WBI11 Jun 2021).
  • Drug trials: lung function is measured as FEV1 (volume breathed out in the first second) with a spirometer; compare the change in mean FEV1 (WBI11 Jan 2023).
  • A carrier man's sperm: meiosis shares the alleles equally, so 50% of his sperm carry the faulty allele (WBI11 Oct 2025).

"The CFTR protein is mutated." The gene is mutated; the protein it codes for is faulty.

Writing a generic CF essay: use the diagram or data in the question.

Spec2.17Genetic screening

(i) Understand the uses of genetic screening: identifying carriers, pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), and prenatal testing, including amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling. (ii) Understand the implications of prenatal genetic screening.

Uses

  • Carrier screening: tests adults (blood or cheek cells) with a family history of a recessive condition, to see if they carry the allele. Couples can make informed decisions about having children.
  • Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD): during IVF, one or two cells are removed from each early embryo and tested; only embryos without the allele are implanted. Avoids a later termination, but is expensive and unaffected embryos may be discarded.
  • Prenatal testing: tests the fetus during pregnancy.

CVS gives an earlier result, so any decision (e.g. about termination) is made earlier, but carries a slightly higher risk. Results can occasionally be wrong (false positives/negatives). (Non-invasive testing of fetal DNA in the mother's blood avoids miscarriage risk.)

Implications of prenatal screening

  • Positive: parents can prepare (medically, emotionally, financially), choose whether to continue the pregnancy, and the child can be treated early.
  • Negative: risk of miscarriage from the procedure; difficult decisions about termination; false results; anxiety; pressure from others.

Carrier screening: identifies heterozygous couples so they can make an informed decision, but "informed choice" alone isn't enough: give the options: no children or adoption; IVF with PGD so "only healthy embryos are implanted"; prenatal testing, then possibly termination. Result: fewer homozygous births.

Amniocentesis: "amniotic fluid from around the fetus" → fetal cells "analysed for the presence of the faulty allele". CVS: "cells / tissue from the placenta" ("chronic" villus is rejected).

Risks: "increases the risk of miscarriage" (name the procedure); false positives / negatives.

Reasons for a prenatal test: family history, to prepare, to choose an unaffected embryo. Abortion is not accepted as the reason.

Often asked as: explain how carrier screening reduces CF births; describe amniocentesis or CVS; 6-mark questions weighing up the options for a named disorder.

  • Other prenatal tests and timings (WBI11 Jan 2025): NIPT (fetal DNA in the mother's blood, no miscarriage risk) from about 7–10 weeks; CVS; amniocentesis 15–20 weeks; ultrasound; cordocentesis (fetal blood from the umbilical cord) at about 22–24 weeks.
  • PGD can also screen embryos for the wrong number of chromosomes (aneuploidy); it may give false hope and wastes embryos (WBI11 Oct 2021).

Treating carrier screening (of adults) as prenatal screening, or confusing PGD (embryos in IVF) with CVS / amniocentesis (a fetus in the uterus).

Use the right word: embryo for PGD, fetus for CVS and amniocentesis, never "baby".

In 6-mark answers, take each option in turn, use the data given, and tie it to the named disorder. Offering gene therapy is not screening.

Spec2.18Ethical and social issues of genetic screening

Identify and discuss the ethical and social issues of genetic screening from a range of ethical viewpoints, including religious, moral and social ones.

Frameworks to use

  • Rights-based / deontological: some actions are right or wrong in themselves (e.g. "every embryo has a right to life", or "people have a right to know or not to know").
  • Utilitarian: the right choice gives the greatest good for the greatest number, weighing suffering prevented against harms.
  • Autonomy: individuals should make informed decisions about their own bodies and families.

Credited: termination is "taking a human life", which is unethical or against religious / cultural beliefs; the embryo "has a right to life" and "cannot give consent"; "spare embryos from IVF are destroyed"; relatives who share the genes "may be exposed to unwanted facts"; false positives / negatives; miscarriage risk tied to CVS or amniocentesis; the perceived worth of an affected child.

Often asked as: discuss the ethics of a named test; ethical points inside 6-mark screening questions.

One-sided answers: "discuss" means both sides.

Mentioning miscarriage without saying which procedure causes the risk.

Calling an IVF embryo a "fetus" or "baby".

Quick checks

A characteristic shows continuous variation and a bell-shaped distribution. Which explanation is most likely?

Continuous variation with a normal distribution is produced by polygenic inheritance, where several loci each contribute a small effect, and the environment adds further variation. A single gene with no environmental effect gives discontinuous variation instead, which is the most common wrong answer.

Which change would most directly switch a gene off by making its DNA less accessible to transcription?

Methylation of cytosine next to guanine is linked to gene silencing, because the methyl group reduces transcription. Acetylation and demethylation both tend to activate genes. Splicing acts after transcription and does not change DNA accessibility, so it is the commonest wrong answer.

A single gene produces three different proteins in three different cell types. What is the most likely reason?

Alternative splicing joins the exons in different combinations, giving mRNAs of different length and sequence, and therefore different proteins. The DNA is the same in every cell, so mutation is not the explanation. Gene copy number is normally the same in each diploid cell.
